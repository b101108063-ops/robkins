# Chapter 15: Oral Cavities and Gastrointestinal Tract

## Diseases of Teeth and Supporting Structures

### Caries

**Dental caries** results from focal demineralization of tooth structure (enamel and dentin) caused by acids generated during the fermentation of sugars by bacteria.

- Worldwide, **caries** is the main cause of tooth loss before 35 years of age
- The prevalence of caries used to be very high in developed countries with access to processed and refined foods containing large amounts of carbohydrates
- The rate of caries has dropped markedly in countries such as the United States, where:
  - Oral hygiene has improved
  - Fluoridation of the drinking water is widespread
- **Fluoride** is incorporated into the crystalline structure of enamel, forming **fluoroapatite**, which is resistant to degradation by bacterial acids
- With the globalization of the world's economy, processed foods are being increasingly consumed in developing nations; as a result, the rate of caries is increasing in these regions

### Gingivitis

**Gingivitis** is inflammation involving the squamous mucosa (gingiva) and associated soft tissues that surround teeth.

- Poor oral hygiene is the most frequent cause, facilitating buildup of:
  - **Dental plaque** — a sticky biofilm composed of bacteria, salivary proteins, and desquamated epithelial cells
  - **Calculus** (tartar) — formed when plaque becomes mineralized
- In chronic gingivitis:
  - Gingival erythema
  - Edema
  - Bleeding
- Gingivitis may occur at any age but is most prevalent and severe in adolescence (40–60% of individuals), after which incidence tapers off
- Gingivitis can be reversed by regular brushing and flossing, which reduces accumulation of plaque and calculus

### Periodontitis

**Periodontitis** is an inflammatory process that affects the supporting structures of the teeth (periodontal ligaments), alveolar bone, and cementum.

- With progression, periodontitis may result in:
  - Destruction of periodontal ligament
  - Destruction of alveolar bone
  - Eventual tooth loss
- Associated with poor oral hygiene that affects the composition of gingival bacteria:
  - **Facultative Gram-positive organisms** are found at healthy sites
  - **Anaerobic and microaerophilic Gram-negative bacteria** colonize plaque within areas of active periodontitis
- Although about 300 bacterial species reside within the oral cavity, periodontitis is most closely associated with:
  - **Aggregatibacter (Actinobacillus) actinomycetemcomitans**
  - **Porphyromonas gingivalis**
  - **Prevotella intermedia**

#### Summary: Diseases of Teeth and Supporting Structures

- **Caries** is the most common cause of tooth loss in individuals younger than 35 years of age. The primary cause is destruction of tooth structure by acid end products of sugar fermentation by bacteria.
- **Gingivitis** is a common and reversible inflammation of the mucosa surrounding the teeth. It is associated with buildup of dental plaque and calculus.
- **Periodontitis** is a chronic inflammatory condition that can lead to the destruction of the supporting structures of the teeth with eventual loss of dentition. It is associated with poor oral hygiene and altered oral microbiota.

## Oral Inflammatory Lesions

### Aphthous Ulcers (Canker Sores)

- Common superficial mucosal ulcerations affecting up to 40% of the population
- More frequent in the first 2 decades of life, extremely painful, and often recur
- The cause is unknown, but aphthous ulcers tend to be familial and may be associated with:
  - Celiac disease
  - Inflammatory bowel disease
  - Behçet disease
- Ulcers can be solitary or multiple; typically:
  - Shallow
  - Hyperemic base covered by a thin exudate
  - Rimmed by a narrow zone of erythema (Fig. 15.1)
- In most cases they resolve spontaneously in 7 to 10 days but can recur

**Fig. 15.1 Aphthous ulcer.** Single ulceration with an erythematous halo surrounding a yellowish fibrinopurulent membrane.

### Herpes Simplex Virus Infections

**Herpes simplex virus (HSV)** causes a self-limited primary infection that can be reactivated when there is a compromise in host resistance.

- Most orofacial herpetic infections are caused by **herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1)**
- The remainder are caused by **HSV-2** (genital herpes); with changing sexual practices, oral HSV-2 is becoming increasingly common
- **Primary infections** typically occur in children between 2 and 4 years of age and are often asymptomatic
  - In 10% to 20% of cases, the primary infection manifests as **acute herpetic gingivostomatitis**, with abrupt onset of vesicles and ulcerations throughout the oral cavity
- Most adults harbor latent HSV-1, and the virus can be reactivated, resulting in a so-called "cold sore" or **recurrent herpetic stomatitis**
- Factors associated with HSV reactivation:
  - Trauma
  - Allergies
  - Exposure to ultraviolet light
  - Extremes of temperature
  - Upper-respiratory tract infections
  - Pregnancy
  - Menstruation
  - Immunosuppression
- Recurrent lesions:
  - Occur at the site of primary inoculation or in adjacent mucosa innervated by the same ganglion
  - Typically appear as groups of small (1-to 3-mm) vesicles
  - Most common locations: lips (herpes labialis), nasal orifices, buccal mucosa, gingiva, and hard palate
  - Typically resolve within 7 to 10 days
  - Can persist in immunocompromised patients, who may require systemic anti-viral therapy
- Morphologically:
  - Infected cells become ballooned and have large eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions
  - Adjacent cells commonly fuse to form large multinucleated polykaryons

**Fig. 15.2 Fibrous proliferations.** (A) Fibroma. Smooth pink exophytic nodule on the buccal mucosa. (B) Pyogenic granuloma. Erythematous hemorrhagic exophytic mass arising from the gingival mucosa.

### Oral Candidiasis (Thrush)

**Candidiasis** is the most common fungal infection of the oral cavity. **Candida albicans** is a normal component of the oral flora and only produces disease under unusual circumstances.

Predisposing factors:
- Immunosuppression
- The specific strain of C. albicans
- The composition of the oral microbial flora (microbiota)
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics that alter the normal microbiota

Three major clinical forms of oral candidiasis:
1. **Pseudomembranous** (most common; known as **thrush**)
2. **Erythematous**
3. **Hyperplastic**

**Pseudomembranous form (thrush):**
- Characterized by a superficial, curdlike, gray to white inflammatory membrane
- Composed of matted organisms enmeshed in a fibrinosuppurative exudate
- Can be readily scraped off to reveal an underlying erythematous base
- In mildly immunosuppressed or debilitated individuals (e.g., diabetics), the infection usually remains superficial
- May spread to deep sites in association with more severe immunosuppression (organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, patients with neutropenia, chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression, or AIDS)

#### Summary: Oral Inflammatory Lesions

- **Aphthous ulcers** are painful superficial ulcers of unknown etiology that may in some cases be associated with systemic diseases.
- **Herpes simplex virus** causes a self-limited infection that presents with vesicles (cold sores, fever blisters) that rupture and heal, without scarring, and often leave latent virus in nerve ganglia. Reactivation can occur.
- **Oral candidiasis** may occur when the oral microbiota is altered (e.g., after antibiotic use). Invasive disease may occur in immunosuppressed individuals.

## Proliferative and Neoplastic Lesions of the Oral Cavity

### Fibrous Proliferative Lesions

#### Fibromas

- **Fibromas** are submucosal nodular fibrous tissue masses
- Formed when chronic irritation results in reactive connective tissue hyperplasia (Fig. 15.2A)
- Occur most often on the buccal mucosa along the bite line
- Treatment: complete surgical excision and removal of the source of irritation

#### Pyogenic Granuloma

- An inflammatory lesion typically found on the gingiva of children, young adults, and pregnant women (pregnancy tumor)
- **Morphology:**
  - Richly vascular
  - Typically ulcerated
  - Red to purple color
- In some cases, growth can be rapid and raise fear of a malignant neoplasm
- Histologic examination demonstrates a proliferation of immature vessels similar to that seen in granulation tissue
- May:
  - Regress
  - Mature into dense fibrous masses
  - Develop into a peripheral ossifying fibroma
- Treatment: complete surgical excision

### Leukoplakia and Erythroplakia

**Leukoplakia** is defined by the World Health Organization as "a white patch or plaque that cannot be scraped off and cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other disease."

- Approximately 3% of the world's population has leukoplakic lesions
- Of these, 5% to 25% are dysplastic and at risk for progression to **squamous cell carcinoma**
- Until proved otherwise by histologic evaluation, all leukoplakias must be considered precancerous

**Erythroplakia:**
- A red, velvety, sometimes eroded lesion
- Flat or slightly depressed relative to the surrounding mucosa
- Associated with a much greater risk for malignant transformation than leukoplakia
- More than 50% of these cases undergo malignant transformation

**Epidemiology:**
- Both leukoplakia and erythroplakia typically affect individuals between 40 and 70 years of age
- 2:1 male predominance
- Most common risk factor: **tobacco use** (cigarettes, pipes, cigars, and chewing tobacco)

**Histology (Morphology):**
- Leukoplakia and erythroplakia show a spectrum of epithelial changes:
  - Hyperkeratosis overlying a thickened, acanthotic but orderly mucosal epithelium
  - To lesions with markedly dysplastic changes sometimes merging into **carcinoma in situ** (Fig. 15.3B)
- The most severe dysplastic changes are associated with erythroplakia
- With increasing dysplasia and anaplasia, a subjacent inflammatory cell infiltrate of lymphocytes and macrophages is often present

**Fig. 15.3 Leukoplakia.** (A) Gross appearance of leukoplakia is highly variable. In this example, the lesion is smooth with well-demarcated borders and minimal elevation. (B) Histologic appearance of leukoplakia showing dysplasia, characterized by nuclear and cellular pleomorphism and loss of normal maturation.

### Squamous Cell Carcinoma

- Approximately 95% of cancers of the oral cavity are **squamous cell carcinomas**
- The remainder largely consist of adenocarcinomas of salivary glands
- **Squamous cell carcinoma** is an aggressive epithelial malignancy and the sixth most common neoplasm in the world today
- Despite numerous advances in treatment, the overall long-term survival rate has remained less than 50% for the past 50 years
- This dismal outlook is due to several factors, in large part because oral cancer often is diagnosed at an advanced stage

**Multiple primary tumors:**
- May be present at initial diagnosis but more often detected later
- Estimated rate: 3% to 7% per year
- Patients who survive 5 years after diagnosis of the initial tumor have up to a 35% chance of developing at least one new primary tumor within that interval
- This elevated risk has led to the concept of **"field cancerization"** — multiple primary tumors develop independently as a result of years of chronic mucosal exposure to carcinogens such as alcohol or tobacco

**Pathogenesis — Two distinct pathogenic pathways:**

**1. Carcinogen exposure pathway:**
- Chronic alcohol and tobacco use (both smoked and chewed)
- In India and Southeast Asia: chewing of **betel quid** and **paan** (containing araca nut, slaked lime, and tobacco, all wrapped in betel nut leaf)
- Deep sequencing has revealed frequent mutations bearing a molecular signature consistent with exposure to tobacco carcinogens
- Mutations frequently involve **TP53** and genes that regulate cell proliferation, such as **RAS**

**2. HPV-related pathway:**
- Tumors tend to occur in the tonsillar crypts or the base of the tongue
- Harbor oncogenic "high-risk" subtypes, particularly **HPV-16**
- Carry far fewer mutations than tobacco-associated tumors
- Often overexpress **p16**, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor
- The incidence of HPV-associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma is predicted to surpass that of cervical cancer
- Prognosis for patients with HPV-positive tumors is better than for HPV-negative tumors
- The HPV vaccine offers hope to limit the increasing frequency of HPV-associated oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma

**Rising incidence in young individuals:**
- The incidence of oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (particularly in the tongue) has been on the rise in individuals younger than 40 years of age who have no known risk factors
- The pathogenesis in this group (nonsmokers, not infected with HPV) is unknown

**Morphology:**
- Squamous cell carcinoma may arise anywhere in the oral cavity
- Most common locations:
  - Ventral surface of the tongue
  - Floor of the mouth
  - Lower lip
  - Soft palate
  - Gingiva (Fig. 15.4A)
- In early stages: raised, firm, pearly plaques or irregular, roughened, or verrucous mucosal thickenings
- As lesions enlarge: ulcerated and protruding masses with irregular and indurated (rolled) borders
- Histologic patterns range from well-differentiated keratinizing neoplasms (Fig. 15.4B) to anaplastic, sometimes sarcomatoid tumors
- The degree of histologic differentiation does not necessarily correlate with biologic behavior
- Typically, oral squamous cell carcinoma infiltrates locally before it metastasizes
- **Cervical lymph nodes** are the most common sites of regional metastasis
- Distant metastases: mediastinal lymph nodes, lungs, and liver

**Fig. 15.4 Oral squamous cell carcinoma.** (A) Gross appearance demonstrating ulceration and induration of the oral mucosa. (B) Histologic appearance demonstrating numerous nests and islands of malignant keratinocytes invading the underlying connective tissue stroma.

#### Summary: Lesions of the Oral Cavity

- **Fibromas** and **pyogenic granulomas** are common reactive lesions of the oral mucosa.
- **Leukoplakia** and **erythroplakia** are mucosal plaques that may undergo malignant transformation.
- The risk for malignant transformation is greater in erythroplakia (relative to leukoplakia).
- A majority of oral cavity cancers are squamous cell carcinomas.
- Oral squamous cell carcinomas are classically linked to tobacco and alcohol use, but the incidence of HPV-associated lesions is rising.

## Diseases of Salivary Glands

There are three major salivary glands — **parotid**, **submandibular**, and **sublingual** — as well as innumerable minor salivary glands distributed throughout the oral mucosa. Inflammatory or neoplastic disease may develop within any of these.

### Xerostomia

**Xerostomia** is defined as a dry mouth resulting from a decrease in the production of saliva.

- Incidence has been reported in more than 20% of individuals older than 70 years of age
- A major feature of the autoimmune disorder **Sjögren syndrome**, in which it is usually accompanied by dry eyes (Chapter 5)
- A lack of salivary secretions is also a major complication of radiation therapy
- Most frequently observed as a side effect of many common classes of medications:
  - Anti-cholinergic
  - Anti-depressant/anti-psychotic
  - Diuretic
  - Anti-hypertensive
  - Sedative
  - Muscle relaxant
  - Analgesic
  - Anti-histaminic agents
- The oral cavity may reveal:
  - Dry mucosa and/or atrophy of the papillae of the tongue
  - Fissuring and ulcerations
  - In Sjögren syndrome: concomitant inflammatory enlargement of the salivary glands
- Complications:
  - Increased rates of dental caries
  - Increased rates of candidiasis
  - Difficulty in swallowing and speaking

### Sialadenitis

**Sialadenitis** is inflammation of the salivary glands and may be induced by trauma, viral or bacterial infection, or autoimmune disease.

#### Viral Sialadenitis — Mumps

- The most common form of viral sialadenitis
- May produce enlargement of all salivary glands but predominantly involves the parotids
- Produces interstitial inflammation marked by a mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate
- In children: most often a self-limited benign condition
- In adults: can cause pancreatitis or orchitis (sometimes causes sterility)

#### Mucocele

- The most common inflammatory lesion of the salivary glands
- Results from either blockage or rupture of a salivary gland duct, with consequent leakage of saliva into the surrounding connective tissue stroma
- Occurs most often in toddlers, young adults, and older adults
- Typically manifests as a fluctuant swelling of the lower lip that may change in size, particularly in association with meals (Fig. 15.5A)
- Histologic examination demonstrates:
  - A cystlike space lined by granulation tissue or fibrous connective tissue
  - Filled with mucin and inflammatory cells, particularly macrophages (Fig. 15.5B)
- Treatment: complete excision of the cyst and the minor salivary gland

**Fig. 15.5 Mucocele.** (A) Fluctuant fluid-filled lesion on the lower lip subsequent to trauma. (B) Cystlike cavity (right) filled with mucinous material and lined by organizing granulation tissue. The normal gland acini are seen on the left.

#### Bacterial Sialadenitis

- Common infection most often involving the major salivary glands, particularly the submandibular glands
- Most frequent pathogens:
  - **Staphylococcus aureus**
  - **Streptococcus viridans**
- **Sialolithiasis** (duct obstruction by stones) is a common antecedent to infection
- Other antecedents: impacted food debris, edema consequent to injury
- **Dehydration and decreased secretory function** can predispose to bacterial invasion
- Systemic dehydration may predispose to suppurative bacterial parotitis in older adult patients following major thoracic or abdominal surgery
- May be largely interstitial or, when induced by staphylococci or other pyogens, may be associated with suppurative necrosis and abscess formation

### Neoplasms of Salivary Glands

Despite their relatively simple morphology, the salivary glands give rise to at least 30 histologically distinct tumors.

**Epidemiology:**
- Small number of these neoplasms account for more than 90% of tumors
- Salivary gland tumors are relatively uncommon (less than 2% of all human tumors)
- 65%–80% arise within the parotid
- 10% in the submandibular gland
- Remainder in the minor salivary glands (including sublingual glands)

**Malignancy rates by gland:**
- Parotid: 15%–30% are malignant
- Submandibular: approximately 40% are malignant
- Minor salivary glands: approximately 50% are malignant
- Sublingual: 70%–90% are cancerous
- **The likelihood that a salivary gland tumor is malignant is inversely proportional to the size of the gland**

- Usually occur in adults with a slight female predominance
- About 5% occur in children younger than 16 years of age
- Parotid gland neoplasms produce swelling in front of and below the ear
- Benign tumors may be present for months to several years before coming to clinical attention
- Cancers more often come to attention promptly (more rapid growth)
- No reliable criteria to differentiate benign from malignant lesions on clinical grounds; histopathologic evaluation is essential

#### Pleomorphic Adenoma

- **Pleomorphic adenomas** are benign tumors consisting of a mixture of ductal (epithelial) and myoepithelial cells
- Exhibit both epithelial and mesenchymal differentiation
- Epithelial elements are dispersed throughout the matrix, which may contain variable mixtures of:
  - Myxoid
  - Hyaline
  - Chondroid (cartilaginous)
  - Even osseous tissue
- Also known as **mixed tumor** (though this name is less preferred)
- Represent about 60% of tumors in the parotid
- Less common in the submandibular glands
- Relatively rare in the minor salivary glands
- Present as slow-growing, painless, mobile discrete masses
- **Recur if incompletely excised:**
  - Recurrence rates approach 25% after simple enucleation
  - Recurrence rates only 4% after wider resection
  - Recurrence stems from a failure to recognize minute extensions of tumor into surrounding soft tissues

**Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma (malignant mixed tumor):**
- The incidence of malignant transformation increases with time:
  - 2% of tumors present for less than 5 years
  - Almost 10% for tumors present for more than 15 years
- The cancer usually takes the form of an adenocarcinoma or undifferentiated carcinoma
- Among the most aggressive malignant neoplasms of salivary glands
- Mortality rates of 30% to 50% at 5 years

**Morphology:**
- Typically manifest as rounded, well-demarcated masses rarely exceeding 6 cm
- Although they are encapsulated, in some locations (particularly the palate), the capsule is not fully developed and expansile growth produces protrusions into surrounding tissues
- Cut surface: gray-white with myxoid and blue translucent chondroid (cartilage-like) areas
- Striking histologic feature: characteristic heterogeneity
  - Epithelial elements (ductal or myoepithelial cells) arranged in ducts, acini, irregular tubules, strands, or sheets
  - Dispersed within a mesenchyme-like background of loose myxoid tissue containing islands of chondroid and, rarely, foci of bone (Fig. 15.6)
- Sometimes epithelial cells form well-developed ducts lined by cuboidal to columnar cells with an underlying layer of deeply chromatic, small myoepithelial cells
- In other instances there may be strands or sheets of myoepithelial cells
- Islands of well-differentiated squamous epithelium also may be present
- Usually no epithelial dysplasia or mitotic activity

**Fig. 15.6 Pleomorphic adenoma.** (A) Low-power view showing a well-demarcated tumor with adjacent, deeply staining, normal salivary gland parenchyma. (B) High-power view showing epithelial cells as well as myoepithelial cells within chondroid matrix material.

#### Mucoepidermoid Carcinoma

- Composed of variable mixtures of:
  - Squamous cells
  - Mucus-secreting cells
  - Intermediate cells
- Represent about 15% of all salivary gland tumors
- Occur mainly (60%–70%) in the parotids
- Account for a large fraction of salivary gland neoplasms in the other glands (particularly minor salivary glands)
- **The most common form of primary malignant tumor of the salivary glands**

**Morphology:**
- Can grow as large as 8 cm in diameter
- Apparently circumscribed but lack well-defined capsules and often are infiltrative
- Cut surface: pale gray to white with small, mucinous cysts
- Histologic examination:
  - Contain cords, sheets, or cysts lined by squamous, mucous, or intermediate cells
  - **Intermediate cells** have both squamous features and mucus-filled vacuoles (most easily detected with mucin stains)
- Cytologically, tumor cells may be benign-appearing or highly anaplastic and unmistakably malignant

**Grading and prognosis:**
- **Low-grade tumors:**
  - May invade locally and recur in about 15% of cases
  - Metastasize only rarely
  - 5-year survival rate: over 90%
- **High-grade neoplasms** (and to a lesser extent intermediate-grade tumors):
  - Invasive and difficult to excise
  - Recur in 25% to 30% of cases
  - About 30% metastasize to distant sites
  - 5-year survival rate: only 50%

#### Summary: Diseases of Salivary Glands

- **Sialadenitis** (inflammation of the salivary glands) can be caused by trauma, infection (such as mumps), or an autoimmune reaction.
- **Pleomorphic adenoma** is a slow-growing neoplasm composed of a heterogeneous mixture of epithelial and mesenchymal cells. It is typically benign.
- **Mucoepidermoid carcinoma** is a malignant neoplasm of variable biologic aggressiveness that is composed of a mixture of squamous and mucous cells.

## Odontogenic Cysts and Tumors

**Odontogenic cysts** are usually derived from remnants of odontogenic epithelium present within the jaws. In contrast to the rest of the skeleton, epithelium-lined cysts are quite common in the jaws. These cysts are subclassified as either inflammatory or developmental.

### Developmental Cysts

#### Dentigerous Cyst

- Originates around the crown of an unerupted tooth
- Thought to be the result of degeneration of the dental follicle (primordial tissue that makes the enamel surface of teeth)
- Lined by a thin, stratified squamous epithelium
- Typically associated with a dense chronic inflammatory infiltrate in the underlying connective tissue
- Complete removal is curative

#### Odontogenic Keratocyst

- Can occur at any age but most frequent in individuals between 10 and 40 years of age
- Male predominance
- Typically located within the posterior mandible
- **Locally aggressive and has a high recurrence rate**
- Histologic examination:
  - Thin layer of parakeratinized or orthokeratinized stratified squamous epithelium
  - Prominent basal cell layer
  - Corrugated luminal epithelial surface
- Treatment requires aggressive and complete removal
- **Recurrence rates of up to 60% are associated with inadequate resection**

### Inflammatory Cysts

#### Periapical Cyst

- In contrast to developmental cysts, the periapical cyst has an **inflammatory etiology**
- Extremely common lesions
- Occur at the tooth apex as a result of long-standing pulpitis (caused by advanced caries or trauma)
- Necrosis of the pulpal tissue can traverse the length of the root and exit the apex of the tooth into the surrounding alveolar bone, leading to a periapical abscess
- Over time, granulation tissue (with or without an epithelial lining) may develop
- Successful treatment necessitates complete removal of the offending material followed by restoration or extraction of the tooth

### Odontogenic Tumors

A complex group of lesions with diverse histologic appearances and clinical behaviors:
- Some are true neoplasms (benign or malignant)
- Others are thought to be hamartomas
- Derived from **odontogenic epithelium**, **ectomesenchyme**, or both

#### Ameloblastoma

- Arise from odontogenic epithelium
- Typically cystic, slow-growing
- **Locally invasive** but have an indolent course
- Cysts are lined by:
  - Palisading columnar epithelium
  - Overlying loose stroma with stellate cells

#### Odontoma

- The most common type of odontogenic tumor
- Arises from epithelium but shows extensive deposition of enamel and dentin
- **Cured by local excision**

#### Summary: Odontogenic Cysts and Tumors

- The jaws are a common site of epithelium-lined cysts derived from odontogenic remnants.
- The **odontogenic keratocyst** is locally aggressive, with a high recurrence rate.
- The **periapical cyst** is a reactive, inflammatory lesion associated with caries or dental trauma.
- The most common odontogenic tumors are **ameloblastoma** and **odontoma**.

## The Esophagus

The esophagus develops from the cranial portion of the foregut. It is a hollow, highly distensible muscular tube that extends from the epiglottis to the gastroesophageal junction (located just above the diaphragm). Acquired diseases of the esophagus range from lethal cancers to the persistent "heartburn" of gastroesophageal reflux.

## Obstructive and Vascular Diseases of the Esophagus

### Mechanical Obstruction — Atresia, Fistulas, and Duplications

- May occur in any part of the gastrointestinal tract
- When they involve the esophagus, discovered shortly after birth (usually because of regurgitation during feeding)
- **Prompt surgical repair is required**

**Atresia:**
- A thin, noncanalized cord replaces a segment of esophagus
- Occurs most frequently at or near the tracheal bifurcation
- Usually associated with a fistula connecting the upper or lower esophageal pouches to a bronchus or the trachea
- This abnormal connection can result in aspiration, suffocation, pneumonia, or severe fluid and electrolyte imbalances

**Esophageal stenosis:**
- May be congenital or acquired (more common)
- Acquired narrowing generally caused by:
  - Fibrous thickening of the submucosa
  - Atrophy of the muscularis propria
- Stenosis due to inflammation and scarring may be caused by:
  - Chronic gastroesophageal reflux
  - Irradiation
  - Ingestion of caustic agents
  - Other forms of severe injury
- Stenosis-associated dysphagia is usually progressive; difficulty eating solids typically occurs long before problems with liquids

### Functional Obstruction — Esophageal Dysmotility

Efficient delivery of food and fluids to the stomach requires coordinated waves of peristaltic contractions. Esophageal dysmotility interferes with this process and is characterized by discoordinated contraction or spasm of the muscularis.

**Achalasia** is characterized by the triad of:
1. Incomplete lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation
2. Increased LES tone
3. Esophageal aperistalsis

- **Primary achalasia** is caused by failure of distal esophageal inhibitory neurons and is idiopathic
- Degenerative changes in neural innervation may lead to **secondary achalasia**:
  - Intrinsic to the esophagus
  - Within the extraesophageal vagus nerve
  - Within the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus
- **Chagas disease**: *Trypanosoma cruzi* infection causes destruction of the myenteric plexus, failure of LES relaxation, and esophageal dilatation
  - Duodenal, colonic, and ureteric myenteric plexuses also can be affected
- Other causes of achalasia-like disease:
  - Diabetic autonomic neuropathy
  - Infiltrative disorders (malignancy, amyloidosis, sarcoidosis)
  - Lesions of dorsal motor nuclei (polio, surgical ablation)

### Ectopia

**Ectopic tissues** (developmental rests) are common in the gastrointestinal tract.

**Inlet patch:**
- Most frequent site of ectopic gastric mucosa is the upper third of the esophagus
- Usually asymptomatic
- Acid released by gastric mucosa within the esophagus can result in:
  - Dysphagia
  - Esophagitis
  - Barrett esophagus
  - Rarely, adenocarcinoma

**Gastric heterotopia:**
- Small patches of ectopic gastric mucosa in the small bowel (particularly within a **Meckel diverticulum**) or colon
- May present with occult blood loss due to local injury caused by acid secretion

### Esophageal Varices

Venous blood from the gastrointestinal tract is delivered to the liver via the portal vein before reaching the inferior vena cava. This circulatory pattern is responsible for the **first-pass effect**.

**Portal hypertension** induces development of collateral channels that allow portal blood to shunt into the caval system. These collateral veins enlarge the subepithelial and submucosal venous plexi within the distal esophagus — these enlarged vessels are termed **varices**.

- Develop in 50% of cirrhotic patients (most commonly associated with alcoholic liver disease)
- Worldwide, **hepatic schistosomiasis** is the second most common cause of varices

**Morphology:**
- Detected by angiography but most commonly during endoscopy (Fig. 15.7A and B)
- Appear as tortuous dilated veins within the submucosa of the distal esophagus and proximal stomach (see Fig. 15.7C and D)
- The overlying mucosa can be intact or ulcerated and necrotic (particularly if rupture has occurred)

**Clinical Features:**
- Often asymptomatic
- **Rupture can lead to massive hematemesis and death — a medical emergency**
- Despite intervention, as many as half of the patients die from the first bleeding episode (direct consequence of hemorrhage or hepatic coma triggered by the protein load and hypovolemic shock)
- Among survivors, additional episodes of hemorrhage occur in as many as 20% of cases

**Fig. 15.7 Esophageal varices.** (A) Angiogram showing several tortuous esophageal varices. (B) Endoscopy is more commonly used to identify varices. (C) Collapsed varices in a postmortem specimen. The polypoid areas are sites of variceal hemorrhage that were ligated with bands. (D) Dilated varices beneath intact squamous mucosa.

## Esophagitis

### Esophageal Lacerations — Mallory-Weiss Syndrome

The most common esophageal lacerations are **Mallory-Weiss tears**, often induced by severe retching or vomiting.

- Normally, a reflex relaxation of the gastroesophageal musculature precedes the anti-peristaltic contractile wave associated with vomiting
- This relaxation may fail during prolonged vomiting, and refluxing gastric contents cause the esophageal wall to stretch and tear
- Patients usually present with **hematemesis**

**Mallory-Weiss syndrome:**
- Roughly linear lacerations, longitudinally oriented
- Usually cross the gastroesophageal junction (Fig. 15.8A)
- Superficial tears that generally heal quickly without surgical intervention

**Boerhaave syndrome:**
- Severe, transmural esophageal tears
- Result in mediastinitis
- Catastrophic; require prompt surgical intervention

### Chemical and Infectious Esophagitis

The stratified squamous mucosa of the esophagus may be damaged by a variety of irritants:
- Alcohol
- Corrosive acids or alkalis
- Excessively hot fluids
- Heavy smoking
- Medicinal pills (most commonly **doxycycline** and **bisphosphonates**), which may adhere to the esophageal lining and dissolve in the esophagus rather than passing into the stomach, resulting in **pill-induced esophagitis**

**Iatrogenic esophageal injury:**
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy
- Radiation therapy
- Graft-versus-host disease
- Morphologic changes: nonspecific ulceration and acute inflammation
- Irradiation causes blood vessel damage adding an element of ischemic injury

**Infectious esophagitis:**
- May occur in otherwise healthy individuals but most frequent in those who are debilitated or immunosuppressed
- Common pathogens in immunosuppressed patients:
  - **Herpes simplex viruses**
  - **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**
  - Fungal organisms (Candida is most common; also mucormycosis and aspergillosis)
- The esophagus may also be involved in desquamative skin diseases (bullous pemphigoid, epidermolysis bullosa) and, rarely, Crohn disease

**Fungal or bacterial infection:**
- Can be primary or complicate a preexisting ulcer
- Nonpathogenic oral bacteria frequently found in ulcer beds
- Pathogenic organisms (about 10% of infectious esophagitis cases) may invade the lamina propria and cause necrosis of overlying mucosa
- Candidiasis in its most advanced form: adherent, gray-white pseudomembranes composed of densely matted fungal hyphae and inflammatory cells covering the esophageal mucosa

**Endoscopic clues to infectious agent identity:**
- **Herpesviruses** typically cause punched-out ulcers (Fig. 15.8B)
  - Histopathologic analysis demonstrates nuclear viral inclusions within a rim of degenerating epithelial cells at the ulcer edge (Fig. 15.8C)
- **CMV** causes shallower ulcerations
  - Biopsy shows characteristic nuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions within capillary endothelium and stromal cells (Fig. 15.8D)
- Immunohistochemical staining for viral antigens can be a useful ancillary diagnostic tool

**Fig. 15.8 Traumatic and viral esophagitis.** (A) Endoscopic view of a longitudinally-oriented Mallory-Weiss tear. (B) Postmortem specimen with multiple herpetic ulcers in the distal esophagus. (C) Multinucleate squamous cells containing herpesvirus nuclear inclusions. (D) Cytomegalovirus-infected endothelial cells with nuclear and cytoplasmic inclusions.

### Reflux Esophagitis

**Reflux of gastric contents** into the lower esophagus is:
- The most frequent cause of esophagitis
- The most common gastrointestinal ailment with which patients present in the outpatient setting in the United States

Associated clinical condition: **gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)**

The stratified squamous epithelium of the esophagus is:
- Resistant to abrasion from foods
- Sensitive to acid

Protective mechanisms:
- Submucosal glands of the proximal and distal esophagus secrete mucin and bicarbonate
- High lower esophageal sphincter tone protects against reflux of acidic gastric contents

**Pathogenesis:**
- Central factor: reflux of gastric juices
- In severe cases, duodenal bile reflux may exacerbate the damage
- Conditions that decrease lower esophageal sphincter tone or increase abdominal pressure:
  - Alcohol and tobacco use
  - Obesity
  - Central nervous system depressants
  - Pregnancy
  - Hiatal hernia
  - Delayed gastric emptying
  - Increased gastric volume

**Morphology:**
- Simple hyperemia (redness to the endoscopist) may be the only alteration
- In mild GERD: mucosal histology is often unremarkable
- With more significant disease:
  - **Eosinophils** are recruited into the squamous mucosa
  - Followed by **neutrophils** (usually associated with more severe injury) (Fig. 15.9A)
  - **Basal zone hyperplasia** exceeding 20% of the total epithelial thickness
  - **Elongation of lamina propria papillae** extending into the upper third of the epithelium

**Clinical Features:**
- Most common in those over 40 years of age but also occurs in infants and children
- Most frequently reported symptoms:
  - Heartburn
  - Dysphagia
  - Less often, noticeable regurgitation of sour-tasting gastric contents
  - Rarely, chronic GERD is punctuated by attacks of severe chest pain that may be mistaken for heart disease
- Treatment with **proton pump inhibitors** reduces gastric acidity and typically provides symptomatic relief
- Complications:
  - Esophageal ulceration
  - Hematemesis
  - Melena
  - Stricture development
  - **Barrett esophagus** (precursor lesion to esophageal carcinoma)

**Hiatal hernia:**
- Characterized by separation of the diaphragmatic crura and protrusion of the stomach into the thorax through the resulting gap
- Congenital hiatal hernias are recognized in infants and children; many are acquired in later life
- Symptomatic in fewer than 10% of adults
- When present, symptoms resemble GERD; hiatal hernia can cause LES incompetence

### Eosinophilic Esophagitis

A chronic immunologically mediated disorder that is being increasingly recognized.

**Symptoms:**
- Adults: food impaction and dysphagia
- Children: feeding intolerance or GERD-like symptoms

**Cardinal histologic feature:**
- Epithelial infiltration by large numbers of **eosinophils** (particularly superficially) (Fig. 15.9B)
- Eosinophils at sites far from the gastroesophageal junction also help differentiate from GERD, Crohn disease, and other causes of esophagitis

**Endoscopic findings:**
- Rings in the upper and mid portions of the esophagus (Fig. 15.9C)

**Key features:**
- Patients typically refractory to high-dose proton pump inhibitor treatment
- Most patients are atopic
- Many have atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, asthma, or modest peripheral eosinophilia

**Treatments:**
- Dietary restrictions to prevent exposure to food allergens (cow milk, soy products)
- Topical or systemic corticosteroids

**Fig. 15.9 Esophagitis.** (A) Reflux esophagitis with scattered intraepithelial eosinophils. (B) Eosinophilic esophagitis with numerous intraepithelial eosinophils. (C) Circumferential rings in the proximal esophagus of a patient with eosinophilic esophagitis.

### Barrett Esophagus

A complication of **chronic GERD** characterized by **intestinal metaplasia** within the esophageal squamous mucosa.

**Epidemiology:**
- Estimated to occur in as many as 10% of individuals with symptomatic GERD
- Incidence is rising
- White males are most often affected, typically between 40 and 60 years of age
- **Greatest concern:** confers an increased risk for development of esophageal adenocarcinoma

**Molecular studies suggest:**
- Barrett epithelium may be more similar genetically to adenocarcinoma than to normal esophageal epithelium
- Barrett esophagus is a direct precursor of cancer
- Epithelial dysplasia (preinvasive lesion) develops in 0.2% to 1% of individuals with Barrett esophagus each year
- Incidence of dysplasia increases with duration of symptoms and increasing patient age
- **Note:** Most individuals with Barrett esophagus do not develop esophageal cancer

**Morphology:**
- Recognized endoscopically as tongues or patches of red, velvety mucosa extending upward from the gastroesophageal junction (Fig. 15.10A)
- This metaplastic mucosa alternates with:
  - Residual smooth, pale squamous (esophageal) mucosa proximally
  - Light-brown columnar (gastric) mucosa distally (Fig. 15.10B and C)
- High-resolution endoscopes have increased the sensitivity of Barrett esophagus detection
- Most experts require both:
  - Endoscopic evidence of abnormal mucosa above the gastroesophageal junction
  - Histologically documented gastric or intestinal metaplasia

**Defining feature of intestinal metaplasia (Barrett esophagus):**
- Presence of **goblet cells** — distinct mucous vacuoles that stain pale blue by H&E and impart the shape of a wine goblet to the remaining cytoplasm (Fig. 15.10C)

**Dysplasia classification:**
- **Low-grade** or **high-grade** based on morphologic criteria
- **Intramucosal carcinoma:** invasion of neoplastic epithelial cells into the lamina propria

**Clinical Features:**
- Diagnosis usually prompted by GERD symptoms
- Requires endoscopy and biopsy
- Best course of management is debated
- Most clinicians recommend periodic surveillance endoscopy with biopsy to screen for dysplasia
- Dysplasia is often treated
- More advanced lesions (high-grade dysplasia and intramucosal carcinoma) always require therapeutic intervention
- Available modalities:
  - Surgical resection (esophagectomy)
  - Photodynamic therapy
  - Radiofrequency ablation
  - Endoscopic mucosectomy

**Fig. 15.10 Barrett esophagus.** (A) Narrow band imaging showing tan islands of ectopic metaplasia within bluish squamous mucosa. (B) Gross image of Barrett esophagus. Only a few areas of pale squamous mucosa remain within the predominantly metaplastic, reddish mucosa of the distal esophagus. (C) Histologic appearance of the gastroesophageal junction in Barrett esophagus. Transition between esophageal squamous mucosa (lower right) and metaplastic mucosa containing goblet cells (upper).

## Esophageal Tumors

Two morphologic variants account for a majority of esophageal cancers:
1. **Adenocarcinoma**
2. **Squamous cell carcinoma**

Worldwide, squamous cell carcinoma is more common, but adenocarcinoma is on the rise.

### Adenocarcinoma

**Epidemiology:**
- Typically arises in a background of Barrett esophagus and long-standing GERD
- Risk factors:
  - Documented dysplasia
  - Tobacco use
  - Obesity
  - Previous radiation therapy
- In the United States: most frequently in whites
- **Strong gender bias:** seven times more common in men than in women
- **Incidence varies by a factor of 60-fold worldwide:**
  - Highest in Western countries (United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Netherlands)
  - Lowest in Korea, Thailand, Japan, and Ecuador
- Incidence has increased markedly since 1970 (more rapidly than for almost any other cancer)
- Before 1970: less than 5% of esophageal cancers
- Now: accounts for half of all esophageal cancers in some Western countries

**Pathogenesis:**
- Progression of Barrett esophagus to adenocarcinoma occurs over an extended period through stepwise acquisition of genetic and epigenetic changes
- Epithelial clones identified in nondysplastic Barrett metaplasia persist and accumulate mutations during progression to dysplasia and invasive carcinoma
- Chromosomal abnormalities and **TP53 mutation** are often present in early stages
- Additional genetic changes and inflammation contribute to tumor progression

**Morphology:**
- Usually occurs in the distal third of the esophagus and may invade the adjacent gastric cardia (Fig. 15.11A)
- Early lesions may appear as flat or raised patches in otherwise intact mucosa
- Later tumors may form large exophytic masses, infiltrate diffusely, or ulcerate and invade deeply
- Barrett esophagus frequently is present adjacent to the tumor
- Tumors typically produce mucin and form glands (Fig. 15.11B)

**Clinical Features:**
- Most commonly present with:
  - Pain or difficulty in swallowing
  - Progressive weight loss
  - Chest pain
  - Vomiting
- By the time signs and symptoms appear, the tumor usually has invaded submucosal lymphatic vessels
- **Overall 5-year survival rate: less than 25%**
- 5-year survival approximates **80%** in the few patients with adenocarcinoma limited to the mucosa or submucosa

**Fig. 15.11 Esophageal adenocarcinoma.** (A) Adenocarcinoma usually occurs distally and often involves the gastric cardia. (B) Esophageal adenocarcinoma growing as back-to-back glands.

### Squamous Cell Carcinoma

**Epidemiology:**
- In the United States: typically occurs in adults older than 45 years of age
- Males affected four times more frequently than females
- **Risk factors:**
  - Alcohol and tobacco use
  - Poverty
  - Caustic esophageal injury
  - Achalasia
  - Plummer-Vinson syndrome
  - Frequent consumption of very hot beverages
  - Previous radiation therapy to the mediastinum
- Nearly six times more common in African Americans than in whites (striking risk disparity not accounted for by differences in alcohol and tobacco use)
- Incidence can vary by more than 100-fold between and within countries (more common in rural and underdeveloped areas)
- Countries with highest incidences: Iran, central China, Hong Kong, Argentina, Brazil, and South Africa

**Pathogenesis:**
- A majority of esophageal squamous cell carcinomas in Europe and the United States are at least partially related to alcohol and tobacco use (synergistic effects)
- In regions where alcohol and tobacco use are uncommon, nutritional deficiencies and exposure to polycyclic hydrocarbons, nitrosamines, and other mutagenic compounds (such as those found in fungus-contaminated foods) are suspected risk factors
- **HPV infection** has also been implicated in high-risk but not low-risk regions
- Molecular pathogenesis remains incompletely defined

**Morphology:**
- Half of squamous cell carcinomas occur in the middle third of the esophagus (Fig. 15.12A) — in contrast to the distal location of most adenocarcinomas
- Begins as an in situ lesion in the form of **squamous dysplasia**
- Early lesions: small, gray-white plaquelike thickenings
- Over months to years, grow into tumor masses that may be:
  - Polypoid and protrude into and obstruct the lumen
  - Ulcerated
  - Diffusely infiltrative lesions that cause thickening, rigidity, and luminal narrowing
- May invade surrounding structures:
  - Respiratory tree (causing pneumonia)
  - Aorta (causing catastrophic exsanguination)
  - Mediastinum and pericardium
- Most squamous cell carcinomas are moderately to well-differentiated (Fig. 15.12B)
- Regardless of histology, symptomatic tumors have often invaded the esophageal wall at time of diagnosis
- The rich submucosal lymphatic network promotes circumferential and longitudinal spread
- Intramural tumor nodules may be present several centimeters away from the main mass
- **Sites of lymph node metastases vary with tumor location:**
  - Upper third → cervical lymph nodes
  - Middle third → mediastinal, paratracheal, and tracheobronchial nodes
  - Lower third → gastric and celiac nodes

**Clinical Features:**
- Begin insidiously and include:
  - Dysphagia
  - Odynophagia (pain on swallowing)
  - Obstruction
- Patients may unconsciously adjust to progressively increasing obstruction by altering diet from solid to liquid foods
- Extreme weight loss and debilitation may occur from impaired nutrition and tumor-associated cachexia
- Hemorrhage and sepsis may accompany tumor ulceration
- Occasionally, squamous cell carcinomas of the upper and mid esophagus present with symptoms caused by aspiration of food via a tracheoesophageal fistula
- **Early detection is critical:**
  - 5-year survival rate: 75% for patients with superficial esophageal carcinoma
  - Much lower for patients with more advanced tumors
- **Overall 5-year survival rate: 9%**

**Fig. 15.12 Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.** (A) Squamous cell carcinoma most frequently is found in the mid-esophagus, where it commonly causes strictures. (B) Squamous cell carcinoma composed of nests of malignant cells that partially recapitulate the stratified organization of squamous epithelium.

#### Summary: Diseases of the Esophagus

- **Achalasia** is a motor disorder characterized by failure of LES relaxation and absence of esophageal peristalsis.
- **Barrett esophagus** represents intestinal metaplasia of the esophageal squamous epithelium as a complication of chronic GERD; it predisposes to adenocarcinoma.
- **Esophageal adenocarcinoma** arises in the distal esophagus in a background of Barrett esophagus and is increasing in frequency.
- **Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma** is linked to alcohol and tobacco use and typically arises in the mid-esophagus.

## Stomach

The stomach is subdivided into:
- **Cardia**
- **Fundus**
- **Body**
- **Antrum**
- **Pylorus**

The gastric mucosa contains:
- **Surface mucous cells** (form a continuous blanket line the entire gastric surface)
- **Mucous neck cells** (found in the neck of each gastric gland)
- **Parietal (oxyntic) cells** (secrete hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor; found in the body and fundus)
- **Chief (zymogenic) cells** (secrete pepsinogen; found in the body and fundus)
- **G cells** (secrete gastrin; found predominantly in the antrum)
- **D cells** (secrete somatostatin; found throughout the stomach)
- **Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells** (secrete histamine; found in the body and fundus)

### Acute Gastritis

**Acute gastritis** is an acute inflammatory process of the gastric mucosa that is most often caused by:
- **Acute excessive alcohol consumption**
- **Acute stress**
- **Aspirin and other NSAIDs**
- **Systemic stress** from severe illness

**Morphology:**
- Neutrophilic infiltration of the gastric mucosa
- Mucosal erosion (superficial)
- Mucosal ulceration (deep penetration)
- Hemorrhage (extravasation of blood from damaged vessels)
- **The depth of injury distinguishes acute erosive gastritis from acute ulceration**

**Clinical Features:**
- Epigastric pain
- Nausea and vomiting
- Hematemesis (coffee-ground or frankly bloody)
- Melena

### Stress-Related Gast mucosal Disease

**Stress ulcers** are acute mucosal lesions that arise in critically ill patients. Predisposing conditions include:
- **Hypotension**
- **Respiratory failure**
- **Severe trauma**
- **Head injury (Cushing ulcers)**
- **Severe burns (Curling ulcers)**
- **Sepsis**
- **Hepatic failure**
- **Renal failure**
- **Intracranial disease**
- **Major surgery**

**More than 75% of critically ill patients develop endoscopically visible gastric lesions during the first 3 days of their illness.**

**Named stress ulcers:**
- **Stress ulcers** affecting critically ill patients with shock, sepsis, or severe trauma
- **Curling ulcers** occur in the proximal duodenum and are associated with severe burns or trauma
- **Cushing ulcers** arise in the stomach, duodenum, or esophagus of those with intracranial disease and have a high incidence of perforation

**Pathogenesis of stress-related gastric mucosal injury:**
- Most often due to **local ischemia**
- May be caused by:
  - Systemic hypotension
  - Reduced blood flow resulting from stress-induced splanchnic vasoconstriction
- **Upregulation and increased release of the vasoconstrictor endothelin-1** contributes to ischemic gastric mucosal injury
- **Increased COX-2 expression** appears to be protective
- **Cushing ulcers** are thought to be caused by direct stimulation of vagal nuclei resulting in acid hypersecretion
- **Systemic acidosis** may also contribute to mucosal injury by lowering the intracellular pH of mucosal cells

**Morphology:**
- Ranges from shallow erosions (superficial epithelial damage) to deeper lesions that penetrate the depth of the mucosa
- **Acute ulcers:** rounded, typically less than 1 cm in diameter
- The ulcer base frequently is stained brown to black by acid-digested extravasated red cells
- Unlike peptic ulcers, acute stress ulcers are found anywhere in the stomach and are often multiple
- Sharply demarcated, with essentially normal adjacent mucosa (may be suffusion of blood into the mucosa and submucosa and some inflammatory reaction)
- **Scarring and thickening of blood vessels that characterize chronic peptic ulcers are absent**
- Healing with complete reepithelialization occurs days or weeks after the injurious factors are removed

**Clinical Features:**
- Most critically ill patients admitted to hospital intensive care units have histologic evidence of gastric mucosal damage
- Ulcers are associated with:
  - Nausea
  - Vomiting
  - Melena
  - Coffee-ground hematemesis
- Bleeding from superficial gastric erosions or ulcers requiring transfusion develops in 1% to 4% of these patients
- Other complications (including perforation) also may occur
- Prophylaxis with proton pump inhibitors may blunt the impact of stress ulceration
- **The most important determinant of outcome is the severity of the underlying condition**

### Chronic Gastritis

The most common cause of chronic gastritis is infection with the bacillus **Helicobacter pylori**. **Autoimmune gastritis**, typically associated with gastric atrophy, represents less than 10% of cases of chronic gastritis but is the most common cause in patients without H. pylori infection. **Chronic NSAID use** is a third important cause. Less common causes include radiation injury and chronic bile reflux.

The signs and symptoms associated with chronic gastritis are typically less severe but more persistent than those of acute gastritis: nausea and upper-abdominal discomfort may occur, sometimes with vomiting, but hematemesis is uncommon.

#### Helicobacter pylori Gastritis

The discovery of the association of **H. pylori** with peptic ulcer disease revolutionized the understanding of chronic gastritis. These spiral-shaped or curved bacilli are present...

#### Helicobacter pylori Gastritis (Continued)

**H. pylori** organisms have adapted to the ecologic niche provided by gastric mucus. Although H. pylori may invade the gastric mucosa, the contribution of invasion to disease pathogenesis is not known. Four features are linked to H. pylori virulence:

- **Flagella** — allow the bacteria to be motile in viscous mucus
- **Urease** — generates ammonia from endogenous urea, thereby elevating local gastric pH around the organisms and protecting the bacteria from the acidic pH of the stomach
- **Adhesins** — enhance bacterial adherence to surface foveolar cells
- **Toxins** — such as that encoded by **cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA)**, that may be involved in ulcer or cancer development

These factors allow H. pylori to create an imbalance between gastroduodenal mucosal defenses and damaging forces.

**Morphology:**
- H. pylori is concentrated within mucus overlying epithelial cells in the surface and neck regions
- Inflammatory reaction includes:
  - Neutrophils within the lamina propria (some cross the basement membrane to assume an intraepithelial location)
  - Accumulation of neutrophils in the lumen of gastric pits (creating **pit abscesses**)
  - Large numbers of plasma cells (often in clusters or sheets) in the superficial lamina propria
  - Increased numbers of lymphocytes and macrophages
- **Lymphoid aggregates** (some with germinal centers) are frequently present — represents an induced form of **mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT)** that has the potential to transform into lymphoma
- **Intestinal metaplasia** (goblet cells and columnar absorptive cells) also may be present and is associated with increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma
- H. pylori shows tropism for gastric foveolar epithelium and generally is not found in areas of intestinal metaplasia or acid-producing mucosa of the gastric body
- **Antral biopsies are preferred** for evaluation of H. pylori gastritis

**Clinical Features:**
- Diagnosis: histologic identification of the organism, serologic test for anti–H. pylori antibodies, stool test, urea breath test, rapid urease test, bacterial culture, or PCR assay
- Effective treatments: combinations of antibiotics and proton pump inhibitors
- Patients with H. pylori gastritis usually improve after treatment, although relapses can follow incomplete eradication or reinfection

#### Autoimmune Gastritis

**Autoimmune gastritis** accounts for less than 10% of cases of chronic gastritis. In contrast to H. pylori-associated gastritis, autoimmune gastritis typically **spares the antrum** and induces **marked hypergastrinemia**.

**Characteristics:**
- **Antibodies to parietal cells and intrinsic factor** that can be detected in serum and gastric secretions
- **Reduced serum pepsinogen I levels**
- **Antral endocrine cell hyperplasia**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Associated with immune-mediated loss of parietal cells and subsequent reductions in acid and intrinsic factor secretion
- Deficient acid secretion stimulates gastrin release, resulting in hypergastrinemia and hyperplasia of antral gastrin-producing G cells
- Lack of intrinsic factor disables ileal vitamin B12 absorption, leading to **vitamin B12 deficiency** and **pernicious anemia** (Chapter 12)
- Reduced serum concentration of pepsinogen I reflects chief cell loss

**Morphology:**
- Diffuse damage of the oxyntic (acid-producing) mucosa within the body and fundus
- Damage to the antrum and cardia typically is absent or mild
- With diffuse atrophy, the oxyntic mucosa appears markedly thinned, and rugal folds are lost
- Neutrophils may be present, but the inflammatory infiltrate more commonly is composed of lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells
- Parietal and chief cell loss can be extensive, and intestinal metaplasia may develop

**Clinical Features:**
- Antibodies to parietal cells and intrinsic factor are present early in the disease, but B12 deficiency and pernicious anemia develop in only a minority of patients
- Median age at diagnosis is 60 years, with a slight female predominance
- Often associated with other autoimmune diseases
- Not linked to specific HLA alleles

**Table 15.2 Characteristics of H. pylori–Associated and Autoimmune Gastritis:**

| Feature | H. pylori–Associated | Autoimmune |
|---------|---------------------|------------|
| **Location** | Antrum | Body |
| **Inflammatory infiltrate** | Neutrophils, subepithelial plasma cells | Lymphocytes, macrophages |
| **Acid production** | Increased to slightly decreased | Normal to markedly decreased |
| **Gastrin** | Decreased | Markedly increased |
| **Other lesions** | Hyperplastic/inflammatory polyps; antibodies to H. pylori | Neuroendocrine hyperplasia; antibodies to parietal cells (H+,K+-ATPase, intrinsic factor) |
| **Sequelae** | Peptic ulcer, adenocarcinoma, lymphoma | Atrophy, pernicious anemia, adenocarcinoma, carcinoid tumor |
| **Associations** | Low socioeconomic status, poverty, residence in rural areas | Autoimmune disease; thyroiditis, diabetes mellitus, Graves disease |

## Complications of Chronic Gastritis

Three important complications of chronic gastritis:
1. **Peptic ulcer disease**
2. **Mucosal atrophy and intestinal metaplasia**
3. **Dysplasia**

### Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

**Peptic ulcer disease** most often is associated with **H. pylori infection** or **NSAID use**. In the United States, NSAID use is becoming the most common cause of gastric ulcers as H. pylori infection rates are falling and the aging population is increasing.

**Epidemiology:**
- Common; leads to treatment of over 3 million individuals, 190,000 hospitalizations, and 5,000 deaths in the United States each year
- Lifetime risk for developing an ulcer: approximately 10% for males and 4% for females

**Pathogenesis:**
- More than 70% of PUD cases are associated with H. pylori infection
- Only 5% to 10% of H. pylori–infected individuals develop ulcers (host factors and variation among H. pylori strains contribute)
- **Gastric acid** is fundamental to the pathogenesis
- **Hyperacidity** may be caused by H. pylori infection, parietal cell hyperplasia, and excessive secretory responses
- **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome**: multiple peptic ulcerations caused by uncontrolled gastrin release by a tumor
- Cofactors:
  - Chronic NSAID use
  - Cigarette smoking (impairs mucosal blood flow and healing)
  - High-dose corticosteroids
- Peptic ulcers are more frequent in individuals with alcoholic cirrhosis, COPD, chronic renal failure, and hyperparathyroidism

**Morphology:**
- Four times more common in the proximal duodenum than in the stomach
- Duodenal ulcers: usually within a few centimeters of the pyloric valve, involving the anterior duodenal wall
- Gastric peptic ulcers: predominantly located near the interface of the body and antrum
- **Solitary in more than 80% of patients**
- Lesions less than 0.3 cm are shallow; those over 0.6 cm are likely deeper
- Classic peptic ulcer: **round to oval, sharply punched-out defect**
- Base of peptic ulcers is smooth and clean; histologically composed of richly vascular granulation tissue
- Ongoing bleeding within the ulcer base may cause life-threatening hemorrhage
- Perforation requires emergent surgical intervention

**Clinical Features:**
- Chronic, recurring lesions; most often in middle-aged to older adults
- Most come to clinical attention after complaints of **epigastric burning or aching pain**
- Pain tends to occur 1 to 3 hours after meals, is worse at night, and is relieved by alkali or food
- Complications: iron deficiency anemia, frank hemorrhage, perforation
- Current therapies: H. pylori eradication with antibiotics and proton pump inhibitors

### Mucosal Atrophy and Intestinal Metaplasia

- Long-standing chronic gastritis may be associated with **intestinal metaplasia** (goblet cells)
- Strongly associated with increased risk for development of **gastric adenocarcinoma**
- Achlorhydria of gastric mucosal atrophy permits overgrowth of bacteria that produce carcinogenic nitrosamines
- Intestinal metaplasia caused by chronic H. pylori gastritis **may regress after eradication of the organism**

### Dysplasia

- Chronic gastritis exposes the epithelium to inflammation-related free radical damage and proliferative stimuli
- Over time, this leads to accumulation of genetic alterations that result in carcinoma
- **Dysplasia** is marked by:
  - Variations in epithelial size, shape, and orientation
  - Coarse chromatin texture
  - Hyperchromasia
  - Nuclear enlargement
- Overlaps with and sometimes difficult to distinguish from injury-associated regenerative changes

#### Summary: Acute and Chronic Gastritis

- **Gastritis** is a mucosal inflammatory process. When inflammatory cells are absent or rare, the term **gastropathy** can be applied.
- The spectrum of acute gastritis ranges from asymptomatic disease to mild epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting.
- The most common cause of chronic gastritis is **H. pylori infection**; most remaining cases are caused by NSAIDs, alcohol, or autoimmune gastritis.
- **H. pylori gastritis** typically affects the antrum and is associated with increased gastric acid production.
- H. pylori gastritis induces **mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT)** that can give rise to B-cell lymphomas (MALTomas).
- **Autoimmune gastritis** causes atrophy of the gastric body oxyntic glands, resulting in decreased gastric acid production, antral G-cell hyperplasia, achlorhydria, and vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Intestinal metaplasia develops in both forms of chronic gastritis and is a risk factor for development of gastric dysplasia and adenocarcinoma.
- **Peptic ulcer disease** can be caused by H. pylori chronic gastritis and NSAID use. Ulcers can develop in the stomach or duodenum and usually heal after suppression of gastric acid production, discontinuation of NSAID use, or H. pylori eradication.

## Gastric Polyps and Tumors

### Gastric Polyps

**Gastric polyps** are nodules or masses that project above the level of the surrounding mucosa. They are identified in up to 5% of upper gastrointestinal tract endoscopies. Polyps may develop as a result of epithelial or stromal cell hyperplasia, inflammation, ectopia, or neoplasia.

#### Inflammatory and Hyperplastic Polyps

- Up to 75% of all gastric polyps are inflammatory or hyperplastic polyps
- Represent opposite ends of the morphologic spectrum of a single entity (distinction based on degree of inflammation)
- Most commonly affect individuals between 50 and 60 years of age
- Usually arise in a background of chronic gastritis
- If associated with H. pylori gastritis, polyps may regress after bacterial eradication
- **Dysplasia** risk correlates with size; significant increase with polyps larger than 1.5 cm

#### Fundic Gland Polyps

- Occur sporadically and in individuals with **familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)**
- Polyps associated with FAP (but not sporadic) may show dysplasia, but almost never progress to malignant
- Incidence of sporadic lesions has increased markedly due to widespread use of proton pump inhibitors (increased gastrin secretion and glandular hyperplasia)
- Nearly always asymptomatic; usually an incidental finding
- Well-circumscribed polyps in the gastric body and fundus
- Often multiple
- Composed of cystically dilated, irregular glands lined by flattened parietal and chief cells

#### Gastric Adenoma

- Represent up to 10% of all gastric polyps
- Incidence increases with age
- Males affected three times more often than females
- Almost always occur on a background of chronic gastritis with atrophy and intestinal metaplasia
- All gastrointestinal adenomas exhibit **epithelial dysplasia** (low- or high-grade)
- Risk for development of adenocarcinoma is related to lesion size; particularly elevated with lesions greater than 2 cm in diameter
- **Malignant potential of gastric adenomas is far greater than that of colonic counterparts**
- **Carcinoma may be present in up to 30% of gastric adenomas**

### Gastric Adenocarcinoma

**Adenocarcinoma** is the most common malignancy of the stomach, comprising more than 90% of all gastric cancers.

**Early symptoms resemble chronic gastritis:**
- Dyspepsia
- Dysphagia
- Nausea

**Advanced manifestations:**
- Weight loss
- Anorexia
- Altered bowel habits
- Anemia
- Hemorrhage

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence up to 20 times higher in Japan, Chile, Costa Rica, and Eastern Europe than in North America, northern Europe, Africa, and Southeast Asia
- Mass endoscopic screening in Japan: 35% of newly detected cases are early gastric cancer (limited to mucosa and submucosa)
- Less than 20% detected at early stage in North America and northern Europe
- More common in lower socioeconomic groups
- Rates in the United States have dropped by more than 85% during the 20th century
- **Cancer of the gastric cardia is on the rise** (related to increased Barrett esophagus, obesity, and chronic GERD)

**Pathogenesis — Genetic heterogeneity:**

**1. Mutations:**
- **CDH1** (encodes E-cadherin): germ line mutations associated with familial diffuse gastric cancers; present in about 50% of sporadic diffuse tumors
- Loss of E-cadherin function seems to be a key step in diffuse gastric cancer
- **APC** mutations: associated with increased risk for intestinal-type gastric cancer in FAP patients
- Sporadic intestinal-type gastric cancer associated with: acquired mutations of β-catenin, microsatellite instability, hypermethylation of TGFβRII, BAX, IGFRII, and p16/INK4a
- **TP53 mutations** present in a majority of sporadic gastric cancers

**2. H. pylori:** Chronic gastritis promotes development and progression of cancers through diverse genetic alterations. Polymorphisms of genes encoding proinflammatory proteins (IL-1β, TNF) confer increased risk.

**3. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV):** Approximately 10% of gastric adenocarcinomas are EBV-associated. EBV episomes in these tumors are frequently clonal. TP53 mutations are uncommon in EBV-positive gastric tumors. Morphologically, these tumors tend to occur in the proximal stomach and have a diffuse morphology with a marked lymphocytic infiltrate.

**Morphology — Lauren Classification:**

**Intestinal-type cancers:**
- Tend to be bulky (Fig. 15.16A)
- Composed of glandular structures similar to esophageal and colonic adenocarcinoma
- Grow along broad cohesive fronts to form either an exophytic mass or an ulcerated tumor
- Neoplastic cells often contain apical mucin vacuoles; abundant mucin may be present in gland lumina

**Diffuse gastric cancers:**
- Display an infiltrative growth pattern (Fig. 15.16B)
- Composed of discohesive cells with large mucin vacuoles that expand the cytoplasm and push the nucleus to the periphery (**signet ring cell morphology**)
- Cells permeate the mucosa and stomach wall individually or in small clusters
- May evoke a desmoplastic reaction that stiffens the gastric wall
- May cause diffuse rugal flattening and a rigid, thickened wall — **linitis plastica** ("leather bottle" appearance)

**Clinical Features:**
- **Intestinal-type:** predominates in high-risk areas; mean age 55 years; male-to-female ratio 2:1
- **Diffuse-type:** relatively uniform incidence across countries; similar frequencies in males and females
- The decrease in gastric cancer incidence applies only to the intestinal type
- **Depth of invasion** and **extent of nodal and distant metastasis** are the most powerful prognostic indicators
- **5-year survival for early gastric cancer: exceeds 90%** (even with lymph node metastases)
- **5-year survival for advanced gastric cancer: below 20%**
- Treatment: surgical resection; patients whose tumors overexpress HER2 benefit from HER2 inhibitors
- Overall 5-year survival in the United States: less than 30%

**Fig. 15.16 Gastric adenocarcinoma.** (A) Intestinal-type adenocarcinoma consisting of an elevated mass with heaped-up borders and central ulceration. (B) Linitis plastica. The gastric wall is markedly thickened, and rugal folds are partially lost. (C) Signet ring cells with large cytoplasmic mucin vacuoles and peripherally displaced, crescent-shaped nuclei.

### Lymphoma

- Nearly 5% of all gastric malignancies are primary lymphomas
- Most common: **indolent extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphomas** (lymphomas of MALT, or **MALTomas**)
- Second most common: **diffuse large B-cell lymphoma** (Chapter 12)

### Neuroendocrine (Carcinoid) Tumor

**Neuroendocrine tumors** arise from neuroendocrine-differentiated gastrointestinal epithelia (e.g., G cells).

- More than 40% occur in the small intestine
- Tracheobronchial tree and lungs are the next most commonly involved sites
- Gastric neuroendocrine tumors may be associated with endocrine cell hyperplasia, chronic atrophic gastritis, and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
- **WHO classification:** low- or intermediate-grade neuroendocrine tumors; high-grade **neuroendocrine carcinoma** resembles small cell carcinoma of the lung (Chapter 13)

**Prognostic factors by location:**
- **Foregut** (stomach, duodenum proximal to ligament of Treitz, esophagus): rarely metastasize; generally cured by resection
  - Duodenal gastrin-producing neuroendocrine tumors (gastrinomas) may present with increased acid production, pain, bleeding, or diarrhea
- **Midgut** (jejunum and ileum): often multiple; tend to be aggressive. Prognosis based on depth of local invasion, size, and presence of necrosis and mitoses
- **Hindgut** (appendix and colorectum): typically discovered incidentally; appendix tumors almost uniformly pursue a benign course

**Morphology:**
- Intramural or submucosal masses that create small polypoid lesions (Fig. 15.17A)
- Yellow or tan in appearance
- Elicit an intense desmoplastic reaction that may cause kinking of the bowel and obstruction
- Composed of islands, trabeculae, strands, glands, or sheets of uniform cells with scant, pink granular cytoplasm and a round-to-oval stippled nucleus (Fig. 15.17B)
- **"Salt-and-pepper" chromatin pattern**

**Clinical Features:**
- Peak incidence in the sixth decade
- Symptoms determined by hormones produced
- **Carcinoid syndrome:** cutaneous flushing, sweating, bronchospasm, colicky abdominal pain, diarrhea, and right-sided cardiac valvular fibrosis
- Occurs in less than 10% of patients and is strongly associated with metastatic disease (vasoactive substances are metabolized by the liver — "first-pass" effect)

**Fig. 15.17 Gastrointestinal carcinoid tumor (neuroendocrine tumor).** (A) Carcinoid tumors often form a submucosal nodule composed of tumor cells embedded in dense fibrous tissue. (B) Bland cytology with "salt-and-pepper" chromatin pattern.

### Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST)

**GIST** is the most common mesenchymal tumor of the abdomen; more than half occur in the stomach.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Most common genetic change: **gain-of-function mutations of KIT** (tyrosine kinase, receptor for stem cell factor) — present in 75%–85% of all GISTs
- Additional 8%: mutations that activate **PDGFRA** (platelet-derived growth factor receptor A)
- Arise from the **interstitial cells of Cajal** (pacemaker cells) of the gastrointestinal muscularis propria
- GISTs bearing PDGFRA mutations are overrepresented in the stomach
- KIT and PDGFRA mutations are mutually exclusive
- Germ line mutations in these genes cause familial GISTs
- GISTs without KIT or PDGFRA mutations most commonly have mutations in genes encoding components of the **mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex** — confer increased risk for both GIST and paraganglioma
- Loss of SDH causes: increased reactive oxygen species, activation of HIF, increased dependency on glycolysis

**Morphology:**
- Usually a solitary, well-circumscribed, fleshy, submucosal mass
- Metastases may form multiple small serosal nodules or fewer large nodules in the liver
- Spread outside of the abdomen is uncommon
- Composed of thin, elongated spindle cells or plumper epithelioid cells
- Most useful diagnostic marker: **KIT** (immunohistochemically detectable in 95% of tumors)

**Clinical Features:**
- Peak incidence around 60 years of age; less than 10% occur in individuals younger than 40 years
- Slightly more common in males
- Small GISTs may be incidental findings
- Larger GISTs may present with mass effects, mucosal ulceration, intestinal obstruction, or gastrointestinal bleeding
- Treatment: complete surgical resection for localized gastric GIST
- Prognosis correlates with tumor size, mitotic index, and location (gastric GISTs somewhat less aggressive than small intestinal GISTs)
- Recurrence or metastasis: rare for GISTs less than 5 cm; common for mitotically active tumors larger than 10 cm
- Unresectable or metastatic GISTs often respond to **tyrosine kinase inhibitors** (imatinib); resistance may develop due to additional mutations

#### Summary: Gastric Polyps and Tumors

- Inflammatory and hyperplastic gastric polyps are reactive lesions associated with chronic gastritis. Risk for dysplasia increases with polyp size.
- Gastric adenomas develop in a background of chronic gastritis and are particularly associated with intestinal metaplasia and mucosal atrophy. Adenocarcinoma frequently arises in gastric adenomas (up to 30%).
- Gastric adenocarcinomas are classified as **intestinal-type** (bulky tumors) or **diffuse-type** (signet ring cells, linitis plastica).
- H. pylori infection is the most common etiologic agent for gastric adenocarcinoma.
- Primary gastric lymphomas most often are derived from MALT.
- Low- or intermediate-grade neuroendocrine (carcinoid) tumors are most common in the small intestine. Gastric carcinoids tend to be less aggressive.
- **GIST** (most common mesenchymal tumor of the abdomen) occurs most often in the stomach. A majority have activating mutations in KIT or PDGFRA and respond to tyrosine kinase inhibitors.

## Intestinal Obstruction

Obstruction of the gastrointestinal tract may occur at any level, but the small intestine is most often involved because of its relatively narrow lumen.

**Major causes (80% of mechanical obstructions):**
1. **Hernias**
2. **Intestinal adhesions**
3. **Intussusception**
4. **Volvulus**

Other causes: tumors and infarction.

**Clinical manifestations:**
- Abdominal pain and distention
- Vomiting
- Constipation

**Fig. 15.18 Intestinal obstruction.** The four major mechanical causes: (1) herniation, (2) adhesion between loops of intestine, (3) volvulus, and (4) intussusception.

### Intussusception

- Occurs when a segment of intestine telescopes into the immediately distal segment
- Once trapped, the invaginated segment is propelled by peristalsis and pulls the mesentery along
- May progress to intestinal obstruction, compression of mesenteric vessels, and infarction
- **Most common cause of intestinal obstruction in children younger than 2 years of age**
- Usually no underlying anatomic defect; other cases associated with viral infection and rotavirus vaccines (reactive hyperplasia of Peyer patches)
- Contrast enemas are diagnostically useful and effective in correcting idiopathic intussusception
- Surgical intervention necessary when an intraluminal mass or tumor serves as the initiating point of traction (typical in older children and adults)

### Hirschsprung Disease

- Occurs in approximately 1 of 5,000 live births
- Congenital defect in colonic innervation
- More common in males but more severe in females
- Siblings at increased risk
- Patients typically present as neonates with:
  - Failure to pass meconium in the immediate postnatal period
  - Obstructive constipation
- Major threats to life: enterocolitis, fluid and electrolyte disturbances, perforation, and peritonitis
- Treatment: surgical resection of the aganglionic segment with anastomosis of the normal colon to the rectum

**Pathogenesis:**
- Enteric neuronal plexus develops from neural crest cells that migrate into the bowel wall during embryogenesis
- **Hirschsprung disease** (congenital aganglionic megacolon) results from disrupted migration of neural crest cells from cecum to rectum
- Produces a distal intestinal segment that lacks both Meissner submucosal plexus and Auerbach myenteric plexus ("aganglionosis")
- **RET** mutations account for a majority of familial cases and approximately 15% of sporadic cases

**Morphology:**
- Always affects the rectum
- Length of additional involved segments varies
- Most cases limited to rectum and sigmoid colon
- Severe disease can involve the entire colon
- Aganglionic region may have a grossly normal or contracted appearance
- Normally innervated proximal colon may undergo progressive dilation (functional distal obstruction)
- Diagnosis: demonstrating absence of ganglion cells in the affected segment

**Fig. 15.19 Hirschsprung disease.** (A) Preoperative barium enema showing constricted rectum and dilated sigmoid colon. (B) Intraoperative appearance of the dilated sigmoid colon.

### Abdominal Hernia

- Any weakness or defect in the wall of the peritoneal cavity may permit protrusion of a serosa-lined pouch of peritoneum (hernia sac)
- Acquired hernias most commonly occur anteriorly:
  - Inguinal and femoral canals
  - Umbilicus
  - Sites of surgical scars
- Particularly concerning: inguinal hernias (narrow orifices and large sacs)
- Most often: small bowel loops herniate
- **Progression:** venous drainage impairment → edema → permanent entrapment (incarceration) → arterial and venous compromise (strangulation) → infarction

#### Summary: Intestinal Obstruction

- Intussusception is the most common cause of intestinal obstruction in children younger than 2 years of age and can usually be treated by barium enema or air enema.
- Hirschsprung disease results from defective neural crest cell migration from cecum to rectum. It gives rise to functional obstruction.
- Abdominal herniation may occur through any weakness or defect in the wall of the peritoneal cavity.

## Vascular Disorders of Bowel

The largest portion of the gastrointestinal tract is supplied by the **celiac**, **superior mesenteric**, and **inferior mesenteric arteries**.

**Causes of intestinal ischemia:**
- Acute arterial obstruction (severe atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm, hypercoagulable states, oral contraceptive use, embolization from cardiac vegetations or aortic atheromas)
- Intestinal hypoperfusion (cardiac failure, shock, dehydration, vasoconstrictive drugs)
- Systemic vasculitides
- Mesenteric venous thrombosis (uncommon; causes include invasive neoplasms, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, trauma, or abdominal masses)
- **Watershed zones:** segments at the end of their respective arterial supplies particularly susceptible to ischemia:
  - **Splenic flexure** (superior and inferior mesenteric arterial circulations terminate)
  - Sigmoid colon and rectum (inferior mesenteric, pudendal, and iliac arterial circulations end)

**Pathogenesis:**
- Severity depends on: severity of vascular compromise, time frame, and vessels affected
- **Initial hypoxic injury:** at onset of vascular compromise; intestinal epithelial cells relatively resistant to transient hypoxia
- **Reperfusion injury:** greatest damage; involves free radical production, neutrophil infiltration, and inflammatory mediators (complement, cytokines)
- Surface epithelium particularly vulnerable to ischemic injury (capillary configuration)

### Ischemic Bowel Disease

**Morphology — Ranges from:**
- **Mucosal infarction** (no deeper than muscularis mucosa)
- **Mural infarction** (mucosa and submucosa)
- **Transmural infarction** (all three layers of the wall)

**Microscopic findings:**
- Atrophy or sloughing of surface epithelium (Fig. 15.20A)
- Crypts may be hyperproliferative
- Inflammatory infiltrates initially absent; neutrophils recruited within hours of reperfusion
- Chronic ischemia: fibrous scarring of the lamina propria and, uncommonly, stricture formation (Fig. 15.20B)

**Fig. 15.20 Ischemia.** (A) Attenuated and partially detached villous epithelium in acute jejunal ischemia. Note hyperchromatic nuclei of proliferating crypt cells. (B) Chronic colonic ischemia with atrophic surface epithelium and fibrotic lamina propria.

**Clinical Features:**
- Tends to occur in older adults with coexisting cardiac or vascular disease
- **Acute transmural infarction:** sudden, severe abdominal pain and tenderness; nausea, vomiting, bloody diarrhea, or melanotic stool; may progress to shock and vascular collapse within hours
- Physical signs overlap with acute appendicitis, perforated ulcer, and acute cholecystitis
- As mucosal barrier breaks down, bacteria enter the circulation and sepsis can develop; **mortality rate may exceed 50%**
- **Mucosal and mural infarctions** may not be fatal but may progress to transmural infarction if vascular supply not restored
- **Chronic ischemia** may masquerade as inflammatory bowel disease

**Other causes of bowel ischemia:**
- **CMV infection:** viral tropism for endothelial cells causes ischemic gastrointestinal disease
- **Radiation enterocolitis:** epithelial damage plus radiation-induced vascular injury
- **Necrotizing enterocolitis:** idiopathic disorder of small and large intestines; most common acquired gastrointestinal emergency of neonates (particularly premature or low birth weight); often associated with initiation of oral feeding
- **Angiodysplasia:** malformed submucosal and mucosal blood vessels; most often in cecum or right colon; usually presents after the sixth decade; accounts for 20% of major episodes of lower intestinal bleeding

### Hemorrhoids

- Dilated anal and perianal collateral vessels that connect the portal and caval venous systems
- Common (affecting about 5% of the general population)
- Predisposing factors: constipation and straining, pregnancy, portal hypertension

**Types:**
- **External hemorrhoids:** inferior hemorrhoidal plexus (below anorectal line)
- **Internal hemorrhoids:** superior hemorrhoidal plexus within the distal rectum

**Morphology:** thin-walled, dilated, submucosal vessels beneath anal or rectal mucosa

**Clinical features:**
- Subject to trauma (rectal bleeding — bright red blood on toilet tissue)
- Can become thrombosed and inflamed
- Treatment options: sclerotherapy, rubber band ligation, infrared coagulation, hemorrhoidectomy

#### Summary: Vascular Disorders of Bowel

- Intestinal ischemia can occur as a result of either arterial or venous obstruction.
- Ischemic bowel disease resulting from hypoperfusion is most common at watershed zones (splenic flexure, sigmoid colon, rectum).
- Systemic vasculitides and infectious diseases (e.g., CMV infection) can cause vascular disease.
- Angiodysplasia is a common cause of lower gastrointestinal bleeding in older adults.
- Hemorrhoids are collateral vessels that form in response to venous hypertension.

## Diarrheal Disease

**Diarrhea** is defined as an increase in stool mass, frequency, or fluidity (typically >200 grams per day). In severe cases, stool volume can exceed 14 L per day.

- Worldwide, diarrheal diseases account for greater than 700,000 deaths of children under 5 years of age (second leading cause of death in this age group)
- **Dysentery:** painful, bloody, small-volume diarrhea

**Classification:**

| Type | Characteristics |
|------|----------------|
| **Secretory diarrhea** | Isotonic stool; persists during fasting |
| **Osmotic diarrhea** | Due to osmotic forces from unabsorbed luminal solutes (e.g., lactase deficiency); stool at least 50 mOsm more concentrated than plasma; abates with fasting |
| **Malabsorptive diarrhea** | Associated with steatorrhea; relieved by fasting |
| **Exudative diarrhea** | Due to inflammatory disease; purulent, bloody stools that continue during fasting |

## Malabsorptive Diarrhea

**Malabsorption** manifests as chronic diarrhea and is characterized by defective absorption of fats, fat-and water-soluble vitamins, proteins, carbohydrates, electrolytes, minerals, and water.

**Hallmark:** **steatorrhea** — excessive fecal fat; bulky, frothy, greasy, yellow, or clay-colored stools

**Common causes in Western countries:**
1. **Pancreatic insufficiency**
2. **Celiac disease**
3. **Crohn disease**

**Phases of nutrient absorption:**
1. **Intraluminal digestion** — proteins, carbohydrates, and fats broken down into absorbable forms
2. **Terminal digestion** — hydrolysis of carbohydrates and peptides by disaccharidases and peptidases in the brush border
3. **Transepithelial transport** — nutrients, fluid, and electrolytes transported across and processed within the small-intestinal epithelium
4. **Lymphatic transport** — absorbed lipids

- Anemia and mucositis (pyridoxine, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiency)
- Bleeding (vitamin K deficiency)
- Osteopenia and tetany (calcium, magnesium, or vitamin D deficiency)
- Neuropathy (vitamin A or B12 deficiency)

### Cystic Fibrosis

- Discussed in detail in Chapter 7
- Due to mutations of **CFTR** (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator)
- Defects in intestinal and pancreatic ductal ion transport
- Results in: meconium ileus (up to 10% of newborns), pancreatic insufficiency (more than 80% of patients), failure of intraluminal phase of nutrient absorption
- Treated effectively with oral enzyme supplementation

### Celiac Disease

**Celiac disease** (celiac sprue, gluten-sensitive enteropathy) is an **immune-mediated enteropathy** triggered by ingestion of gluten-containing cereals (wheat, rye, barley) in genetically predisposed individuals.

- Estimated prevalence: 0.5% to 1% in countries with populations of white European ancestry
- Primary treatment: **gluten-free diet**

**Pathogenesis:**
- **Gluten** is digested into amino acids and peptides, including a **33–amino acid gliadin peptide** resistant to degradation by gastric, pancreatic, and small-intestinal proteases
- Gliadin is deamidated by **tissue transglutaminase** and interacts with **HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8** on antigen-presenting cells
- **CD4+ T cells** in lamina propria produce cytokines that contribute to tissue damage
- **Antibody response:** anti-tissue transglutaminase, anti-deamidated gliadin, anti-endomysial antibodies
- **CD8+ cells** (not specific for gliadin) play an ancillary role in tissue damage
- **IL-15** production by epithelial cells triggers activation and proliferation of CD8+ intraepithelial lymphocytes
- **Anti-gliadin, anti-endomysium, and anti-tTG antibodies** are produced
- Host factors (especially **HLA proteins**) are critical — almost all individuals with celiac disease carry HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 alleles
- Associated with other immune diseases: type 1 diabetes, thyroiditis, Sjögren syndrome

**Fig. 15.21 Celiac disease pathogenesis.** Morphologic alterations include villous atrophy, increased intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs), and crypt elongation. Pathogenesis involves both innate and adaptive immune mechanisms.

**Morphology:**
- Biopsy specimens from the second portion of the duodenum or proximal jejunum are generally diagnostic
- Histopathology characterized by:
  - **Increased intraepithelial T lymphocytes**
  - **Crypt hyperplasia**
  - **Villous atrophy** (Fig. 15.22)
- Loss of mucosal and brush-border surface area accounts for malabsorption
- Increased epithelial turnover may limit absorptive enterocyte differentiation
- Other features: increased plasma cells, mast cells, and eosinophils in lamina propria

**Fig. 15.22 Celiac disease.** (A) Advanced cases show complete loss of villi (total villous atrophy) with dense plasma cell infiltrates. (B) Infiltration of surface epithelium by T lymphocytes (densely stained nuclei labeled T).

**Clinical Features:**
- **Pediatric:** classic symptoms typically between 6 and 24 months of age (after introduction of gluten); irritability, abdominal distention, anorexia, diarrhea, failure to thrive, weight loss, muscle wasting
- **Children with nonclassic symptoms:** present at older ages with abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, bloating, or constipation
- **Dermatitis herpetiformis:** pruritic, blistering skin lesion present in up to 10% of patients
- **Adults:** most commonly between 30 and 60 years of age; many cases escape clinical attention
- **Silent celiac disease:** positive serology and villous atrophy without symptoms
- **Latent celiac disease:** positive serology without villous atrophy or symptoms
- Symptomatic adult celiac disease: anemia (iron deficiency; less commonly B12 and folate deficiency), diarrhea, bloating, fatigue
- **Associated with higher rate of malignancy:** most common is **enteropathy-associated T cell lymphoma** (aggressive tumor of intraepithelial T lymphocytes); also small-intestinal adenocarcinoma

**Serologic tests:**
- Most sensitive: IgA antibodies to tissue transglutaminase; IgA or IgG antibodies to deamidated gliadin
- Highly specific but less sensitive: anti-endomysial antibodies
- Titers typically fall after 3 to 6 months of gluten-free diet

**Important:** When symptoms develop despite strict gluten-free diet, cancer or **refractory sprue** (loss of response to gluten-free diet) must be considered. Failure to adhere to gluten-free diet is the most common cause of recurrent symptoms.

### Environmental Enteric Dysfunction

- Also known as **environmental enteropathy**
- Syndrome of stunted growth and impaired intestinal function
- Common in developing countries (sub-Saharan Africa, aboriginal populations in northern Australia, some groups in South America and Asia)
- Estimated to affect over 150 million children worldwide
- Repeated bouts of diarrhea in first 2–3 years of life most closely linked
- No single infectious agent established as causal
- Neither supplementary feeding nor vitamin/mineral supplementation fully reverses the syndrome

### Lactase (Disaccharidase) Deficiency

- Gives rise to **osmotic diarrhea** because osmotically active lactose remains in the lumen
- Disaccharidases (including lactase) located in the apical brush border membrane of villous absorptive epithelial cells
- Because the defect is biochemical, biopsies are generally unremarkable

**Two types:**
1. **Congenital lactase deficiency:** autosomal recessive; rare; manifests as explosive diarrhea with watery, frothy stools and abdominal distention after milk ingestion; symptoms abate when milk and milk products are terminated
2. **Acquired lactase deficiency:** downregulation of lactase gene expression; particularly common among Native Americans, African Americans, and Chinese populations; downregulation occurs after childhood; sometimes associated with enteric viral or bacterial infections

### Abetalipoproteinemia

- Autosomal recessive disease characterized by inability to secrete triglyceride-rich lipoproteins
- Mutation in **microsomal triglyceride transfer protein** renders enterocytes unable to export lipoproteins and free fatty acids
- Monoglycerides and triglycerides accumulate within epithelial cells
- Manifests in infancy with failure to thrive, diarrhea, and steatorrhea
- Failure to absorb essential fatty acids leads to deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins
- **Acanthocytic red blood cells** (spur cells) in peripheral blood smears

### Microscopic Colitis

**Two entities:**
1. **Collagenous colitis** — primarily middle-aged and older women; dense subepithelial collagen layer; increased intraepithelial lymphocytes; mixed inflammatory infiltrate in lamina propria
2. **Lymphocytic colitis** — similar but subepithelial collagen layer is of normal thickness; greater increase in intraepithelial T lymphocytes
- Both manifest with chronic, nonbloody, watery diarrhea without weight loss
- Radiologic and endoscopic studies typically normal
- Associated with celiac disease and autoimmune diseases (thyroiditis, arthritis, lymphocytic gastritis)

### Graft-Versus-Host Disease

- Occurs after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
- Small bowel and colon involved in most cases
- Caused by donor T cell–mediated damage to recipient's epithelial cells
- Lymphocytic infiltrate in lamina propria is typically sparse
- **Epithelial apoptosis** (particularly of crypt cells) is the most common histologic finding
- Often manifests as watery diarrhea

#### Summary: Malabsorptive Diarrhea

- Diarrhea can be characterized as secretory, osmotic, malabsorptive, or exudative.
- The malabsorption associated with cystic fibrosis is the result of pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Celiac disease** is an immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by gluten. The malabsorptive diarrhea is due to loss of brush border surface area and deficient enterocyte maturation.
- Lactase deficiency causes osmotic diarrhea owing to inability to break down or absorb lactose.
- Abetalipoproteinemia is characterized by inability to secrete triglyceride-rich lipoproteins due to an inherited transepithelial transport defect.
- The two forms of microscopic colitis (collagenous colitis and lymphocytic colitis) both cause chronic watery diarrhea. The intestines are grossly normal; diseases are identified by histologic features.

## Infectious Enterocolitis

- Responsible for more than 1 million deaths annually and greater than 10% of all deaths in patients younger than 5 years of age worldwide
- Presents with diarrhea, abdominal pain, urgency, perianal discomfort, incontinence, and hemorrhage
- Bacterial infections (such as enterotoxigenic E. coli) frequently responsible; most common pathogens vary with age, nutrition, host immune status, and environment
- **Table 15.4** summarizes epidemiology and clinical features of selected bacterial enterocolitides

### Cholera

**Vibrio cholerae** — comma-shaped, gram-negative bacteria

- Causes cholera, a disease that has plagued humanity throughout recorded history
- In the United States: associated with consumption of contaminated seafood (particularly raw oysters)
- Produces **cholera toxin** that activates adenylate cyclase, leading to increased cAMP and massive secretion of fluid and electrolytes into the intestinal lumen
- Manifests with rapid onset of profuse, watery diarrhea ("rice-water stools") and vomiting
- Can cause life-threatening dehydration and metabolic acidosis
- Treatment: fluid and electrolyte replacement; tetracycline may reduce duration of bacterial shedding
- Effective vaccines are available for travelers to endemic areas

## Colonic Polyps, Adenomas, and Adenocarcinomas

### Colonic Polyps

**Classification:**

**Nonneoplastic polyps:**
1. **Inflammatory polyps** — form as a result of chronic cycles of injury and healing
2. **Hamartomatous polyps** — occur sporadically or as part of genetic diseases; associated with increased risk for malignancy
3. **Hyperplastic polyps** — benign epithelial proliferations; most common in left colon and rectum; no malignant potential; must be distinguished from sessile serrated adenomas

**Neoplastic polyps (adenomas):**
- Benign epithelial neoplastic polyps of the colon
- **Hallmark: cytologic dysplasia** — precursor of colonic adenocarcinomas
- **Sessile serrated adenomas/polyps** — lack cytologic dysplasia; share some morphologic features with hyperplastic polyps

### Familial Colon Cancer Syndromes

**1. Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP):**
- Caused by **APC mutations**
- Patients typically have **over 100 adenomas**
- Develop colon cancer before 30 years of age

**2. Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer (HNPCC):**
- Caused by mutations in **DNA mismatch repair genes**
- Far fewer polyps than FAP
- Develop cancer at older age than FAP but younger than sporadic colon cancer

### Colorectal Adenocarcinoma

- **Vast majority of colonic cancers are adenocarcinomas**
- Two pathways:
  1. **APC-β-catenin pathway** (adenoma-carcinoma sequence)
  2. **Microsatellite instability pathway**

**Epidemiology:**
- Second most common cause of cancer death in the United States
- More than 90% occur after 50 years of age
- Incidence rates have been declining (attributed to increased screening and polyp removal)
- Right colon cancers have been increasing relative to left colon cancers

**Pathogenesis — Adenoma-Carcinoma Sequence (Fig. 15.36):**

**APC-β-catenin pathway:**
- **APC** (5q21) — tumor suppressor; "gatekeeper"; germ line or somatic mutations ("first hit")
- **β-catenin** accumulation → activation of Wnt signaling
- **K-RAS** (12p12) — proto-oncogene mutations
- **TP53** (17p13) — tumor suppressor mutations
- **LOH at 18q21** (SMAD2 and SMAD4)
- **COX-2 overexpression**
- **Telomerase activation**
- Progression: normal epithelium → small adenoma → large adenoma → carcinoma → metastases

**Microsatellite instability pathway:**
- Defective DNA mismatch repair
- Accelerated progression through adenoma-carcinoma sequence

**Morphology — Polypoid vs. flat lesions:**
- Most are polypoid (exophytic or ulcerated)
- Flat lesions are more common in high-risk populations and may be more aggressive
- **Adenocarcinoma:** malignant glands that invade through the muscularis mucosae into the submucosa

**Clinical Features:**
- Early stages: often asymptomatic
- Common presentations:
  - **Iron deficiency anemia** (right-sided lesions)
  - **Rectal bleeding** (left-sided lesions)
  - Change in bowel habits
  - Obstruction (advanced disease)
- **Screening colonoscopy** recommended starting at 45–50 years of age in average-risk individuals
- **Dukes staging** and **TNM staging** used for prognosis
- **Depth of invasion** and **lymph node/distant metastases** are the two most important prognostic factors

**Treatment:**
- Surgical resection (partial colectomy)
- **Adjuvant chemotherapy** for node-positive disease
- Targeted therapy: anti-EGFR antibodies (cetuximab, panitumumab) for tumors with wild-type KRAS
- **Neoadjuvant therapy:** radiation or chemotherapy before primary tumor resection

**Metastasis:**
- Lymphatic spread → regional lymph nodes
- Hematogenous spread → liver (via portal circulation), lung
- Rectal carcinoma → lung directly (bypasses portal circulation)
- 5-year survival for distant metastases: approximately 15%
- Some patients with limited metastases (liver, lung) do well for years after resection of distant tumor nodules

#### Summary: Colonic Polyps, Adenomas, and Adenocarcinomas

- Intestinal polyps can be classified as nonneoplastic or neoplastic. Nonneoplastic polyps can be inflammatory, hamartomatous, or hyperplastic.
- Hyperplastic polyps are benign; must be distinguished from sessile serrated adenomas.
- Benign epithelial neoplastic polyps are adenomas — the precursors of colonic adenocarcinomas.
- **FAP** (APC mutations): over 100 adenomas; cancer before 30 years of age.
- **HNPCC** (DNA mismatch repair gene mutations): fewer polyps; cancer at younger age than sporadic cases.
- The vast majority of colonic cancers are adenocarcinomas arising via APC-β-catenin or microsatellite instability pathways.
- Two most important prognostic factors: **depth of invasion** and **presence of metastases**.

## Appendix

The appendix is a normal true diverticulum of the cecum. It is prone to acute and chronic inflammation, and acute appendicitis is a relatively common entity.

### Acute Appendicitis

- Most common in adolescents and young adults
- Lifetime risk: 7%; males slightly more often than females
- May be confused with mesenteric lymphadenitis, acute salpingitis, ectopic pregnancy, mittelschmerz, and Meckel diverticulitis

**Pathogenesis:**
- Initiated by progressive increase in intraluminal pressure that compromises venous outflow
- In 50% to 80% of cases: overt luminal obstruction
  - Usually by a **fecalith** (stone-like mass of stool)
  - Less commonly: gallstone, tumor, or mass of worms
- Ischemic injury and stasis of luminal contents favor bacterial proliferation
- Triggers inflammatory responses: tissue edema and neutrophilic infiltration

**Morphology:**
- Early acute appendicitis: subserosal vessels congested; modest perivascular neutrophilic infiltrate within all layers
- Inflammatory reaction transforms glistening serosa into dull, granular-appearing, erythematous surface
- **Diagnosis requires neutrophilic infiltration of the muscularis propria**
- More severe cases:
  - Focal abscesses within the wall (acute suppurative appendicitis)
  - Hemorrhagic ulceration and gangrenous necrosis extending to the serosa (acute gangrenous appendicitis)
  - May progress to rupture and suppurative peritonitis

**Clinical Features:**
- **Classic sequence:** periumbilical pain → localization to right lower quadrant → nausea, vomiting, low-grade fever, mildly elevated WBC
- **McBurney's sign:** deep tenderness at a point two-thirds of the distance from the umbilicus to the right anterior superior iliac spine (McBurney's point)
- Diagnosis can be difficult; signs and symptoms often absent

### Tumors of the Appendix

**Carcinoid (well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor):**
- Most common tumor of the appendix
- Usually discovered incidentally at surgery or on examination of a resected appendix
- Most frequently involves the distal tip
- Produces solid bulbous swelling up to 2–3 cm in diameter
- Nodal metastases very infrequent; distant spread exceptionally rare
- **Almost always benign**

**Adenocarcinoma of the appendix:**
- Conventional adenomas or non–mucin-producing adenocarcinomas
- May cause obstruction and enlargement mimicking acute appendicitis
- Tends to present in older patients than appendicitis

**Mucocele:**
- Dilated appendix filled with mucin
- May stem from obstructed appendix with inspissated mucin or from mucinous cystadenoma/cystadenocarcinoma
- Mucinous cystadenocarcinoma: invasion through appendiceal wall → intraperitoneal seeding
- In women: peritoneal implants may be mistaken for mucinous ovarian tumors
- **Pseudomyxoma peritonei:** abdomen fills with tenacious, semi-solid mucin; disseminated intraperitoneal disease; held in check by repeated debulking but ultimately fatal in most instances

#### Summary: Appendix

- Acute appendicitis is most common in children and adolescents. It is initiated by increased intraluminal pressure consequent to obstruction of the appendiceal lumen.
- The most common tumor of the appendix (carcinoid/well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor) is most often discovered incidentally and is almost always benign.
- Appendiceal adenocarcinoma can be indistinguishable from acute appendicitis clinically; tends to present in older patients.
