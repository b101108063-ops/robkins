# Chapter 21: Bones, Joints, and Soft Tissue Tumors

## Basic Structure and Biology of Bone

### Bone Cells

**Osteoblasts**
- Derived from **mesenchymal stem cells**
- Located on bone surfaces under the periosteum and in the medullary space
- Synthesize **osteoid** (unmineralized bone matrix) including type I collagen
- Regulate mineralization by releasing matrix vesicles that contain enzymes for hydroxyapatite crystal formation
- Derive from mesenchymal stem cells located under the periosteum in developing bone and in the medullary space later in life

**Osteocytes**
- Located within the bone, interconnected by an intricate network of cytoplasmic processes
- Connected through tunnels known as **canaliculi**
- Help control **calcium** and **phosphate** levels
- Detect mechanical forces and translate them into biologic activity — a process called **mechanotransduction**

**Osteoclasts**
- Located on the surface of bone
- Specialized **multinucleated macrophages**, derived from circulating **monocytes**
- Responsible for **bone resorption**
- Attach to bone matrix via cell surface **integrins**, creating a sealed extracellular trench (resorption pit)
- Secrete **acid** and **neutral proteases** (predominantly **matrix metalloproteases [MMPs]**) into the pit to resorb bone

### Development

**Endochondral Ossification**
- During embryogenesis, long bones develop from a cartilage mold (anlagen) by the process of **endochondral ossification**
- A cartilage mold (anlagen) is synthesized by mesenchymal precursor cells
- At approximately 8 weeks' gestation, the central portion of the anlagen is resorbed, creating the **medullary canal**
- At midshaft (diaphysis), osteoblasts begin to deposit the cortex beneath the periosteum, producing the **primary center of ossification** and growing the bone radially
- At each longitudinal end (epiphysis), endochondral ossification proceeds in a centrifugal fashion (secondary center of ossification)
- A plate of cartilage becomes entrapped between the two expanding centers of ossification, forming the **physis** or **growth plate** (Fig. 21.3)
- Chondrocytes within the growth plate undergo sequential proliferation, hypertrophy, and apoptosis
- In the region of apoptosis, the matrix mineralizes and is invaded by capillaries, providing nutrients for osteoblasts that synthesize osteoid
- This process produces **longitudinal bone growth**

**Intramembranous Ossification**
- Responsible for development of **flat bones**
- Bones of the cranium are formed by osteoblasts directly from a fibrous layer of tissue, without cartilage anlagen
- Enlargement of flat bones is achieved by deposition of new bone on a preexisting surface

### Homeostasis and Remodeling

- The adult skeleton appears static but is constantly turning over in a tightly regulated process known as **remodeling**
- Remodeling takes place at a microscopic locus known as the **bone (or basic) multicellular unit (BMU)**
- BMU consists of a unit of coupled osteoblast and osteoclast activity on the bone surface

**Key Signaling Pathways**
- **RANK (Receptor Activator of NF-κB)**: expressed on osteoclast precursors
- **RANKL (RANK Ligand)**: expressed on osteoblasts and marrow stromal cells
- **OPG (Osteoprotegerin)**: secreted "decoy" receptor made by osteoblasts that blocks RANK interaction with RANKL
- RANK signaling activates **NF-κB**, essential for generation and survival of osteoclasts
- **M-CSF (Monocyte-Colony Stimulating Factor)**: produced by osteoblasts
- **WNT proteins**: bind to LRP5 and LRP6 receptors on osteoblasts and trigger production of OPG
- **PTH, IL-1, glucocorticoids**: promote osteoclast differentiation and bone turnover
- **BMPs and sex hormones (estrogen, testosterone)**: generally block osteoclast differentiation or activity by favoring OPG expression

**Peak Bone Mass**
- Achieved in early adulthood after cessation of skeletal growth
- Determined by many factors including polymorphisms in **vitamin D** and **LRP5/6 receptors**, nutrition, and physical activity
- Beginning in the fourth decade, resorption exceeds formation, resulting in a decline in skeletal mass

## Congenital Disorders of Bone and Cartilage

- Congenital abnormalities of the skeleton frequently result from inherited mutations
- **Dysostoses**: result from defects in the formation of mesenchymal condensations and their differentiation into the cartilage anlagen
  - Manifest as absent (aplasia), supernumerary, or abnormally fused bones
  - Examples: syndactyly, craniosynostosis
- **Dysplasias**: arise from mutations in genes that control development or remodeling of the entire skeleton
  - Note: "dysplasia" here implies abnormal growth, not a premalignant lesion as used in neoplasia
- More than 350 skeletal dysostoses and dysplasias have been described
- Various point mutations in a single gene (e.g., **COL2A1**) can result in different phenotypes
- Mutations in diverse genes (e.g., **LRP5, RANKL**) can give rise to similar clinical phenotypes

### Type I Collagen Diseases (Osteogenesis Imperfecta)

**Osteogenesis Imperfecta (OI)**
- Most common inherited disorder of connective tissue
- Caused by deficiencies in synthesis of **type I collagen**
- Principally affects bone and other tissues rich in type I collagen (joints, eyes, ears, skin, and teeth)
- Usually results from **autosomal dominant** mutations in genes encoding the α1 and α2 chains of type I collagen
- Defects cause misfolding of mutated collagen polypeptides and interfere with proper assembly of wild-type collagen chains (dominant negative loss of function)

**Clinical Features:**
- Fundamental abnormality: too little bone, resulting in extreme **skeletal fragility**
- **Blue sclerae**: caused by decreased collagen content, making the sclera translucent
- **Hearing loss**: related to sensorineural deficit and impeded conduction due to abnormalities in bones of the middle ear
- **Dental imperfections**: small, misshapen, blue-yellow teeth secondary to dentin deficiency

**Clinical Subtypes:**
- **Type 2**: uniformly fatal in utero or during perinatal period
- **Type 1**: normal life span despite susceptibility to fractures, particularly during childhood

### Achondroplasia

- Most common **skeletal dysplasia** and a major cause of **dwarfism**
- **Autosomal dominant** disorder resulting from retarded cartilage growth
- Caused by **gain-of-function mutations in FGFR3** (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3)
- Normally, FGF inhibits endochondral growth
- FGFR3 mutation results in constitutively active receptor, exaggerating this effect and suppressing growth
- Approximately 90% of cases stem from **new mutations**, almost all occurring in the paternal allele

**Clinical Features:**
- Shortened proximal extremities
- Trunk of relatively normal length
- Enlarged head with bulging forehead and conspicuous depression of the root of the nose
- Skeletal abnormalities are usually not associated with changes in longevity, intelligence, or reproductive status

### Thanatophoric Dysplasia

- Most common **lethal form of dwarfism**
- Results from diminished proliferation of chondrocytes and disorganization in the zone of proliferation
- Also caused by **gain-of-function mutations in FGFR3**, distinct from those causing achondroplasia
- Occurs in about 1 of every 20,000 live births

**Clinical Features:**
- Micromelic shortening of the limbs
- Frontal bossing
- Relative macrocephaly
- Small chest cavity
- Bell-shaped abdomen
- Underdeveloped thoracic cavity leads to respiratory insufficiency
- Usually fatal at birth or soon thereafter

### Osteopetrosis

- Also known as **marble bone disease**
- Group of rare genetic diseases characterized by:
  - Reduced bone resorption
  - Diffuse symmetric skeletal sclerosis
  - Impaired formation or function of osteoclasts
- Bones are abnormally brittle and fracture easily, like a piece of chalk

**Pathophysiology:**
- Most mutations interfere with acidification of the osteoclast resorption pit (required for dissolution of calcium hydroxyapatite)
- **Autosomal recessive** mutations in **carbonic anhydrase 2 (CA2)**
- Mutations in **TCIRG1 gene** (encodes a component of vacuolar ATPase)

**Clinical Features:**
- Bones involved by osteopetrosis lack a medullary canal
- Ends of long bones are bulbous (**Erlenmeyer flask deformity**) and misshapen
- Neural foramina are small and compress exiting nerves
- Primary spongiosa persists and fills the medullary cavity, leaving no room for hematopoietic marrow

**Variants:**
- **Severe infantile osteopetrosis**: autosomal recessive, often fatal due to leukopenia despite extensive extramedullary hematopoiesis leading to hepatosplenomegaly
- **Mild autosomal dominant form**: may not be detected until adolescence or adulthood, discovered on radiographic studies for repeated fractures; may have mild cranial nerve deficits and anemia

## Metabolic Disorders of Bone

### Osteopenia and Osteoporosis

- **Osteopenia**: decreased bone mass
- **Osteoporosis**: osteopenia severe enough to significantly increase the risk of fracture
- Radiographically, osteoporosis is bone mass at least **2.5 standard deviations** below mean peak bone mass
- Osteopenia is **1 to 2.5 standard deviations** below the mean
- Most osteoporosis is primary; secondary causes include endocrine disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, and drugs

**Senile and Postmenopausal Osteoporosis:**
- Most common forms of osteoporosis
- Peak bone mass influenced by: hereditary factors, physical activity, muscle strength, diet, and hormonal state

**Pathogenesis (Fig. 21.5):**

*Age-Related Changes:*
- Osteoblasts from older individuals have reduced proliferative and biosynthetic potential
- Reduced response to growth factors
- Diminished capacity to make bone
- Known as **senile osteoporosis** (low-turnover osteoporosis)

*Reduced Physical Activity:*
- Decreased physical activity associated with normal aging contributes to senile osteoporosis
- Mechanical forces stimulate normal bone remodeling
- Type of exercise is important — resistance exercises (weight training) are more effective stimuli than repetitive endurance activities

*Genetic Factors:*
- Single gene defects (e.g., **LRP5 mutations**) account for only a small fraction of cases
- Polymorphisms in genes in the **Wnt signaling pathway** have been linked to osteoporosis by genome-wide association studies

*Calcium Nutritional State:*
- Adolescents (particularly girls) tend to have low dietary calcium intake
- Calcium deficiency, increased PTH concentrations, and reduced levels of vitamin D may play a role in senile osteoporosis

*Hormonal Influences:*
- In the decade after menopause, yearly reductions in bone mass may be 2% of cortical bone and 9% of medullary bone
- **Estrogen deficiency** plays the major role
- Approximately 40% of postmenopausal women are affected by osteoporosis
- Decreased estrogen levels increase both bone resorption and formation, but formation does not keep up
- Decreased estrogen may increase secretion of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1) by monocytes
- These cytokines stimulate osteoclast recruitment and activity by:
  - Increasing RANKL levels
  - Diminishing OPG expression
  - Decreasing osteoclast proliferation
  - Preventing osteoclast apoptosis

**Morphology:**
- Hallmark of osteoporosis: histologically normal bone that is decreased in quantity
- Entire skeleton affected in postmenopausal and senile osteoporosis
- In postmenopausal osteoporosis, increased osteoclast activity affects mainly bones with increased surface area, such as the **cancellous compartment of vertebral bodies**
- Trabecular plates become perforated, thinned, and lose their interconnections
- Progressive microfractures and eventual vertebral collapse occur

**Clinical Course:**
- Vertebral fractures frequently occur in thoracic and lumbar regions
- Painful; multiple fractures can cause significant loss of height and deformities (lumbar lordosis, kyphoscoliosis)
- Fractures of femoral neck, pelvis, or spine lead to complications: pulmonary embolism, pneumonia
- These complications account for 40,000 to 50,000 deaths per year
- Osteoporosis cannot be reliably detected on plain radiographs until 30% to 40% of bone mass is lost
- Best estimates of bone loss: **dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry** and **quantitative computed tomography**

**Treatment:**
- Exercise, appropriate calcium and vitamin D intake
- Pharmacologic agents that decrease resorption (**bisphosphonates**)
- **Denosumab**: anti-RANKL antibody that blocks osteoclast activation
- **Bisphosphonates**: reduce osteoclast activity and induce osteoclast apoptosis
- Novel agents undergoing clinical trials to overcome inability to uncouple bone formation and resorption

### Rickets and Osteomalacia

- Both are manifestations of **vitamin D deficiency** or its abnormal metabolism
- **Rickets**: disorder in children, interferes with deposition of bone in growth plates
- **Osteomalacia**: adult counterpart, bone formed during remodeling is undermineralized, resulting in predisposition to fractures
- Fundamental defect: impairment of mineralization and accumulation of unmineralized matrix

### Hyperparathyroidism

- Excess production and activity of **PTH** result in:
  - Increased osteoclast activity
  - Bone resorption
  - Osteopenia

**Pathogenesis:**
- PTH plays a central role in calcium homeostasis through the following effects:
  - Osteoclast activation, increasing bone resorption and calcium mobilization (mediated indirectly by increased RANKL expression on osteoblasts)
  - Increased resorption of calcium by the renal tubules
  - Increased urinary excretion of phosphates
  - Increased synthesis of active vitamin D, **1,25(OH)2-D**, by the kidneys

**Three Primary Skeletal Abnormalities in Primary Hyperparathyroidism:**
- **Osteoporosis**: generalized, most severe in phalanges, vertebrae, and proximal femur
- **Brown tumors**: bone loss predisposes to microfractures and secondary hemorrhages, eliciting influx of macrophages and ingrowth of reparative fibrous tissue
- **Osteitis fibrosa cystica**: increased bone cell activity, peritrabecular fibrosis, and cystic brown tumors
  - Osteoclasts may tunnel into and dissect centrally along the length of the trabeculae, creating "railroad tracks" (**dissecting osteitis**)
  - Marrow spaces are replaced by fibrovascular tissue

**Clinical Course:**
- As bone mass decreases, patients are susceptible to fractures, bone deformation, and joint problems
- Osteitis fibrosa cystica is now rarely encountered because hyperparathyroidism is usually diagnosed on routine blood tests and treated early
- Restoration of PTH levels to normal can completely reverse bone changes

## Paget Disease of Bone (Osteitis Deformans)

- Condition of increased, but disordered and structurally unsound, bone
- Three sequential phases:
  1. **Initial osteolytic stage**
  2. **Mixed osteoclastic-osteoblastic stage** (ends with predominance of osteoblastic activity)
  3. **Final burned-out quiescent osteosclerotic stage**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Evidence suggests both **genetic and environmental causes**
- Approximately 50% of familial Paget disease and 10% of sporadic cases harbor mutations in the **SQSTM1 gene**
- Mutations increase activity of NF-κB, which increases osteoclast activity
- Activating mutations in **RANK** and inactivating mutations in **OPG** account for some cases of juvenile Paget disease
- Geographic distribution suggests environmental influence
- Cell culture studies show infection of osteoclast precursors with measles or other RNA viruses alters vitamin D sensitivity and IL-6 secretion

**Morphology:**
- Hallmark in the sclerotic phase: **mosaic pattern of lamellar bone** (Fig. 21.11)
- Jigsaw puzzle-like appearance produced by unusually prominent cement lines
- In sclerotic phase, bone is thickened but lacks structural stability
- Initial lytic phase: numerous large osteoclasts and resorption pits (may have 100+ nuclei)
- Mixed phase: osteoclasts persist, but many bone surfaces are lined by prominent osteoblasts

**Clinical Course:**
- Monostotic in about 15% of cases; polyostotic in remainder
- Axial skeleton or proximal femur involved in up to 80% of cases
- Most cases are asymptomatic; discovered as incidental radiographic finding
- Pain localized to affected bone (caused by microfractures or bone overgrowth compressing nerve roots)
- Enlargement of craniofacial skeleton may produce **leontiasis ossea** (lion face)
- **Platybasia**: invagination of skull base, compresses posterior fossa
- Weight bearing causes anterior bowing of femurs and tibiae
- Distorts femoral heads, resulting in severe secondary **osteoarthritis**
- **Chalk stick–type fractures**: frequent complication in long bones of lower extremities
- Compression fractures of spine result in spinal cord injury and kyphosis
- Many affected individuals have elevated serum alkaline phosphatase levels but normal serum calcium and phosphorus
- **Secondary osteosarcoma** occurs in less than 1% of all individuals, but 5% to 10% of those with severe polyostotic disease
- Most affected individuals have mild symptoms readily suppressed by treatment with **calcitonin** and **bisphosphonates**

## Fractures

- **Fracture**: loss of bone integrity resulting from mechanical injury and/or diminished bone strength
- Most common pathologic conditions affecting bone

**Types of Fractures:**
- **Simple**: overlying skin is intact
- **Compound**: bone communicates with skin surface
- **Comminuted**: bone is fragmented
- **Displaced**: ends of the bone at the fracture site are not aligned
- **Stress**: slowly developing fracture following increased physical activity with repetitive loads
- **Greenstick**: extending only partially through the bone, common in infants when bones are soft
- **Pathologic**: involving bone weakened by an underlying disease process (e.g., tumor)

### Healing of Fractures

- Involves regulated expression of a multitude of genes
- Overlapping stages with particular molecular, biochemical, histologic, and biomechanical features

**Stages of Fracture Healing:**

*Immediately after fracture:*
- Rupture of blood vessels results in a **hematoma** filling the fracture gap
- Clotted blood provides a fibrin mesh, sealing off the fracture site
- Provides scaffold for influx of inflammatory cells and ingrowth of fibroblasts and new capillaries
- Degranulated platelets and migrating inflammatory cells release **PDGF, TGF-β, FGF**, and other factors
- These activate osteoprogenitor cells and stimulate osteoclastic and osteoblastic activity

*End of first week:*
- Mass of predominantly uncalcified tissue called **soft callus** or **procallus** provides anchorage between fractured bone ends

*After approximately 2 weeks:*
- Soft callus is transformed into a **bony callus**
- Activated osteoprogenitor cells deposit **woven bone**
- Mesenchymal cells may differentiate into chondrocytes making fibrocartilage and hyaline cartilage
- Newly formed cartilage undergoes **endochondral ossification**
- Fractured ends are bridged

*After approximately 3 weeks to months:*
- Callus matures and is subjected to weight-bearing forces
- Portions that are not physically stressed are resorbed
- Remodeling reduces size of callus until shape and outline of fractured bone are reestablished as **lamellar bone**
- Healing process completes with restoration of the medullary cavity

**Factors That Impede Healing:**
- Displaced and comminuted fractures frequently result in some deformity
- Inadequate immobilization permits movement of callus and prevents normal maturation (**delayed union** or **nonunion**)
- If nonunion persists, malformed callus undergoes cystic degeneration; luminal surface may become lined by synovial-like cells, creating a **pseudoarthrosis** (false joint)
- Infection of fracture site (especially common in open fractures) is a serious obstacle to healing
- Malnutrition and skeletal dysplasia also hinder fracture healing

## Osteonecrosis (Avascular Necrosis)

- Refers to **infarction (ischemic necrosis) of bone and marrow cells**
- Diverse conditions predispose to bone ischemia:
  - **Vascular injury** (e.g., trauma, vasculitis)
  - **Drugs** (e.g., corticosteroids)
  - **Systemic disease** (e.g., sickle cell crisis)
  - **Radiation**
- In about 25% of cases, the cause is unknown

**Suspected Mechanisms:**
- Mechanical disruption of vessels
- Thrombotic occlusion
- Extravascular compression

**Clinical Features:**
- Wide age range but peaks in 30s to 50s
- Accounts for about 10% of hip replacements in the United States

**Morphology:**
- **Medullary infarcts**: geographic, involve trabecular bone and marrow; cortex usually not affected because of collateral blood flow
- **Subchondral infarcts**: triangular or wedge-shaped segment of tissue with subchondral bone plate as its base undergoes necrosis
- Overlying articular cartilage remains viable (can access nutrients in synovial fluid)
- Microscopically: dead bone recognized by **empty lacunae** surrounded by necrotic adipocytes that frequently rupture
- In healing response, osteoclasts resorb necrotic trabeculae
- Remaining trabeculae act as scaffolding for deposition of new bone (**creeping substitution**)
- In subchondral infarcts, pace of substitution is too slow to be effective → collapse of necrotic bone and distortion, fracture, or sloughing of articular cartilage

**Clinical Course:**
- Symptoms depend on location and extent of infarct
- Subchondral infarcts cause pain that initially occurs only with activity, then becomes constant
- Subchondral infarcts often collapse and may lead to severe, secondary **osteoarthritis**
- Treatment ranges from conservative measures (limited weight bearing, immobilization) to surgery

## Osteomyelitis

- **Inflammation of bone and marrow**, virtually always secondary to infection
- May be a complication of any systemic infection but frequently manifests as a primary solitary focus of disease
- All types of organisms can produce osteomyelitis, but infections caused by certain **pyogenic bacteria** and **mycobacteria** are most common

### Pyogenic Osteomyelitis

- Almost always caused by bacteria; rarely by fungi
- Organisms may reach bone by:
  1. **Hematogenous spread**
  2. **Extension from a contiguous site**
  3. **Direct implantation** after compound fractures or orthopedic procedures
- In otherwise healthy children, most osteomyelitis is hematogenous and develops in long bones
- In adults, osteomyelitis more often occurs as a complication of open fractures, surgical procedures, and infections of feet in diabetics

**Common Pathogens:**
- **Staphylococcus aureus**: responsible for 80% to 90% of culture-positive pyogenic osteomyelitis cases
  - Expresses cell wall proteins that bind to bone matrix components (e.g., collagen) and facilitate adherence to bone
- **Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella**: more frequently isolated from individuals with genitourinary tract infections or intravenous drug abusers
- **Haemophilus influenzae and group B streptococci**: frequent pathogens in neonatal period
- **Salmonella**: predisposes individuals with sickle cell disease
- In almost 50% of suspected cases, no organisms can be isolated

**Vascular Circulation and Infection Spread:**
- In infants and adults, vascular communication allows spread of infection between metaphysis and epiphysis
- In children, avascular growth plate interrupts infection spread between metaphysis and epiphysis

**Morphology:**

*Acute Phase:*
- Bacteria proliferate and induce a neutrophilic inflammatory reaction
- Necrosis of bone cells and marrow ensues within first 48 hours
- Bacteria and inflammation spread longitudinally and radially through Haversian systems to reach periosteum
- In children, periosteum is loosely attached to cortex → sizable subperiosteal abscess may form
- Lifting of periosteum further impairs blood supply, contributing to necrosis
- Dead bone is known as **sequestrum**
- Rupture of periosteum leads to soft tissue abscess that can channel to skin, creating a draining **sinus**
- In infants, epiphyseal infection may spread through articular surface or along capsular and tendoligamentous insertions into a joint, producing **septic arthritis**

*Chronic Phase (after first week):*
- Chronic inflammatory cells release cytokines that stimulate:
  - Osteoclastic bone resorption
  - Ingrowth of fibrous tissue
  - Deposition of reactive bone at periphery
- Newly deposited bone forms a shell of living tissue known as **involucrum** around the segment of devitalized infected bone
- Histologic findings: marrow fibrosis, sequestrum, inflammatory infiltrate of lymphocytes and plasma cells

**Clinical Course:**

*Acute Osteomyelitis:*
- Sometimes manifests as acute systemic illness with malaise, fever, chills, leukocytosis, and marked throbbing pain over affected region
- In other instances, presentation is subtle (unexplained fever in infants, localized pain in adults)
- Diagnosis strongly suggested by characteristic radiographic findings: lytic focus of bone destruction surrounded by zone of sclerosis
- Biopsy and bone cultures required to identify pathogen
- Combination of **antibiotics** and **surgical drainage** is usually curative

*Chronic Osteomyelitis:*
- In 5% to 25% of cases, acute osteomyelitis fails to resolve and persists as chronic infection
- Develops when there is delay in diagnosis, extensive bone necrosis, inadequate antibiotic therapy or surgical debridement, or weakened host defenses
- Course may be punctuated by acute flare-ups after years of dormancy
- Other complications: pathologic fracture, secondary amyloidosis, endocarditis, sepsis, development of **squamous cell carcinoma** in draining sinus tracts, **sarcoma** in infected bone

### Mycobacterial Osteomyelitis

- Historically a problem in developing countries; increased in developed world due to immigration patterns and immunocompromised patients
- Approximately 1% to 3% of individuals with pulmonary or extrapulmonary tuberculosis exhibit osseous infection
- Organisms are usually blood borne from a focus of active visceral disease
- Direct extension may also occur (e.g., from pulmonary focus into rib, from tracheobronchial nodes into adjacent vertebrae)
- Bone infection may persist for years before being recognized
- Histologic findings: **caseous necrosis** and **granulomas** (typical of tuberculosis)
- More destructive and resistant to control than pyogenic osteomyelitis

**Tuberculous Spondylitis (Pott Disease):**
- Destructive infection of vertebrae
- Spine is involved in 40% of cases of mycobacterial osteomyelitis
- Infection breaks through intervertebral discs to affect multiple vertebrae and extends into soft tissues
- Destruction of discs and vertebrae frequently results in permanent compression fractures producing **scoliosis** or **kyphosis** and neurologic deficits from spinal cord and nerve compression

## Bone Tumors and Tumorlike Lesions

- Rarity of primary bone tumors and often-disfiguring surgery required to treat bone malignancy make this group of disorders especially challenging
- Approximately 2,400 primary bone sarcomas diagnosed annually in the United States
- Therapy aims to optimize survival while maintaining function of affected body parts
- Predilection of specific tumor types for certain age groups and anatomic sites provides important diagnostic clues

**Age Distribution Clues:**
- Bone tumor in first three decades of life: more likely benign
- Bone tumor in older adult: more likely **malignant**

**Clinical Presentation:**
- Benign lesions often asymptomatic incidental findings
- Many tumors produce pain or slow-growing mass
- Sometimes first hint is a **pathologic fracture**
- Radiographic imaging studies have important role in diagnosis
- Biopsy necessary for definitive diagnosis in almost all instances

**Classification:**
- Bone tumors are classified according to the normal cell or matrix they produce
- Lesions without normal tissue counterparts are grouped according to clinicopathologic features
- **Benign tumors** greatly outnumber malignant counterparts
- **Table 21.1** provides classification of selected primary bone tumors

### Bone-Forming Tumors

#### Osteoid Osteoma and Osteoblastoma

- **Benign bone-producing tumors** with similar histologic features but differ clinically and radiographically

**Osteoid Osteoma:**
- By definition, less than 2 cm in diameter
- Most common in young men
- About 50% of cases involve the femur or tibia; typically arise in the cortex
- Usually has thick rim of reactive cortical bone
- Despite small size, presents with **severe nocturnal pain** relieved by **aspirin** and other NSAIDs
- Pain is probably caused by **prostaglandin E2** produced by osteoblasts

**Osteoblastoma:**
- Larger than 2 cm
- Involves posterior components of vertebrae (laminae and pedicles) more frequently
- Pain is unresponsive to aspirin
- Usually does not induce marked bony reaction
- Treatment: **radiofrequency ablation** for osteoid osteoma; **curettage or excision** for osteoblastoma
- Malignant transformation is rare

**Morphology:**
- Round-to-oval masses of hemorrhagic gritty tan tissue
- Well circumscribed
- Composed of randomly interconnecting delicate trabeculae of woven bone prominently rimmed by a single layer of osteoblasts
- Stroma: loose connective tissue with many dilated and congested capillaries
- Relatively small size, well-defined margins, and benign cytologic features distinguish from osteosarcoma
- Osteoid osteomas elicit formation of large amount of reactive bone encircling the lesion

#### Osteosarcoma

- **Malignant tumor** that produces **osteoid matrix** or **mineralized bone**
- Excluding hematopoietic tumors (myeloma and lymphoma), the most common primary malignant tumor of bone
- **Bimodal age distribution**:
  - 75% occur in persons younger than 20 years
  - Smaller second peak in older adults with conditions predisposing to osteosarcoma (Paget disease, bone infarcts, previous radiation)
- Men more commonly affected than women (1.6:1)
- Most common sites in adolescents: **metaphyseal regions of distal femur and proximal tibia**

**Clinical Presentation:**
- Painful, progressively enlarging masses
- Sometimes pathologic fracture is first indication
- Radiographs: large, destructive, mixed lytic and sclerotic mass with infiltrative margins
- Frequently breaks through cortex and lifts periosteum, resulting in reactive subperiosteal bone formation
- **Codman triangle**: triangular shadow between cortex and raised ends of periosteum (indicative of aggressive tumor but not pathognomonic)

**Pathogenesis:**
- Approximately 70% have acquired genetic abnormalities (complex structural and numerical chromosomal aberrations)
- Key tumor suppressors and oncogenes:
  - **RB**: patients with germline RB mutations have 1000-fold increased risk of osteosarcoma; somatic RB mutations present in up to 70% of sporadic tumors
  - **TP53**: patients with **Li-Fraumeni syndrome** (germline TP53 mutations) have greatly elevated incidence of osteosarcoma; abnormalities interfering with p53 function are common in sporadic tumors
  - **CDKN2A**: inactivated in many osteosarcomas; encodes p16 (negative regulator of cyclin-dependent kinases) and p14 (augments p53 function)
  - **MDM2 and CDK4**: overexpressed in many low-grade osteosarcomas; inhibit p53 and RB function, respectively
- Osteosarcomas peak in incidence around adolescent growth spurt
- Occur most frequently in region of growth plate in bones with fastest growth

**Morphology:**
- Bulky, gritty, gray-white tumors often containing areas of hemorrhage and cystic degeneration
- Frequently destroy surrounding cortices and produce soft tissue masses
- Spread extensively in medullary canal, infiltrating and replacing marrow
- Occasionally penetrate epiphyseal plate or enter joint
- **Formation of osteoid matrix or mineralized bone by malignant tumor cells is diagnostic of osteosarcoma**
- Neoplastic bone usually has fine, lacelike configuration but may be deposited in broad sheets or as primitive trabeculae
- Tumor cells vary in size and shape (pleomorphic) with large hyperchromatic nuclei
- Bizarre tumor giant cells, vascular invasion, and necrosis are common
- Mitotic activity is high, including abnormal forms (e.g., tripolar mitoses)

**Clinical Course:**
- Multimodality treatment: neoadjuvant chemotherapy, surgery, chemotherapy
- Amount of chemotherapy-induced necrosis at surgical resection is important prognostic finding
- Spread hematogenously to lungs
- With chemotherapy, 5-year survival rates reach 60% to 70% in patients without detectable metastases at initial diagnosis
- Outcome for patients with metastases, recurrent disease, or secondary osteosarcoma is still poor

### Cartilage-Forming Tumors

- Characterized by formation of **hyaline cartilage**
- Benign cartilaginous tumors are much more common than malignant ones

#### Osteochondroma

- Known clinically as **exostosis**
- **Benign cartilage-capped tumor** attached to underlying skeleton by bony stalk
- About 85% are solitary; remainder seen as part of **multiple hereditary exostoses syndrome**
- Usually first diagnosed in late adolescence and early adulthood (multiple osteochondromas become apparent during childhood)
- Men affected three times more often than women
- Develop in bones of endochondral origin; arise from metaphysis near growth plate of long tubular bones, especially near the knee
- Present as slow-growing masses (painful if impinge on nerve or if stalk fractured)
- Many detected incidentally
- In multiple hereditary exostoses, underlying bones may be bowed and shortened

**Pathogenesis:**
- Hereditary exostoses associated with germline **loss-of-function mutations in EXT1 or EXT2 gene**
- Reduced expression of EXT1 or EXT2 observed in sporadic osteochondromas
- These genes encode enzymes that synthesize **heparan sulfate glycosaminoglycans**
- Reduced or abnormal glycosaminoglycans may prevent normal diffusion of Hedgehog factors, disrupting chondrocyte differentiation and skeletal development

**Morphology:**
- Sessile or pedunculated; range in size from 1 to 20 cm
- Cap composed of benign hyaline cartilage covered peripherally by perichondrium
- Cartilage has appearance of disorganized growth plate; undergoes endochondral ossification
- Cortex of stalk merges with cortex of host bone; continuity between medulla of osteochondroma and host bone

**Clinical Course:**
- Usually stop growing at time of growth plate closure
- Symptomatic tumors cured by simple excision
- Rarely in sporadic cases (but more commonly in multiple hereditary exostoses, 5%–20%) progress to **chondrosarcoma**

#### Chondroma

- **Benign tumors of hyaline cartilage** usually occurring in bones of endochondral origin
- Arise within medullary cavity (**enchondroma**) or on cortical surface (**juxtacortical chondroma**)
- Usually diagnosed in individuals 20 to 50 years of age
- Typically appear as solitary metaphyseal lesions of tubular bones of hands and feet
- Radiographic features: circumscribed lucency with central irregular calcifications, sclerotic rim, intact cortex
- **Ollier disease** and **Maffucci syndrome**: disorders characterized by multiple enchondromas (enchondromatosis); Maffucci syndrome also associated with other rare tumors

**Pathogenesis:**
- Heterozygous **gain-of-function mutations in IDH1 and IDH2 genes** (encoding isocitrate dehydrogenases) identified in chondrocytes of syndromic and solitary enchondromas
- Patients with enchondroma syndromes are mosaics, harboring IDH mutations in only a subset of cells
- Mutations confer new enzymatic activity leading to synthesis of **2-hydroxyglutarate** ("oncometabolite")
- Interferes with regulation of DNA methylation

**Morphology:**
- Usually smaller than 3 cm; gray-blue and translucent
- Composed of well-circumscribed nodules of hyaline cartilage containing benign chondrocytes
- Peripheral portion of nodules may undergo endochondral ossification; center can calcify and infarct

**Clinical Course:**
- Growth potential is limited
- Treatment: observation or curettage
- Solitary chondromas rarely undergo sarcomatous transformation
- Those associated with enchondromatosis do so more frequently
- Individuals with Maffucci syndrome also at risk for other neoplasms (e.g., brain gliomas)

#### Chondrosarcoma

- **Malignant tumors that produce cartilage**
- Subclassified into:
  - **Conventional** (hyaline cartilage-producing): 90% of cases
  - **Dedifferentiated**
  - **Clear cell**
  - **Mesenchymal**

**Epidemiology:**
- About half as common as osteosarcoma
- Usually 40s or older
- Men affected twice as frequently as women
- Clear cell and mesenchymal variants occur in children and young adults
- Commonly arise in axial skeleton: pelvis, shoulder, ribs
- Distal extremities rarely involved
- About 15% are secondary, arising from preexisting enchondroma or osteochondroma

**Pathogenesis:**
- Genetically heterogeneous
- Chondrosarcomas arising in multiple osteochondroma syndrome exhibit **EXT gene mutations**
- Both chondromatosis-related and sporadic chondrosarcomas may have **IDH1 and IDH2 mutations**
- Mutation of **COL2A1 gene** and silencing of **CDKN2A** tumor suppressor gene by DNA methylation are relatively common in sporadic tumors

**Morphology:**
- Large bulky tumors composed of nodules of glistening gray-white, translucent cartilage with gelatinous or myxoid areas
- Spotty calcifications typically present; central necrosis may create cystic spaces
- Tumor spreads through cortex into surrounding muscle or fat
- Histologically, cartilage infiltrates marrow space and entraps normal bony trabeculae
- Graded 1 to 3 based on cellularity, cytologic atypia, and mitotic activity

**Clinical Course:**
- Usually present as painful, progressively enlarging masses
- Tumor grade predicts outcome:
  - Grade 1: 80% 5-year survival; rarely metastasize
  - Grade 3: 43% 5-year survival; 70% metastasize hematogenously (especially to lungs)
- Treatment: wide surgical excision
- Mesenchymal and dedifferentiated tumors also treated with chemotherapy due to more aggressive clinical course

### Tumors of Unknown Origin

#### Ewing Sarcoma

- **Malignant tumor** composed of **primitive round cells** with varying degrees of **neuroectodermal differentiation** and a characteristic molecular signature
- Entities previously classified as primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET) and Askin tumor unified into single category of Ewing sarcoma
- Accounts for approximately 10% of primary malignant bone tumors
- Second most common bone sarcoma in children
- Youngest average age at presentation (80% younger than 20 years)
- Boys affected slightly more frequently than girls; predilection for Caucasians
- Usually arise in **diaphysis of long tubular bones**; 20% are extraskeletal
- Present as painful enlarging masses; site frequently tender, warm, and swollen
- Radiographs: destructive lytic tumor with permeative margins extending into surrounding soft tissues
- Characteristic periosteal reaction produces layers of reactive bone deposited in **onion-skin fashion**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Vast majority (85%) contain **balanced (11;22)(q24;q12) translocation** generating in-frame fusion of **EWSR1 gene** on chromosome 22 to **FLI1 gene** on chromosome 11
- Variant translocations fuse EWSR1 to other members of ETS transcription factor family
- Cell of origin still to be identified; leading candidates: mesenchymal stem cells and primitive neuroectodermal cells

**Morphology:**
- Usually invades cortex, periosteum, and soft tissue
- Soft, tan-white, frequently containing hemorrhage and necrosis
- One of the **small, round blue cell tumors** found in children
- Composed of sheets of uniform small, round cells slightly larger and more cohesive than lymphocytes
- Scant cytoplasm (may appear clear because rich in glycogen)
- **Homer-Wright rosettes** may be present (indicating greater degree of neuroectodermal differentiation)
- Tumor cells do not produce bone or cartilage

**Clinical Course:**
- Treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgical excision with or without radiation
- With chemotherapy: 5-year survival of 75%; long-term cure in 50% of patients

#### Giant Cell Tumor

- So named because **multinucleated osteoclast-type giant cells** dominate histology
- **Locally aggressive neoplasm** almost exclusively affecting adults
- Arise in **epiphyses of long bones**, most commonly distal femur and proximal tibia
- Location near joints frequently causes arthritis-like symptoms
- Occasionally present with pathologic fractures

**Pathogenesis:**
- Neoplastic cells are **osteoblast precursors** (represent only a minority of cells in tumor)
- Neoplastic cells express high levels of **RANKL**, promoting proliferation and differentiation of normal osteoclast precursors into osteoclasts
- Osteoclasts cause localized but highly destructive bone resorption

**Morphology:**
- Often destroy overlying cortex, producing bulging soft tissue mass delineated by thin shell of reactive bone
- Grossly: red-brown masses frequently undergoing cystic degeneration
- Microscopically: conspicuously lacks bone or cartilage
- Numerous osteoclast-type giant cells with 100 or more nuclei with uniform, oval mononuclear tumor cells in between

**Clinical Course:**
- Treated with curettage
- 40% to 60% recur locally
- Up to 4% metastasize to lungs (sometimes spontaneously regress; seldom fatal)
- **Denosumab** (RANKL inhibitor) has shown promise in treatment

#### Aneurysmal Bone Cyst (ABC)

- **Benign tumor** characterized by multiloculated **blood-filled cystic spaces**
- Generally occurs during first 2 decades of life; no sex predilection
- Most frequently develops in metaphysis of long bones and posterior elements of vertebral bodies
- Pain and swelling are common
- Radiographically: usually eccentric, expansile, lytic, metaphyseal lesion with well-defined margins
- CT and MRI may demonstrate internal septa and characteristic **fluid-fluid levels**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Spindle-shaped cells of primary ABC frequently demonstrate rearrangements of chromosome 17p13
- Results in fusion of coding region of **USP6** to regulatory elements of genes highly expressed in osteoblasts → **USP6 overexpression**
- USP6 encodes a deubiquitinase enzyme
- Proposed that increased USP6 expression enhances activity of transcription factor **NF-κB**
- May upregulate matrix metalloproteases, leading to cystic resorption of bone

**Morphology:**
- Multiple blood-filled cystic spaces separated by thin, tan-white septae
- Septae composed of plump uniform fibroblasts, multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells, and reactive woven bone
- Not covered by endothelium

**Clinical Course:**
- Treated surgically; curettage is effective with low risk of recurrence

### Lesions Simulating Primary Neoplasms

#### Nonossifying Fibroma (NOF)

- **Benign, likely reactive**, mesenchymal proliferation
- Present in as many as 50% of children and young adults aged 2–25 years
- Synonymous with **fibrous cortical defect** or **metaphyseal fibrous defect** if localized to cortex or medulla, respectively
- Vast majority arise eccentrically in metaphysis of distal femur and proximal tibia
- Radiographs: sharply demarcated oval radiolucency with long axis parallel to cortex
- Findings sufficiently specific on plain radiography that biopsy is rarely necessary
- NOFs form gray to yellow-brown cellular lesions containing fibroblasts and macrophages
- Fibroblasts frequently arranged in **storiform (pinwheel) pattern**
- Macrophages may form clustered cells with foamy cytoplasm or multinucleated giant cells
- Most small NOFs undergo spontaneous resolution within several years

#### Fibrous Dysplasia

- **Benign tumor** likened to localized developmental arrest of bone constituents
- Lesions arise during skeletal development

**Clinical Patterns:**
- **Monostotic**: involvement of a single bone
- **Polyostotic**: involvement of multiple bones
- **Mazabraud syndrome**: fibrous dysplasia and soft tissue myxoma
- **McCune-Albright syndrome**: polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, café-au-lait skin pigmentations, endocrine abnormalities (especially precocious puberty)

**Pathogenesis:**
- All variants result from somatic **gain-of-function mutation in GNAS1** (gene also mutated in pituitary adenomas)
- Mutations produce constitutively active **Gs-protein** promoting cellular proliferation by increasing cellular levels of **cAMP**
- Phenotype depends on stage of embryogenesis when mutation is acquired and on fate of cell harboring mutation
- Mutation during early embryogenesis → McCune-Albright syndrome
- Mutation during or after skeleton formation in osteoblast precursor → monostotic fibrous dysplasia
- Skeletal manifestations arise from cAMP-mediated interruption of normal osteoblast differentiation

**Morphology:**
- Well circumscribed, intramedullary, varying greatly in size
- Larger lesions expand and distort bone
- Lesional tissue composed of curvilinear trabeculae of woven bone surrounded by moderately cellular fibroblastic proliferation
- Trabeculae **lack prominent osteoblastic rimming**
- Cystic degeneration, hemorrhage, and foamy macrophages are common findings

**Clinical Course:**
- Monostotic fibrous dysplasia often stops enlarging at time of growth plate closure
- Frequently asymptomatic; discovered incidentally
- May cause pain, fracture, and limb length discrepancies
- Symptomatic lesions cured by curettage
- Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia may continue causing problems into adulthood; if involving limb girdles, can cause crippling deformities and fractures

#### Metastatic Tumors

- **Metastatic tumors greatly outnumber primary bone cancers**
- Pathways of tumor spread to bone:
  1. Direct extension
  2. Lymphatic or hematogenous dissemination
  3. Intraspinal seeding (via **Batson plexus of veins**)
- In adults, more than 75% of skeletal metastases originate from cancers of the **prostate, breast, kidney, and lung**
- In children: **neuroblastoma, Wilms tumor, rhabdomyosarcoma**

**Morphology and Pathogenesis:**
- Typically multifocal; involve axial skeleton especially vertebral column
- Radiographic appearance may be purely **lytic** (bone destroying), purely **blastic** (bone forming), or **mixed**
- Bidirectional interactions between metastatic cancer cells and native bone cells account for changes in bone matrix
- Tumor cells secrete substances (prostaglandins, cytokines, PTH-like peptide) that upregulate RANKL on osteoblasts and stromal cells, stimulating osteoclast activity
- Tumor cell growth supported by release of matrix-bound growth factors (TGF-β, IGF-1, FGF) as bone is resorbed
- Tumor cells secreting WNT proteins that stimulate osteoblastic bone formation may produce **sclerotic metastases**

**Clinical Course:**
- Presence of bone metastases carries poor prognosis
- Therapeutic options: systemic chemotherapy, radiation, bisphosphonates
- Surgery may be necessary to stabilize pathologic fractures

## Joints

### Overview

- Joints allow movement while providing mechanical stability
- Classified as **solid (nonsynovial)** and **cavitated (synovial)**

**Solid Joints (Synarthroses):**
- Provide structural integrity; allow only minimal movement
- Lack a joint space
- Grouped according to type of connective tissue bridging bone ends:
  - **Fibrous synarthroses**: cranial sutures, bonds between roots of teeth and jawbones
  - **Cartilaginous synarthroses (synchondroses)**: symphyses between sternum and ribs, bones of pelvis

**Synovial Joints:**
- Have a joint space allowing wide range of motion
- Enclosed by **synovial membranes**
- Lined by:
  - **Type A synoviocytes**: specialized macrophages with phagocytic activity
  - **Type B synoviocytes**: similar to fibroblasts; synthesize hyaluronic acid and various proteins
- Synovial lining **lacks a basement membrane**, allowing efficient exchange of nutrients, wastes, and gases between blood and synovial fluid
- **Synovial fluid**: plasma filtrate containing hyaluronic acid that acts as viscous lubricant and provides nutrition for articular cartilage

**Hyaline Cartilage:**
- Unique connective tissue ideally suited as elastic shock absorber and wear-resistant surface
- Lacks blood supply, lymphatic drainage, and innervation
- Composition:
  - **Water**: 70%
  - **Type II collagen**: 10%
  - **Proteoglycans**: 8%
  - **Chondrocytes**: remainder
- Collagen resists tensile stresses and transmits vertical loads
- Water and proteoglycans resist compression and limit friction
- Chondrocytes synthesize matrix and enzymatically digest it (secrete degradative enzymes in inactive forms and enrich matrix with enzyme inhibitors)

## Arthritis

### Osteoarthritis

- Also called **degenerative joint disease**
- Characterized by **degeneration of cartilage** resulting in structural and functional failure of synovial joints
- **Most common disease of joints**
- Considered an **intrinsic disorder of cartilage** in which chondrocytes respond to biochemical and mechanical stresses, resulting in breakdown of matrix and failure of repair
- Inflammatory mediators are released, perpetuating and worsening damage

**Classification:**
- **Primary (idiopathic) osteoarthritis**: appears insidiously without apparent initiating cause as aging phenomenon; usually oligoarticular
- **Secondary osteoarthritis**: appears in younger individuals with predisposing condition (joint deformity, previous joint injury, underlying systemic disease)

**Prevalence:**
- Increases exponentially beyond age 50
- About 40% of people older than 70 are affected

**Pathogenesis (Fig. 21.32):**
- Lesions stem from degeneration of articular cartilage and its disordered repair
- Cartilage serves as low-friction surface transmitting loads to underlying bone
- Cartilage resists compression through viscoelastic properties of extracellular matrix (type II collagen, proteoglycans, water) secreted by chondrocytes
- Repeated biomechanical stress contributes to development of OA
- **Genetic factors** play a role (genes encoding matrix components and signaling molecules)
- These factors predispose to chondrocyte injury → alteration of extracellular matrix
- Although chondrocytes proliferate and continuously synthesize and secrete proteoglycans, degradation ultimately exceeds synthesis
- MMPs secreted by chondrocytes degrade the type II collagen network
- Cytokines and diffusible factors from chondrocytes and synovial cells implicated in OA:
  - **TGF-β** (induces MMPs)
  - **TNF**
  - **Prostaglandins**
  - **Nitric oxide**
- Chronic, low-level inflammation contributes to progression
- Late stage: chondrocyte loss and severely degraded matrix

**Morphology:**
- **Early stages**: chondrocytes proliferate, forming clusters; water content of matrix increases and proteoglycan concentration decreases; collagen type II fibers are cleaved, yielding fissures and clefts at articular surface (granular soft articular surface)
- **Progression**: full-thickness portions of cartilage are sloughed; dislodged pieces form **loose bodies** (joint mice); exposed subchondral bone plate becomes new articular surface
- **Bone eburnation**: friction burnishes exposed bone giving appearance of polished ivory
- Small fractures through articulating bone are common
- **Fibrous-walled cysts** form via one-way ball valve mechanism
- **Osteophytes** develop at margins of articular surface; capped by fibrocartilage and hyaline cartilage that gradually ossify
- Synovium usually mildly congested and fibrotic with scattered chronic inflammatory cells

**Clinical Course:**
- Primary OA usually presents in patients in their 50s
- If young person has significant OA manifestations, search for underlying cause
- **Characteristic symptoms**: joint pain worsening with use, morning stiffness, crepitus, limitation of range of movement
- Impingement on spinal foramina by osteophytes results in cervical and lumbar nerve root compression with radicular pain, muscle spasms, muscle atrophy, neurologic deficits
- **Commonly involved joints**: hips, knees, lower lumbar and cervical vertebrae, proximal and distal interphalangeal joints of fingers, first carpometacarpal joints, first tarsometatarsal joints
- **Heberden nodes**: prominent osteophytes at distal interphalangeal joints (common in women)
- With time, joint deformity can occur but, unlike rheumatoid arthritis, **joint fusion does not take place**
- Level of disease severity detected radiographically does not correlate well with pain and disability
- No satisfactory means of preventing primary OA; no effective methods of halting progression
- **Therapy**: management of pain, NSAIDs to reduce inflammation, intra-articular corticosteroids, activity modification, arthroplasty for severe cases

### Rheumatoid Arthritis

- **Chronic inflammatory disorder of autoimmune origin**
- Principally attacks joints, producing **nonsuppurative proliferative and inflammatory synovitis**
- Often progresses to destruction of articular cartilage and, in some cases, **ankylosis** (adhesion) of joints
- Extraarticular lesions may occur in skin, heart, blood vessels, lungs
- Prevalence in the United States: approximately 1%
- Three times more common in women than in men
- Peak incidence: third through fifth decades of life

**Pathogenesis (Fig. 21.36):**
- **Genetic predisposition** and **environmental factors** contribute to development, progression, and chronicity
- Pathologic changes mediated by antibodies against self-antigens and inflammation caused by cytokines predominantly secreted by **CD4+ T cells**

*CD4+ T Helper (TH) Cells:*
- Initiate autoimmune response by reacting with arthritogen (perhaps microbial or chemically modified self-antigen)
- T cells produce cytokines stimulating other inflammatory cells:
  - **IFN-γ** from TH1 cells: activates macrophages and synovial cells
  - **IL-17** from TH17 cells: recruits neutrophils and monocytes
  - **RANKL** on activated T cells: stimulates osteoclasts and bone resorption
  - **TNF and IL-1** from macrophages: stimulate resident synovial cells to secrete proteases that destroy hyaline cartilage
- **TNF** most firmly implicated in pathogenesis; TNF antagonists are effective therapies

*Antibodies:*
- Many serum autoantibodies detected are specific for **citrullinated peptides** (arginine residues posttranslationally converted to citrulline)
- **Anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA)**: react with citrullinated fibrinogen, type II collagen, α-enolase, vimentin
- Approximately 30% of RA patients do not have ACPA
- About 80% of patients have serum IgM or IgA autoantibodies that bind to Fc portions of their own IgG (**rheumatoid factor**); may deposit in joints as immune complexes

*Genetics:*
- Approximately 50% of risk of developing RA is related to inherited genetic susceptibility
- **HLA class II locus** associated with ACPA-positive RA
- Epitope on citrullinated protein (vinculin) mimics epitope on many microbes; target of CD4+ T cells when presented by predisposing HLA-DQ alleles
- **PTPN22**: second gene linked to RA; encodes protein tyrosine phosphatase postulated to inhibit T cell activation

*Environmental Factors:*
- At least 70% of RA patients have ACPA in blood
- Insults such as infection (including periodontitis) and smoking may promote citrullination of self-proteins, creating new epitopes that trigger autoimmune reactions

**Morphology:**
- Typically manifests as **symmetric arthritis** principally affecting small joints of hands and feet
- Grossly: synovium becomes edematous, thickened, and hyperplastic; transforms to covered by delicate and bulbous villi
- **Characteristic histologic features**:
  1. Synovial cell hyperplasia and proliferation
  2. Dense inflammatory infiltrates of CD4+ helper T cells, B cells, plasma cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages
  3. Increased vascularity from angiogenesis
  4. Neutrophils and aggregates of organizing fibrin on synovial and joint surfaces
  5. Osteoclastic activity in underlying bone allowing synovium to penetrate into bone, causing periarticular erosions and subchondral cysts
- **Pannus**: mass of edematous synovium, inflammatory cells, granulation tissue, and fibroblasts that grows over articular cartilage and causes its erosion
- In advanced untreated cases, pannus can bridge bones to form **fibrous ankylosis**, which may later ossify as **bony ankylosis**
- **Rheumatoid nodules**: infrequent manifestation; occur in subcutaneous tissue (forearm, elbows, occiput, lumbosacral area)
- Microscopically resemble necrotizing granulomas

**Clinical Course:**
- Distinguished from other forms of polyarticular inflammatory arthritis by presence of ACPA and characteristic radiographic findings
- In about half of patients, RA begins slowly and insidiously with malaise, fatigue, generalized musculoskeletal pain
- After several weeks to months, joints become involved
- Pattern of joint involvement generally symmetrical; hands and feet, wrists, ankles, elbows, and knees most commonly affected
- **Metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints** frequently involved (in contrast to OA)
- Involved joints are swollen, warm, and painful
- Unlike OA, joints are stiff when patient rises in morning or following inactivity
- Progressive joint enlargement and decreased range of motion pursuing chronic waxing and waning course
- Minority of patients (especially those lacking RF and ACPA): disease may stabilize or regress
- Inflammation in tendons, ligaments, and occasionally adjacent skeletal muscle produces characteristic **ulnar deviation of fingers** and **swan-neck deformity**, **boutonnière deformity**
- **Radiographic hallmarks**: joint effusions, juxtaarticular osteopenia with erosions, narrowing of joint space, loss of articular cartilage

**Treatment:**
- Corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (e.g., methotrexate), **TNF antagonists**
- Anti-TNF agents are not curative; patients must be maintained to avoid disease flares
- Long-term treatment with TNF antagonists carries increased risk of infections (e.g., M. tuberculosis)

### Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis

- **Heterogeneous group of disorders** of unknown cause presenting with arthritis before age 16 and persisting for at least 6 weeks
- Differs from adult RA:
  - Oligoarthritis more common
  - Systemic disease more frequent
  - Large joints affected more often than small joints
  - Rheumatoid nodules and rheumatoid factor usually absent
  - ANA seropositivity common
- Pathogenesis unknown but similar to adult RA
- Risk factors include HLA and PTPN22 variants
- Damage appears caused by TH1 and TH17 cells and cytokines produced by these and other inflammatory cells
- Treatment similar to adult RA with some success using IL-6 receptor antibody in systemic form
- Long-term prognosis very variable; approximately 10% develop serious functional disability

### Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies

**Shared Features:**
- **Absence of rheumatoid factor**
- Pathologic changes in **ligamentous attachments** rather than synovium
- Involvement of **sacroiliac joints**, with or without other joints
- **Association with HLA-B27**
- **Bony proliferation** leading to ankylosis (fusion of joints)

- Manifestations are immune mediated; triggered by T cell response presumably directed against undefined antigen (possibly infectious) that may cross-react with antigens expressed on cells of musculoskeletal system

**Ankylosing Spondylitis:**
- Prototypical spondyloarthritis
- Causes destruction of articular cartilage and bony ankylosis, especially of sacroiliac joints
- Becomes symptomatic in second and third decades of life as lower back pain and spinal immobility
- Peripheral joints (hips, knees, shoulders) involved in at least one-third of affected individuals
- Approximately 90% of patients are **HLA-B27 positive**
- Anti-IL-17 antibody has shown some efficacy

**Reactive Arthritis:**
- Defined by triad: **arthritis, nongonococcal urethritis or cervicitis, conjunctivitis**
- Most affected individuals are men in their 20s or 30s
- More than 80% are HLA-B27 positive
- Probably caused by autoimmune reaction initiated by previous infection of genitourinary system (Chlamydia) or gastrointestinal tract (Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter)
- Within several weeks of urethritis or diarrhea, patients experience low back pain
- Ankles, knees, and feet affected most often, frequently in asymmetric pattern
- Severe chronic disease: involvement of spine indistinguishable from ankylosing spondylitis

### Infectious Arthritis

- Joints can become infected from:
  - Hematogenous dissemination
  - Direct inoculation through skin
  - Contiguous spread from soft tissue abscess or osteomyelitis
- Potentially serious; can cause rapid, permanent joint destruction

#### Suppurative Arthritis

- Bacterial infections usually enter joints from distant sites by hematogenous spread
- In neonates: contiguous spread from underlying epiphyseal osteomyelitis may occur
- **H. influenzae**: predominates in children younger than 2 years
- **S. aureus**: main agent in older children and adults
- **Gonococcus**: prevalent during late adolescence and young adulthood
- Individuals with sickle cell disease: prone to Salmonella infection
- Individuals with deficiencies of complement components (C5, C6, C7, C9): susceptible to disseminated gonococcal infections

**Clinical Presentation:**
- Sudden development of acutely painful, warm, swollen joint with restricted range of motion
- Systemic findings: fever, leukocytosis, elevated sedimentation rate
- In 90% of nongonococcal cases, infection involves only a single joint (most commonly knee, followed by hip, shoulder, elbow, wrist, sternoclavicular joints)
- Axial joints more often involved in drug users
- Joint aspiration is diagnostic if purulent fluid is yielded
- Prompt recognition and effective antimicrobial therapy vital to prevent permanent joint destruction

#### Lyme Arthritis

- Leading arthropod-borne disease in the United States (predominantly New England and mid-Atlantic states)
- Caused by infection with spirochete **Borrelia burgdorferi** transmitted by deer ticks of Ixodes ricinus complex
- Progressively involves multiple organ systems through three clinical phases (Fig. 21.40):
  1. **Early localized**: initial infection of skin (erythema migrans rash)
  2. **Early disseminated**: skin, cranial nerves, heart, meninges
  3. **Late disseminated**: arthritis (especially knee), neurologic disease

**Clinical Features:**
- Currently, arthritis occurs in less than 10% of cases because most patients are cured at earlier stage
- If untreated, approximately 60% to 80% of individuals develop **migratory arthritis** (Lyme arthritis) lasting weeks to months
- Spirochetes can only be identified in about 25% of joints with arthritis
- Diagnosis confirmed by serologic testing for anti-Borrelia antibodies
- Treatment: antibiotics with activity against Borrelia; cure rates of 90% achieved with standard regimens
- **Antibiotic-refractory chronic arthritis** can develop in late disseminated stage
- Chronic manifestations include nonspecific symptoms (fatigue, cognitive complaints) collectively known as **posttreatment Lyme disease syndrome (PTLDS)**

**Morphology:**
- Infected synovium exhibits chronic synovitis marked by synoviocyte hyperplasia, fibrin deposition, mononuclear cell infiltrates (especially CD4+ T cells), onion-skin thickening of arterial walls
- In severe cases, morphology can closely resemble RA

### Crystal-Induced Arthritis

- Articular crystal deposits associated with variety of joint disorders
- **Endogenous crystals**: monosodium urate (MSU) (gout), calcium pyrophosphate dehydrate (pseudogout), basic calcium phosphate
- **Exogenous crystals**: silicone, polyethylene, methyl methacrylate used in prosthetic joints
- Crystals produce disease by triggering inflammatory reaction that destroys cartilage

#### Gout

- Marked by transient attacks of **acute arthritis** initiated by **urate crystals** deposited within and around joints
- Whether primary or secondary to underlying disease, common feature is excessive uric acid in tissues and body fluids
- Primary form (90% of cases): major manifestation; cause usually unknown

**Pathogenesis:**

*Hyperuricemia*: plasma urate level above 6.8 mg/dL; necessary but not sufficient for development of gout

*Uric Acid Metabolism:*
- **Synthesis**: uric acid is end product of purine catabolism
  - **De novo pathway**: purine nucleotides synthesized from nonpurine precursors
  - **Salvage pathways**: synthesized from free purine bases obtained through diet or catabolism of purine nucleotides
- **Excretion**: uric acid filtered from circulation by glomerulus; virtually completely resorbed by proximal tubule; small fraction secreted by distal nephron and excreted in urine

*Causes:*
- **Primary gout**: elevated uric acid most commonly results from **reduced excretion** (basis unknown); small minority caused by **uric acid overproduction** from identifiable enzymatic defects (e.g., partial deficiency of **HGPRT**)
- **Secondary gout**: increased production (rapid cell lysis during chemotherapy — tumor lysis syndrome) or decreased excretion (chronic renal disease)

*Inflammation in Gout:*
- Triggered by precipitation of urate crystals in joints
- Stimulates production of cytokines recruiting leukocytes
- Macrophages and neutrophils phagocytose crystals, activating **inflammasome**
- Inflammasome activates **caspase-1**, producing active **IL-1β**
- IL-1β is proinflammatory; promotes accumulation of neutrophils and macrophages in joint
- These cells release other cytokines, free radicals, proteases, arachidonic acid metabolites
- Crystals damage membranes of phagolysosomes, leading to leakage of mediators
- Activation of complement by alternative pathway may contribute to leukocyte recruitment
- Result: **acute arthritis**, which typically remits spontaneously in days to weeks
- Repeated attacks lead eventually to formation of **tophi** (aggregates of urate crystals and inflammatory tissue) in inflamed synovial membranes and periarticular tissue

**Factors Contributing to Symptomatic Gout:**
- Only about 10% of patients with hyperuricemia develop clinical gout
- Age and duration of hyperuricemia: gout usually appears after 20 to 30 years of hyperuricemia
- Genetic predisposition (well-defined X-linked abnormalities of HGPRT; polymorphisms in genes involved with urate or ion transport)
- Alcohol consumption
- Obesity
- Drugs (e.g., thiazides) that reduce urate excretion

**Morphology:**

*Acute Arthritis:*
- Dense inflammatory infiltrate permeating synovium and synovial fluid
- Urate crystals frequently found in cytoplasm of neutrophils; arranged in small clusters in synovium
- Crystals are **long, slender, needle-shaped**; **negatively birefringent**
- Synovium is edematous and congested; contains scattered lymphocytes, plasma cells, and macrophages

*Chronic Tophaceous Arthritis:*
- Evolves from repetitive precipitation of urate crystals during acute attacks
- Crystals encrust articular surface; form visible chalky deposits in synovium
- Synovium becomes hyperplastic, fibrotic, thickened by inflammatory cells
- Forms pannus that destroys underlying cartilage

*Tophi:*
- Pathognomonic of gout
- Present in articular cartilage, ligaments, tendons, bursae
- Large aggregations of urate crystals surrounded by intense foreign body giant cell reaction

*Gouty Nephropathy:*
- Renal complications caused by urate crystals or tophi in renal medullary interstitium or tubules
- Includes uric acid nephrolithiasis and pyelonephritis

**Clinical Course:**
- Associated with male sex, obesity, metabolic syndrome, excess alcohol intake, renal failure, age greater than 30 years

*Four Clinical Stages:*
1. **Asymptomatic hyperuricemia**: appears around puberty in men and after menopause in women
2. **Acute arthritis**: after several years; sudden onset excruciating joint pain, localized hyperemia, warmth
   - Most first attacks are monoarticular; 50% occur in first metatarsophalangeal joint
   - Untreated, may last hours to weeks; complete resolution occurs
3. **Chronic tophaceous gout**: develops on average about 12 years after initial acute attack
   - Radiographs show characteristic juxtaarticular bone erosions and loss of joint space
4. **Asymptomatic intercritical period**: resolution of acute arthritis leads to symptom-free interval
   - Without therapy, attacks recur at decreasing intervals and frequently become polyarticular

**Treatment:**
- Lifestyle modification
- Medication to reduce symptoms (e.g., NSAIDs)
- Medication to lower urate levels (e.g., xanthine oxidase inhibitor)

#### Calcium Pyrophosphate Crystal Deposition Disease (CPPD) (Pseudogout)

- Usually occurs in individuals more than 50 years old; becomes more common with increasing age
- Equally affects both genders and races
- **Types**:
  - **Sporadic (idiopathic)**
  - **Hereditary**: autosomal dominant; caused by germline mutations in pyrophosphate transport channel
  - **Secondary**: associated with previous joint damage, hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, diabetes
- Articular cartilage proteoglycans (normally inhibit mineralization) are degraded, allowing crystallization around chondrocytes
- Inflammation caused by activation of inflammasome in macrophages

**Morphology:**
- Crystals first develop in articular cartilage, menisci, intervertebral discs
- As deposits enlarge, may rupture and seed joint
- Crystals form chalky, white friable deposits
- Histologically seen as oval blue-purple aggregates in hematoxylin-and eosin-stained preparations
- Individual crystals are **rhomboid**, 0.5 to 5 µm; **positively birefringent**
- Inflammation usually milder than in gout

**Clinical Course:**
- Frequently asymptomatic
- May produce acute, subacute, or chronic arthritis (can be confused with OA or RA)
- Joint involvement may last from several days to weeks; may be monoarticular or polyarticular
- **Knees** most commonly affected, followed by wrists, elbows, shoulders, ankles
- Approximately 50% of affected individuals experience significant joint damage
- Therapy is supportive; no known treatment that prevents or slows crystal formation

## Joint Tumors and Tumorlike Conditions

- Reactive tumorlike lesions (ganglions, synovial cysts, osteochondral loose bodies) commonly involve joints and tendon sheaths
- Usually result from trauma or degenerative processes; much more common than neoplasms
- Primary neoplasms are rare; usually benign; tend to recapitulate cells and tissue types native to joints and related structures
- **Synovial sarcoma**: now recognized as sarcoma of uncertain origin (discussed with soft tissue tumors)

### Ganglion and Synovial Cysts

**Ganglion:**
- Small (1–1.5 cm) cyst almost always located near joint capsule or tendon sheath
- Common location: around joints of wrist
- Appears as firm, fluctuant, pea-sized translucent nodule
- Arises from cystic or myxoid degeneration of connective tissue; cyst wall lacks cell lining
- May be multilocular; enlarges through coalescence of adjacent areas of myxoid change
- Fluid similar to synovial fluid; no communication with joint space
- Unrelated to ganglia of nervous system

**Synovial Cyst:**
- Herniation of synovium through joint capsule or massive enlargement of bursa
- Well-recognized example: **Baker cyst** forming in popliteal space in setting of RA or OA
- Synovial lining may be hyperplastic and contain inflammatory cells and fibrin

### Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumor

- **Benign neoplasm** developing in synovial lining of joints, tendon sheaths, bursae
- **Clinical variants**:
  - **Diffuse type** (previously known as pigmented villonodular synovitis)
  - **Localized type**: usually occurs as discrete nodule attached to tendon sheath (commonly in hand)

**Pathogenesis:**
- Both types harbor reciprocal somatic chromosomal translocation **t(1;2)(p13;q37)**
- Results in fusion of **COL6A3** promoter to **M-CSF gene**
- Tumor cells overexpress **M-CSF**, stimulating proliferation of macrophages through autocrine and paracrine effects

**Morphology:**
- **Diffuse tumors**: normally smooth joint synovium converted into tangled mat of red-brown folds, fingerlike projections, and nodules
- **Localized (nodular) tumors**: well circumscribed
- Neoplastic cells are polygonal, moderately sized, resemble synoviocytes
- Both variants heavily infiltrated by macrophages containing hemosiderin and foamy lipid, or multinucleated

**Clinical Course:**
- Diffuse type: presents in knee in 80% of cases
- Patients complain of pain, locking, recurrent swelling (similar to monoarticular arthritis)
- Sometimes palpable mass appreciated
- Localized variant: solitary, slow-growing, painless mass frequently involving hand
- Both types amenable to surgical excision
- Recurrence more common in diffuse form
- Clinical trials investigating M-CSF signaling pathway antagonists for unresectable disease

## Soft Tissue Tumors

- Soft tissue: non-epithelial tissue excluding skeleton, joints, central nervous system, hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues
- **Benign soft tissue tumors** outnumber **sarcomas** by 100-fold
- Incidence of soft tissue sarcomas: approximately 12,000 per year in the United States (less than 1% of all cancers)
- Sarcomas cause 2% of all cancer mortality
- Most soft tissue tumors arise in extremities (especially thigh)
- Approximately 15% arise in children; incidence increases with age

**Pathogenesis:**

*Most sarcomas are sporadic with no known predisposing cause*

*Associated with germline mutations:*
- Neurofibromatosis 1
- Gardner syndrome
- Li-Fraumeni syndrome
- Osler-Weber Rendu syndrome

*Known environmental exposures:*
- Radiation, burns, toxins

**Origin:**
- Best guess: tumors arise from **pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells** acquiring somatic "driver" mutations

**Genomic Classification:**

*Simple karyotype (15%–20%):*
- Usually euploid tumors with single or limited number of chromosomal changes occurring early in tumorigenesis
- Changes are specific enough to serve as diagnostic markers
- Most commonly arise in younger patients
- Tend to have monomorphic appearance microscopically
- Examples: Ewing sarcoma, synovial sarcoma

*Complex karyotype (80%–85%):*
- Usually aneuploid or polyploid; demonstrate multiple chromosomal gains and losses
- Suggests underlying genomic instability
- Examples: leiomyosarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma
- More common in adults; tend to be microscopically pleomorphic

**Clinical Classification:**
- Ranges from benign, self-limited lesions requiring minimal treatment
- To intermediate grade, locally aggressive tumors with minimal metastatic risk
- To highly aggressive malignancies with significant metastatic risk and mortality
- All highly aggressive malignancies classified as sarcomas
- Pathologic classification integrates morphology, immunohistochemistry, molecular diagnostics
- **Grade** (degree of differentiation) and **stage** (size and depth) are important prognostic indicators

### Tumors of Adipose Tissue

#### Lipoma

- **Benign tumor of fat**
- Most common soft tissue tumor in adults
- Conventional lipoma: most common subtype
- Well-encapsulated mass of **mature adipocytes**
- Usually arises in subcutis of proximal extremities and trunk
- Most frequently during middle adulthood
- Occasionally large, intramuscular, poorly circumscribed
- Usually cured by simple excision

#### Liposarcoma

- **Malignant tumor of adipose tissue**
- One of the most common sarcomas of adulthood
- Occurs mainly in people in their 50s to 60s
- Arises in deep soft tissues and retroperitoneum

**Subtypes:**
- **Well-differentiated liposarcoma**: adipocytes with scattered atypical spindle cells; harbors amplification of chromosome region 12q13-q15 (includes MDM2 gene)
- **Myxoid liposarcoma**: abundant basophilic extracellular matrix, arborizing capillaries, primitive cells at various stages of adipocyte differentiation; characterized by **t(12;16) translocation**
- **Pleomorphic liposarcoma**: sheets of anaplastic cells, bizarre nuclei, variable amounts of immature adipocytes (lipoblasts); complex karyotypes

**Clinical Course:**
- All types recur locally and often repeatedly unless adequately excised
- Well-differentiated: relatively indolent
- Myxoid/round cell: intermediate malignant behavior
- Pleomorphic: usually aggressive; frequently metastasizes

### Fibrous Tumors

#### Nodular Fasciitis

- **Self-limited fibroblastic and myofibroblastic proliferation**
- Typically occurs in upper extremities of young adults
- History of trauma present in approximately 25% to 50% of cases
- Tumors grow rapidly during several weeks or months
- Historically considered purely reactive lesion
- Identification of **t(17;22) translocation** producing **MYH9-USP6 fusion gene** indicates it is clonal but self-limited proliferation
- Can spontaneously regress; if excised, rarely recurs
- Malignant transformation virtually nonexistent

**Morphology:**
- Arises in deep dermis, subcutis, fascia, or muscle
- Less than 5 cm; circumscribed or slightly infiltrative
- Highly cellular; contains plump, immature-appearing fibroblasts and myofibroblasts arranged in pattern reminiscent of cultured fibroblasts
- Gradient of maturation (zonation) from cellular, loose, myxoid to organized and fibrous
- Cells vary in size and shape (spindle to stellate); have conspicuous nucleoli
- Mitotic figures abundant
- Lymphocytes and extravasated red blood cells common; neutrophils unusual

#### Fibromatoses

**Superficial Fibromatosis:**
- Infiltrative proliferation that can cause local deformity but has innocuous clinical course
- Affects males more frequently than females
- Characterized by nodular or poorly defined broad fascicles of fibroblasts in long, sweeping fascicles surrounded by abundant dense collagen

*Clinical Subtypes:*
- **Palmar (Dupuytren contracture)**: irregular or nodular thickening of palmar fascia (unilateral or bilateral)
- **Plantar**: common in young patients; unilateral; without contractures
- **Penile (Peyronie disease)**: palpable induration or mass on dorsolateral aspect of penis

- In about 20% to 25% of cases, palmar and plantar fibromatoses stabilize and do not progress (some resolving spontaneously)
- Some recur after excision (particularly plantar variant)

**Deep Fibromatoses (Desmoid Tumors):**
- Large, infiltrative masses; frequently recur but do not metastasize
- Most frequently occur in teenage years to 30s; predominantly in women
- Abdominal fibromatosis: arises in musculoaponeurotic structures of anterior abdominal wall
- Tumors can arise in limb girdles or mesentery
- Contain mutations in **CTNNB1 (β-catenin)** or **APC genes**, leading to increased Wnt signaling
- Most tumors are sporadic
- Individuals with **familial adenomatous polyposis (Gardner syndrome)** with germline APC mutations are predisposed to deep fibromatosis

**Morphology:**
- Gray-white, firm, poorly demarcated masses (1 to 15 cm)
- Rubber, tough; marked infiltration of surrounding muscle, nerve, fat
- Cytologically bland fibroblasts arranged in broad sweeping fascicles amid dense collagen
- Resembles a scar

**Clinical Course:**
- Because of extensively infiltrative nature, complete excision often difficult
- Recent efforts concentrated on medical therapy and radiation as alternatives to surgery

### Skeletal Muscle Tumors

- In contrast to other lineages, skeletal muscle neoplasms are almost all malignant
- **Rhabdomyoma** (benign) more frequent in individuals with tuberous sclerosis

#### Rhabdomyosarcoma

- **Malignant mesenchymal tumor with skeletal muscle differentiation**
- Three main subtypes:
  - **Alveolar** (20%)
  - **Embryonal** (60%)
  - **Pleomorphic** (20%)

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common soft tissue sarcoma of childhood and adolescence (usually before age 20)
- Pleomorphic subtype seen predominantly in adults
- Pediatric forms often arise in sinuses, head and neck, genitourinary tract (locations that do not normally contain much skeletal muscle)

**Pathogenesis:**
- **Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma** frequently contains fusions of **FOXO1 gene** to either **PAX3 or PAX7 gene** (marked by presence of (2;13) or (1;13) translocations)
- PAX3 is transcription factor initiating skeletal muscle differentiation
- Chimeric PAX3-FOXO1 fusion protein interferes with differentiation
- Embryonal and pleomorphic subtypes are genetically heterogeneous

**Morphology:**

*Embryonal Rhabdomyosarcoma:*
- Soft gray infiltrative mass
- Tumor cells mimic skeletal muscle at various stages of differentiation
- Sheets of both primitive round and spindled cells
- **Rhabdomyoblasts** with straplike cytoplasm and visible cross-striations may be present
- **Sarcoma botryoides**: variant developing in walls of hollow viscera (urinary bladder, vagina)

*Alveolar Rhabdomyosarcoma:*
- Network of fibrous septae divide cells into clusters or aggregates (crude resemblance to pulmonary alveoli)
- Tumor cells uniformly round with little cytoplasm; minimally cohesive

*Pleomorphic Rhabdomyosarcoma:*
- Numerous large, sometimes multinucleated, bizarre eosinophilic tumor cells
- Can resemble other pleomorphic sarcomas
- Immunohistochemical identification of muscle-specific proteins (e.g., myogenin) necessary to confirm rhabdomyoblastic differentiation

**Clinical Course:**
- Aggressive neoplasms treated with surgery and chemotherapy with or without radiation
- Botryoid variant of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma has best prognosis
- Pleomorphic subtype often fatal

### Smooth Muscle Tumors

#### Leiomyoma

- **Benign tumor of smooth muscle**
- Most common in uterus but can arise in any soft tissue site
- **Uterine leiomyomas**: common; may cause infertility, menorrhagia
- Also arise from erector pili muscles (pilar leiomyomas) in skin; rarely in deep somatic soft tissues or gastrointestinal tract
- **Germline loss-of-function mutation in fumarate hydratase (FH) gene** on chromosome 1q42.3 leads to multiple cutaneous leiomyomas, uterine leiomyomas, renal cell carcinoma
- Soft tissue leiomyomas usually 1 to 2 cm; composed of fascicles of densely eosinophilic spindle cells intersecting at right angles
- Blunt-ended, elongated nuclei; minimal atypia; few mitotic figures
- Solitary lesions cured with surgery

#### Leiomyosarcoma

- Accounts for 10% to 20% of soft tissue sarcomas
- Occurs in adults; women more frequently than men
- Most develop in deep soft tissues of extremities, retroperitoneum, great vessels
- Have complex genotypes stemming from acquired defects leading to profound genomic instability

**Morphology:**
- Painless firm masses
- Retroperitoneal tumors may be large and bulky; cause abdominal symptoms
- Eosinophilic spindle cells with blunt-ended, hyperchromatic nuclei arranged in interweaving fascicles
- Express smooth muscle proteins (actin, desmin, caldesmon) detectable by immunohistochemistry
- Unlike leiomyomas: mitotic activity and necrosis common

**Clinical Course:**
- Treatment depends on tumor size, location, grade
- Superficial leiomyosarcomas: usually small; good prognosis
- Retroperitoneal leiomyosarcomas: difficult to control; cause death by local extension and metastatic spread (especially to lungs)

### Tumors of Uncertain Origin

#### Synovial Sarcoma

- Accounts for approximately 10% of all soft tissue sarcomas
- Most occur in people in their 20s to 40s
- Patients usually present with deep-seated mass present for several years
- Most synovial sarcomas show characteristic chromosomal translocation **t(x;18)(p11;q11)** producing fusion genes composed of portions of **SS18 gene** and one of three **SSX genes**
- Encode chimeric transcription factors that derail cell cycle control

**Morphology:**
- Microscopically monophasic or biphasic
- **Monophasic**: uniform spindle cells with scant cytoplasm and dense chromatin; grow in short, tightly packed fascicles
- **Biphasic**: contains glandlike structures composed of cuboidal to columnar epithelioid cells in addition to spindle cell component
- Immunohistochemistry helpful; tumor cells (especially in biphasic type) positive for epithelial antigens (e.g., keratins)

**Clinical Course:**
- Treated aggressively with limb-sparing surgery and frequently chemotherapy
- 5-year survival varies from 25% to 62% (related to stage and patient age)
- Common sites of metastases: lung and (unusual for sarcomas) regional lymph nodes

#### Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Sarcoma (UPS)

- Malignant mesenchymal tumors with high-grade, pleomorphic cells that cannot be classified into another category
- Most arise in deep soft tissues of extremity (especially thigh) of middle-aged or older adults
- Diagnosis of **malignant fibrous histiocytoma (MFH)** (sometimes used interchangeably with UPS) has fallen out of use
- Most tumors are aneuploid with multiple structural and numerical chromosomal changes

**Morphology:**
- Usually large, gray-white fleshy masses (10–20 cm)
- Necrosis and hemorrhage common
- Some of the most pleomorphic malignancies encountered
- Sheets of large, anaplastic, spindled to polygonal cells with hyperchromatic irregular, sometimes bizarre nuclei
- Abundant mitotic figures (including atypical nonsymmetric forms) and necrosis
- By definition, tumor cells lack differentiation along recognized lineages or characteristic genetic defects

**Clinical Course:**
- Aggressive malignancies treated with surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiation
- Prognosis generally poor; metastases arise in 30% to 50% of cases

## Summary

### Bone Tumors and Tumorlike Lesions

- Primary bone tumors are classified according to cell of origin or matrix they produce
- Most primary bone tumors are benign
- **Metastases** (especially from lung, prostate, kidneys, breast) are far more common than primary bone neoplasms

**Major Categories:**
- **Bone forming**: Osteoblastoma and osteoid osteoma (benign); Osteosarcoma (aggressive, predominantly in adolescents)
- **Cartilage forming**: Osteochondroma (exostosis with cartilage cap); Chondroma (benign, usually digits); Chondrosarcoma (malignant, axial skeleton in adults)
- **Ewing sarcomas**: aggressive, malignant, small round cell tumors; most often associated with t(11;22)
- **Fibrous dysplasia**: caused by gain-of-function mutations occurring during development

### Arthritis

- **Osteoarthritis (OA, degenerative joint disease)**: most common disease of joints; degenerative process of articular cartilage in which matrix breakdown exceeds synthesis; inflammation minimal and typically secondary
- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)**: chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease affecting mainly small joints; can be systemic; caused by cellular and humoral immune response against self-antigens (particularly citrullinated proteins); TNF plays central role; TNF antagonists are clinically beneficial
- **Seronegative spondyloarthropathies**: heterogeneous group of likely autoimmune arthritides preferentially involving sacroiliac and vertebral joints; associated with HLA-B27
- **Suppurative arthritis**: direct infection of joint space by bacterial organisms
- **Lyme disease**: systemic infection by Borrelia burgdorferi; manifests as infectious arthritis (possibly with autoimmune component in chronic stages)
- **Gout and pseudogout**: inflammatory responses triggered by precipitation of urate or calcium pyrophosphate, respectively

### Soft Tissue Tumors

- Soft tissue neoplasia describes tumors arising from non-epithelial tissues (excluding skeleton, joints, CNS, hematopoietic and lymphoid tissues)
- **Sarcoma**: malignant mesenchymal tumor
- Probably arise from pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells
- Can be classified as:
  - Tumors that recapitulate a mature mesenchymal tissue (e.g., fat); subdivided into benign and malignant forms
  - Tumors composed of cells for which there is no normal counterpart (e.g., synovial sarcoma, UPS)
- Sarcomas with simple karyotypes demonstrate reproducible chromosomal and molecular abnormalities; sufficiently specific to have diagnostic use
- Most adult sarcomas have complex karyotypes; tend to be pleomorphic; genetically heterogeneous; poor prognosis
