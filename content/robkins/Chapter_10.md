## Structure and Function of Blood Vessels

### Vascular Organization

Blood vessels are organized into distinct types based on their size and structure:

**Large Elastic Arteries** (e.g., aorta, arch vessels, iliac and pulmonary arteries)
- Elastic fibers alternate with smooth muscle cells (SMCs) throughout the media
- Expand during systole (storing energy of cardiac contraction)
- Recoil during diastole to propel blood distally
- With age, diabetes, or hypertension: elasticity is lost → "stiff pipes" transmitting high arterial pressures to distal organs
- May become dilated and tortuous (ectatic) and prone to rupture

**Medium-Sized Muscular Arteries** (e.g., coronary and renal arteries)
- Media composed primarily of SMCs
- Elastin limited to internal and external elastic lamina
- Medial SMCs are circularly or spirally arranged around the lumen
- Regional blood flow regulated by SMC contraction (vasoconstriction) and relaxation (vasodilation)
- Controlled by autonomic nervous system and local metabolic factors (e.g., acidosis)
- Endothelial cells (ECs) modulate arterial SMC tone by releasing:
  - **Nitric oxide (NO)** → causes vasodilation
  - **Endothelin** → causes vasoconstriction

**Small Arteries** (2 mm or less) and **Arterioles** (20 to 100 µm diameter)
- Lie within connective tissue of organs
- Media mostly composed of SMCs
- Arterioles are where blood flow resistance is regulated
- As pressures drop during passage through arterioles, velocity of blood flow is sharply reduced
- Flow becomes steady rather than pulsatile
- **Resistance to fluid flow is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the diameter** (halving diameter increases resistance 16-fold)
- Small changes in arteriolar lumen size have profound effects on blood pressure

**Capillaries**
- Lumen diameters slightly smaller than red blood cells (7 to 8 µm)
- Lined by ECs and partially surrounded by SMC-like cells called **pericytes**
- Very large total cross-sectional area with low rate of blood flow
- Thin walls and slow flow → ideally suited for rapid exchange of diffusible substances between blood and tissue
- Capillary network is very rich because diffusion of oxygen and nutrients is not efficient beyond 100 µm
- Metabolically active tissues (e.g., heart) have the highest capillary density

**Veins**
- Receive blood from capillary beds as postcapillary venules
- Postcapillary venules anastomose to form collecting venules and progressively larger veins
- **Vascular leakage (edema) and leukocyte emigration characteristic of inflammation occurs preferentially in postcapillary venules**
- Compared with arteries at same level of branching, veins have:
  - Larger diameters
  - Larger lumina
  - Thinner walls with less distinct layers
- All adaptations to low pressures on venous side of circulation
- More prone to dilation, external compression, and penetration by tumors or inflammatory processes
- Veins with blood flow against gravity (e.g., lower extremities) have valves to prevent backflow
- Venous system has huge capacitance → contains approximately two thirds of the blood

**Lymphatics**
- Thin-walled, endothelium-lined channels that drain lymph from interstitium of tissues
- Lymph contains: water, electrolytes, glucose, fat, proteins, and inflammatory cells
- Eventually reconnect with blood stream via thoracic duct
- Transport interstitial fluid and inflammatory cells from periphery to lymph nodes
- Facilitate antigen presentation and cell activation in nodal tissues
- Enable continuous monitoring of peripheral tissues for infection
- **Double-edged sword**: can also disseminate disease by transporting microbes or tumor cells to distant sites

**Endothelium** is a continuous sheet of cells lining the entire vascular tree that regulates many aspects of blood and blood vessel function.

**Basal and Activated Endothelial Cell States**
- Normal blood pressure, laminar flow, and stable growth factor levels → basal endothelial cell state
- Basal state maintains nonthrombotic surface and appropriate vascular wall smooth muscle tone
- Injury or exposure to certain mediators → endothelial activation
- Activated state: ECs develop adhesive, procoagulant surfaces and release factors leading to smooth muscle contraction and/or proliferation and matrix synthesis

**Inducers of Endothelial Activation**
- Bacterial products
- Inflammatory cytokines
- Hemodynamic stresses
- Lipid products (relevant to atherosclerosis)
- Advanced glycation end products (important in diabetic vascular injury)
- Viruses
- Complement
- Various metabolic insults (e.g., hypoxia)

**Endothelial Activation - Responses**
- Shape changes
- Expression of adhesion molecules
- Production of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors
- Procoagulant and anti-coagulant factors
- Biologically active products

**Time Course of Endothelial Activation**
- **Rapid responses** (within minutes): reversible, independent of new protein synthesis (e.g., endothelial contraction induced by histamine)
- **Slower responses**: involve alterations in gene and protein expression, may take days to develop or abate

**Endothelial Dysfunction**
- Caused by sustained exposure to high levels of stimuli
- Characterized by:
  - Impaired endothelium-dependent vasodilation
  - Hypercoagulable states
  - Increased oxygen free radical production
- Can initiate thrombosis
- Can promote atherosclerosis
- Can contribute to vascular lesions of hypertension and diabetes

**Specialized Endothelial Functions**
- Fenestrations (holes) in ECs lining hepatocyte cords, renal glomeruli, and choroid plexus → facilitate filtration
- In CNS, ECs + astrocytes collaborate to generate impermeable blood-brain barrier
- EC populations from different parts of vasculature have distinct transcriptional programs and behaviors

### Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells

**Smooth Muscle Cell (SMC) Functions**
- Participate in normal vascular repair
- Participate in pathologic processes such as atherosclerosis
- When stimulated, SMCs can:
  - Proliferate
  - Upregulate ECM collagen, elastin, and proteoglycan production
  - Elaborate growth factors and cytokines
- Mediate vasoconstriction or vasodilation in response to physiologic or pharmacologic stimuli

**Regulation of SMC Migratory and Proliferative Activities**

| Factor Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Pro-growth factors** | PDGF, endothelin, thrombin, fibroblast growth factors, IFN-γ, IL-1 |
| **Quiescent state factors** | Heparan sulfate, NO, TGF-α |

## Summary: Vascular Structure and Function

- All vessels are lined by endothelium; although all ECs share certain homeostatic properties, ECs in specific vascular beds have special features that allow for tissue-specific functions (e.g., fenestrated ECs in renal glomeruli).
- The relative SMC and ECM content of vessel walls (e.g., in arteries, veins, and capillaries) varies according to hemodynamic demands (e.g., pressure, pulsatility) and functional requirements.
- EC function is tightly regulated in both the basal and activated states. Various physiologic and pathophysiologic stimuli induce endothelial activation and dysfunction that alter the EC phenotype (e.g., procoagulative versus anti-coagulative, proinflammatory versus anti-inflammatory, nonadhesive versus adhesive).

## Congenital Anomalies

Although rarely symptomatic, unusual anatomic variants of blood vessels can cause complications during surgery, as may occur when a vessel in an unexpected location is injured. Cardiac surgeons and interventional cardiologists also must be familiar with coronary artery variants (up to 1% to 5% of patients).

### Berry Aneurysms
- Thin-walled arterial outpouchings in cerebral vessels
- Classically at branch points around circle of Willis
- Occur where arterial media is congenitally attenuated
- Can spontaneously rupture causing fatal intracerebral hemorrhage (Chapter 23)

### Arteriovenous (AV) Fistulas
- Abnormal connections between arteries and veins without intervening capillary bed
- Most commonly developmental defects
- Can also result from:
  - Rupture of arterial aneurysms into adjacent veins
  - Penetrating injuries that pierce arteries and veins
  - Inflammatory necrosis of adjacent vessels
- Can be created surgically to provide vascular access for hemodialysis
- Extensive AV fistulas can cause high-output cardiac failure by shunting large volumes of blood from arterial to venous circulation

### Fibromuscular Dysplasia
- Focal irregular thickening of walls of medium- and large-sized muscular arteries
- Due to combination of medial and intimal hyperplasia and fibrosis
- Can manifest at any age but occurs most frequently in young women
- Focal wall thickening results in luminal stenosis or vessel spasm → reduced vascular flow
- In renal arteries → can lead to renovascular hypertension
- Between focal segments of thickened wall, artery often exhibits medial attenuation
- Vascular outpouchings can develop in attenuated portions → sometimes rupture

## Blood Pressure Regulation

Systemic and local blood pressure must be maintained within a narrow range to maintain health.
- **Low blood pressure (hypotension)** → inadequate organ perfusion, organ dysfunction, sometimes tissue death
- **High blood pressure (hypertension)** → vessel and end-organ damage; major risk factor for atherosclerosis

**Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Vascular Resistance**

Both cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance are influenced by multiple genetic and environmental factors.

### Cardiac Output Determinants
- **Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate**
- Most important determinant of stroke volume = filling pressure
- Filling pressure regulated through sodium homeostasis and its effect on blood volume
- Heart rate and myocardial contractility (second factor affecting stroke volume) → regulated by α- and β-adrenergic systems

### Peripheral Resistance Regulation
- Predominantly regulated at level of arterioles by neural and humoral inputs
- **Vascular tone reflects balance between:**
  - **Vasoconstrictors**: angiotensin II, catecholamines, endothelin
  - **Vasodilators**: kinins, prostaglandins, NO
- Resistance vessels exhibit autoregulation: increased blood flow induces vasoconstriction to protect tissues against hyperperfusion
- Blood pressure fine-tuned by tissue pH and hypoxia to accommodate local metabolic demands

### Renal Factors in Blood Pressure Regulation

**Kidneys filter approximately 170 liters of plasma containing 23 moles of salt each day**
- With typical diet containing 100 mEq sodium → 99.5% of filtered salt must be reabsorbed to maintain total body sodium levels
- About 98% of filtered sodium reabsorbed by constitutively active transporters
- Remaining 2% recovered via epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) → tightly regulated by aldosterone (downstream effector of renin-angiotensin system)

**Key Regulators Released by Kidneys and Heart:**

| Regulator | Source | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Renin** | Renal juxtaglomerular cells (myoepithelial cells surrounding glomerular afferent arterioles) | Released in response to low blood pressure in afferent arterioles, elevated circulating catecholamines, or low sodium in distal convoluted renal tubules |
| **Angiotensin II** | Produced from angiotensin I by ACE in periphery | (1) Induces vascular SMC contraction; (2) Stimulates aldosterone secretion; (3) Increases tubular sodium resorption |
| **Vasodilators** | Kidney (prostaglandins, NO) | Counterbalance vasopressor effects of angiotensin |
| **Aldosterone** | Adrenal gland | Increases sodium resorption (and water) in distal convoluted and collecting tubules; drives potassium excretion |
| **Natriuretic peptides** | Atrial and ventricular myocardium (released in response to volume expansion) | Inhibit sodium resorption in distal renal tubules → sodium excretion and diuresis; induce systemic vasodilation |

---

## Summary: Blood Pressure Regulation

- Blood pressure is determined by vascular resistance and cardiac output.
- Vascular resistance is regulated at the level of the arterioles, influenced by neural and hormonal inputs.
- Cardiac output is determined by heart rate and stroke volume, the latter of which is strongly influenced by blood volume. Blood volume in turn is regulated mainly by renal sodium excretion or resorption.
- Renin, a major regulator of blood pressure, is secreted by the kidneys in response to decreased blood pressure in afferent arterioles. In turn, renin cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; subsequent peripheral catabolism produces angiotensin II, which regulates blood pressure by increasing vascular SMC tone and by increasing adrenal aldosterone secretion, which consequently increases renal sodium resorption.

## Hypertensive Vascular Disease

**Hypertension** is a major health problem in the developed world.
- Occasionally manifests in acute aggressive form
- Typically asymptomatic for many years → "essential hypertension"
- Gradually age-associated rise in blood pressure was considered "essential" for normal perfusion of endorgans (e.g., brain)
- **Such increases are neither essential nor benign**

**Consequences of Hypertension:**
- Increased risk for stroke
- Increased risk for atherosclerotic coronary heart disease
- Cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure (hypertensive heart disease; Chapter 11)
- Aortic dissection
- Multi-infarct dementia
- Renal failure

### Epidemiology of Hypertension

**Blood pressure is a continuously distributed variable**
- Detrimental consequences increase continuously as pressure rises
- No rigidly defined threshold predictably ensuring total safety

**Diagnostic Thresholds:**
- Sustained diastolic pressures > 90 mm Hg
- Sustained systolic pressures > 140 mm Hg

**Statistics:**
- Over 25% of individuals in general population are hypertensive
- Prevalence increases with age
- Higher in African Americans
- Without appropriate treatment:
  - ~50% of hypertensive patients die of ischemic heart disease (IHD) or congestive heart failure
  - ~1/3 succumb to stroke
- Reduction of blood pressure dramatically reduces incidence and clinical sequelae of all forms of hypertension-related disease

**Malignant Hypertension**
- Small percentage (~5%) of hypertensive patients
- Rapidly rising blood pressure
- If untreated, death within 1 to 2 years
- Usually severe (systolic > 200 mm Hg or diastolic > 120 mm Hg)
- Frequently associated with renal failure and retinal hemorrhages (with or without papilledema)
- Can arise de novo but most commonly superimposed on preexisting benign hypertension

### Pathogenesis of Hypertension

**Primary (Idiopathic) Hypertension** - ~95% of cases
**Secondary Hypertension** - less common, secondary to identifiable underlying condition

**Causes of Secondary Hypertension:**
- Primary renal disease
- Renal artery narrowing (renovascular hypertension)
- Adrenal disorders

**Table 10.2 Types and Causes of Hypertension (Systolic and Diastolic)**

**Essential Hypertension**
- Accounts for 90% to 95% of all cases

**Secondary Hypertension**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Renal** | Acute glomerulonephritis, Chronic renal disease, Polycystic disease, Renal artery stenosis, Renal vasculitis, Renin-producing tumors |
| **Endocrine** | Adrenocortical hyperfunction (Cushing syndrome, primary aldosteronism, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, licorice ingestion), Exogenous hormones (glucocorticoids, estrogen [including pregnancy-induced and oral contraceptives], sympathomimetics and tyramine-containing foods, monoamine oxidase inhibitors), Pheochromocytoma, Acromegaly, Hypothyroidism (myxedema), Hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis), Pregnancy-induced (pre-eclampsia) |
| **Cardiovascular** | Coarctation of the aorta, Polyarteritis nodosa, Increased intravascular volume, Increased cardiac output, Rigidity of the aorta |
| **Neurologic** | Psychogenic, Increased intracranial pressure, Sleep apnea, Acute stress including surgery |

**Single-Gene Disorders Causing Hypertension:**
- Gene defects in enzymes involved in aldosterone metabolism (e.g., aldosterone synthase, 11β-hydroxylase, 17α-hydroxylase) → increased aldosterone secretion, increased salt and water resorption, plasma volume expansion
- Mutations in proteins affecting sodium resorption (e.g., Liddle syndrome - mutations preventing normal degradation of ENaC sodium channel → increased distal tubular resorption of sodium)

### Mechanisms of Essential Hypertension

Although specific triggers are unknown, both altered renal sodium handling and increased vascular resistance contribute.

**Reduced Renal Sodium Excretion**
- In presence of normal arterial pressure → probable key pathogenic feature
- Decreased sodium excretion causes obligatory increase in fluid volume and increased cardiac output → elevated blood pressure
- At new higher blood pressure, kidneys excrete additional sodium
- New steady state of sodium excretion achieved but at expense of elevated blood pressure

**Increased Vascular Resistance**
- May stem from vasoconstriction or structural changes in vessel walls
- Not necessarily independent factors
- Chronic vasoconstriction may result in permanent thickening of affected vessel walls

**Genetic Factors**
- Familial clustering of hypertension
- Studies of monozygotic and dizygotic twins show important role
- In small proportion of essential hypertension cases: linkage to specific angiotensinogen polymorphisms and angiotensin II receptor variants
- Polymorphisms of renin-angiotensin system may contribute to racial differences in blood pressure regulation
- Susceptibility genes in vast majority of cases currently unknown but probably include those influencing:
  - Renal sodium resorption
  - Production of endogenous pressors
  - SMC growth

**Environmental Factors**
- Stress
- Obesity
- Smoking
- Physical inactivity
- High levels of salt consumption
- Modify impact of genetic determinants

### Morphology of Hypertensive Vascular Disease

Hypertension not only accelerates atherogenesis but also causes degenerative changes in walls of large- and medium-sized arteries → can lead to aortic dissection and cerebrovascular hemorrhage.

**Three Forms of Small Blood Vessel Disease Related to Hypertension:**

**1. Hyaline Arteriolosclerosis**
- Associated with benign hypertension
- Marked by homogeneous, pink hyaline thickening of arteriolar walls
- Loss of underlying structural detail
- Luminal narrowing
- Stem from:
  - Leakage of plasma components across injured ECs into vessel walls
  - Increased ECM production by SMCs in response to chronic hemodynamic stress
- In kidneys: arteriolar narrowing leads to diffuse vascular compromise and nephrosclerosis (glomerular scarring)
- More generalized and severe in patients with hypertension
- Same lesions common in diabetic microangiopathy (underlying etiology = hyperglycemia-associated EC dysfunction)

**2. Hyperplastic Arteriolosclerosis**
- More typical of severe hypertension
- Vessels exhibit "onion skin" - concentric, laminated thickening of arteriolar walls
- Luminal narrowing
- Laminations consist of SMCs and thickened, reduplicated basement membrane
- In malignant hypertension: accompanied by fibrinoid deposits and vessel wall necrosis (necrotizing arteriolitis) → particularly prominent in kidney

## Summary: Hypertension

- Hypertension is a common disorder affecting 25% of the population; it is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis, congestive heart failure, and renal failure.
- Hypertension may be primary (idiopathic) or less commonly secondary to an identifiable underlying condition. In close to 95% of cases hypertension is idiopathic or "essential." The remaining cases (secondary hypertension) are due to primary renal disease, renal artery narrowing (renovascular hypertension), or adrenal disorders.
- Essential hypertension represents 95% of cases and is a complex, multifactorial disorder, involving both environmental influences and genetic polymorphisms that may influence sodium resorption, aldosterone pathways, the adrenergic nervous system, and the renin-angiotensin system.
- Hypertension occasionally is caused by single-gene disorders or is secondary to diseases of the renal arteries, kidneys, adrenal glands, or other endocrine organs.

## Vascular Wall Response to Injury

Injury to vessel wall (particularly to ECs) is the fundamental basis for the vast majority of vascular disorders.
- Injurious stimuli may be biochemical, immunologic, or hemodynamic
- Integrated function of ECs and underlying SMCs is critical for vasculature to respond to various stimuli
- Responses can be adaptive or may lead to pathologic lesions
- EC injury/dysfunction contributes to: thrombosis, atherosclerosis, hypertensive vascular lesions
- Ensuing SMC proliferation and matrix synthesis can help repair damaged vessel wall but can eventually lead to luminal occlusion

### Intimal Thickening: A Stereotypical Response to Vascular Injury

**Process:**
1. Vascular injury leads to EC loss or dysfunction
2. Stimulates SMC growth, ECM synthesis, thickening of vascular wall
3. Healing involves migration of SMCs or SMC precursor cells into intima
4. These cells proliferate and synthesize ECM (like fibroblasts filling a wound)
5. Forms **neointima** typically covered by intact EC layer

**Neointimal Response Occurs With:**
- Infection
- Inflammation
- Immune injury
- Physical trauma (e.g., balloon catheter or hypertension)
- Toxic exposure (e.g., oxidized lipids or cigarette smoke)

**Note:** Intimal thickening is a stereotypical response of vessel wall to any insult.

**Neointimal SMC Phenotype:**
- Distinct from medial SMCs
- Not contractile like medial SMCs
- Have capacity to divide
- Considerably greater synthetic capacity than medial SMCs
- At least a subset derived from circulating precursor cells (previously thought to arise exclusively from dedifferentiated medial SMCs)

**Regulation of Intimal SMC Activities:**
- Migratory, proliferative, and synthetic activities regulated by:
  - Growth factors and cytokines from platelets, ECs, macrophages
  - Activated coagulation and complement factors

**With Restoration/Normalization of EC Layer:**
- Intimal SMCs can return to nonproliferative state
- But not before healing response has produced intimal thickening

**With Persistent/Recurrent Insults:**
- Further thickening → stenosis of small- and medium-sized blood vessels (e.g., atherosclerosis)

**Note:** Gradual ongoing intimal thickening appears to be part of "normal aging"
- Age-related intimal change typically of no consequence
- Partly because compensatory outward remodeling of vessel wall results in little net change in luminal diameter

## Arteriosclerosis

**Arteriosclerosis** literally means "hardening of the arteries"
- Generic term reflecting arterial wall thickening and loss of elasticity
- Four distinct types recognized, each with different clinical and pathologic causes and consequences:

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Arteriolosclerosis** | Affects small arteries and arterioles; may cause downstream ischemic injury. Two variants: hyaline and hyperplastic (discussed earlier in relation to hypertension) |
| **Mönckeberg medial sclerosis** | Calcific deposits in muscular arteries, usually centered on internal elastic lamina; typically in individuals >50 years. Lesions do not encroach on vessel lumen; usually not clinically significant |
| **Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia** | Non-atherosclerotic process in muscular arteries larger than arterioles. SMC- and ECM-rich lesion driven by inflammation (healed arteritis or transplant-associated arteriopathy) or mechanical injury (stents or balloon angioplasty). Can cause substantial stenosis. Underlies in-stent restenosis and major long-term limitation of solid organ transplants |
| **Atherosclerosis** | Most frequent and clinically important pattern. Subject of next section. |

## Atherosclerosis

**Atherosclerosis** is characterized by intimal lesions called **atheromas** (or atheromatous or atherosclerotic plaques)
- Impinge on vascular lumen
- Can rupture to cause sudden occlusion
- Underlies pathogenesis of coronary, cerebral, and peripheral vascular disease
- Causes more morbidity and mortality (~half of all deaths) in Western world than any other disorder

**Plaque Structure:**
- Raised lesions composed of:
  - **Soft friable (grumous) lipid cores** (mainly cholesterol and cholesterol esters with necrotic debris)
  - **Fibrous caps** covering the cores

**Clinical Concerns:**
- As they enlarge, plaques may mechanically obstruct vascular lumina → stenosis
- **Greater concern**: plaques are prone to rupture → thrombosis and sudden occlusion
- Intimal lesions may impede perfusion of underlying media
- Media may be weakened by ischemia and changes in ECM caused by subsequent inflammation
- Together, these factors weaken media → setting stage for aneurysm formation

### Epidemiology of Atherosclerosis

**Geographic Distribution:**
- Virtually ubiquitous among most developed nations
- Prevalence increasing at alarming pace in developing countries
- Mortality rate for IHD in United States among highest in world (~5× higher than Japan)
- IHD increasing in Japan (now second leading cause of death)
- Japanese immigrants to U.S. who adopt American lifestyles/dietary customs acquire same atherosclerosis risk as U.S.-born individuals → importance of environmental factors

**Risk Factors Identified in Framingham Heart Study:**

**Table 10.3 Major Risk Factors for Atherosclerosis**

| Category | Factors |
|---|---|
| **Nonmodifiable (Constitutional)** | Genetic abnormalities, Family history, Increasing age, Male gender |
| **Modifiable** | Hyperlipidemia, Hypertension, Cigarette smoking, Diabetes, Inflammation |

**Risk factors have roughly multiplicative effects:**
- Two factors → ~4-fold increase in risk for myocardial infarction
- Three factors (hyperlipidemia, hypertension, smoking) → ~7-fold increase

### Constitutional Risk Factors

**Genetics**
- Family history is most important independent risk factor
- Certain mendelian disorders strongly associated (e.g., familial hypercholesterolemia) - but account for only small percentage of cases
- Most familial risk related to multifactorial traits associated with atherosclerosis: hypertension, diabetes

**Age**
- Atherosclerosis usually remains clinically silent until lesions reach critical threshold in middle age or later
- Incidence of myocardial infarction increases 5-fold between 40 and 60 years of age
- Death rates from IHD continue to rise with each successive decade

**Gender**
- Premenopausal women relatively protected against atherosclerosis compared to age-matched men
- Myocardial infarction and complications uncommon in premenopausal women without other predisposing factors (diabetes, hyperlipidemia, severe hypertension)
- After menopause: incidence of atherosclerosis-related disease increases and can exceed that in men
- Note: Clinical trials show no benefit of hormonal therapy for prevention of vascular disease
- Estrogen replacement after 65 years appears to actually increase cardiovascular risk

### Modifiable Major Risk Factors

**Hyperlipidemia**
- **Hypercholesterolemia** is major risk factor; sufficient to induce lesions in absence of other risk factors
- Main cholesterol component associated with increased risk = **LDL cholesterol ("bad cholesterol")**
- **HDL cholesterol ("good cholesterol")** mobilizes cholesterol from developing and existing vascular plaques → transports to liver for biliary excretion
- Higher HDL correlates with reduced risk

**Dietary Considerations:**
- High dietary intake of cholesterol and saturated fats (egg yolks, animal fats, butter) raises plasma cholesterol
- Diets low in cholesterol and/or with higher ratios of polyunsaturated fats lower plasma cholesterol
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** (abundant in fish oils) are beneficial
- **(Trans)-unsaturated fats** (artificial hydrogenation of polyunsaturated oils - baked goods, margarine) adversely affect cholesterol profiles

**Lifestyle Factors:**
- Exercise and moderate ethanol consumption raise HDL
- Obesity and smoking lower HDL

**Pharmacologic Intervention:**
- **Statins**: widely used drugs that lower circulating cholesterol by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting enzyme in hepatic cholesterol biosynthesis)

**Hypertension**
- Major risk factor for development of atherosclerosis
- Alone can increase risk for IHD by ~60%
- Major cause of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) → can contribute to myocardial ischemia

**Cigarette Smoking**
- Well-established risk factor in men
- Probably accounts for increasing incidence and severity of atherosclerosis in women
- Prolonged smoking (years) of 1+ packs/day doubles IHD-related mortality
- Smoking cessation reduces risk

**Diabetes Mellitus**
- Associated with raised circulating cholesterol levels
- Markedly increases risk for atherosclerosis
- Incidence of myocardial infarction 2× higher in diabetics than non-diabetics
- Associated with increased risk for stroke
- 100-fold increase in atherosclerosis-induced gangrene of lower extremities

### Additional Risk Factors

**~20% of cardiovascular events occur in absence of identifiable risk factors**

**Inflammation**
- Inflammatory cells present during all stages of atheromatous plaque formation
- Intimately linked with plaque progression and rupture
- Systemic pro-inflammatory state associated with development of atherosclerosis
- **C-reactive protein (CRP)** has emerged as one of simplest and most sensitive inflammatory markers
  - Acute-phase reactant synthesized by liver in response to inflammatory cytokines
  - Independently predicts risk for myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, and sudden cardiac death
  - Even among apparently healthy individuals
- Note: Elevated CRP may be marker of underlying asymptomatic atherosclerosis
- No direct evidence that lowering CRP diminishes cardiovascular risk
- CRP reduced by smoking cessation, weight loss, and exercise

**Hyperhomocysteinemia**
- Serum homocysteine levels correlate with coronary atherosclerosis, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, and venous thrombosis
- **Homocystinuria**: rare inborn errors of metabolism → elevated circulating homocysteine (>100 µmol/L) → associated with early-onset vascular disease
- Low folate and vitamin B12 levels can increase homocysteine levels
- Supplemental vitamin ingestion does not affect incidence of cardiovascular disease

**Metabolic Syndrome**
- Associated with central obesity
- Characterized by:
  - Insulin resistance
  - Hypertension
  - Dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides and depressed HDL)
  - Hypercoagulability
  - Pro-inflammatory state (triggered by cytokines from adipocytes)
- Dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, hypertension are cardiac risk factors
- Systemic hypercoagulability and pro-inflammatory state may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and/or thrombosis

**Lipoprotein(a) Levels**
- LDL-like particle containing apolipoprotein B-100 linked to apolipoprotein(a)
- Levels correlated with risk of coronary and cerebrovascular disease, independent of total cholesterol or LDL levels
- Apolipoprotein(a) is homologous to plasminogen → potential link between thrombogenesis and circulating molecules driving atherosclerosis

**Elevated Procoagulants**
- Potent predictors of risk for major cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, stroke)
- Excessive activation of thrombin may be particularly atherogenic
- Thrombin can initiate inflammation through cleavage of protease-activated receptors (PARs) on leukocytes, endothelium, and other cells

**Clonal Hematopoiesis**
- Surprisingly high fraction of older individuals have clonal hematopoiesis
- Defined by presence of major clone of bone marrow cells with somatic driver mutations in oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes
- Patients typically have normal blood counts
- Strongly associated with increased risk of death from cardiovascular disease
- Possibly due to alterations in function of innate immune cells derived from mutated hematopoietic stem cells

**Other Factors**
- Lack of exercise
- Competitive, stressful lifestyle ("type A personality")

### Pathogenesis of Atherosclerosis

**Current View: Response-to-Injury Hypothesis**
- Atherosclerosis as chronic inflammatory response of arterial wall to endothelial injury
- Lesion progression involves interaction of:
  - Modified lipoproteins
  - Monocyte-derived macrophages
  - T lymphocytes
  - Cellular constituents of arterial wall

**Pathogenic Events:**
1. EC injury (and resultant endothelial dysfunction) → increased permeability, leukocyte adhesion, thrombosis
2. Accumulation of lipoproteins (mainly oxidized LDL and cholesterol crystals) in vessel wall
3. Platelet adhesion
4. Monocyte adhesion to endothelium, migration into intima, differentiation into macrophages and foam cells
5. Lipid accumulation within macrophages → release inflammatory cytokines
6. SMC recruitment (from activated platelets, macrophages, and vascular wall cells)
7. SMC proliferation and ECM production

#### Endothelial Injury

EC injury is cornerstone of response-to-injury hypothesis.
- EC loss due to any kind of injury (mechanical denudation, hemodynamic forces, irradiation, chemicals) → intimal thickening
- In presence of high-lipid diets → typical atheromas ensue
- Early immune complex deposition may occur
- Human atherosclerotic lesions begin at sites of intact but dysfunctional endothelium
- Dysfunctional ECs exhibit:
  - Increased permeability
  - Enhanced leukocyte adhesion
  - Altered gene expression

**Suspected Triggers of Early Atheromatous Lesions:**
- Hypertension
- Hyperlipidemia
- Toxins from cigarette smoke
- Homocysteinemia
- Inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF) → stimulate proatherogenic patterns of EC gene expression

**Two Most Important Causes of Endothelial Dysfunction:**
- Hemodynamic disturbances
- Hypercholesterolemia

#### Hemodynamic Disturbances

- Plaques tend to occur at:
  - Ostia of exiting vessels
  - Branch points
  - Posterior wall of abdominal aorta
  - Areas where there is turbulent blood flow
- Nonturbulent laminar flow → induction of endothelial genes whose products protect against atherosclerosis ("atheroprotective" genes)
- Explains nonrandom localization of early atherosclerotic lesions

#### Lipids

**Lipoproteins**
- Lipids transported in bloodstream bound to specific apoproteins (forming lipoprotein complexes)
- Dyslipoproteinemias can result from:
  - Mutations in genes encoding apoproteins or lipoprotein receptors
  - Disorders deranging lipid metabolism (nephrotic syndrome, alcoholism, hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus)

**Common Lipoprotein Abnormalities:**
1. Increased LDL cholesterol levels
2. Decreased HDL cholesterol levels
3. Increased levels of lipoprotein(a)

**Evidence Implicating Hypercholesterolemia in Atherogenesis:**
- Dominant lipids in atheromatous plaques are cholesterol and cholesterol esters
- Genetic defects in lipoprotein uptake/metabolism causing hyperlipoproteinemia → associated with accelerated atherosclerosis
  - Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (defective LDL receptors) → myocardial infarction by age 20
- Other genetic or acquired disorders (diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism) causing hypercholesterolemia → premature atherosclerosis
- Epidemiologic analyses demonstrate significant correlation between plasma cholesterol or LDL levels and severity of atherosclerosis
- Lowering serum cholesterol by diet or drugs:
  - Slows progression of atherosclerosis
  - Causes regression of some plaques
  - Reduces risk for cardiovascular events

**Mechanisms by Which Dyslipidemia Contributes to Atherogenesis:**
- Chronic hypercholesterolemia directly impairs EC function by increasing local oxygen free radical production → oxygen free radicals accelerate NO decay, damping vasodilator activity
- With chronic hypercholesterolemia, lipoproteins accumulate within intima → generate two pathogenic derivatives:
  - **Oxidized LDL**
  - **Cholesterol crystals**
- LDL oxidized through action of oxygen free radicals generated locally by macrophages or ECs
- Oxidized LDL ingested by macrophages through scavenger receptor → foam cell formation
- Oxidized LDL stimulates:
  - Local release of growth factors, cytokines, chemokines
  - Increased monocyte recruitment
  - Cytotoxic to ECs and SMCs
- Extracellular cholesterol crystals in early atherosclerotic lesions serve as "danger" signals → activate innate immune cells (monocytes, macrophages) to produce IL-1 and other pro-inflammatory mediators

#### Inflammation

Inflammation contributes to initiation, progression, and complications of atherosclerotic lesions.

**Process:**
- Normal vessels do not bind inflammatory cells
- Early in atherogenesis, dysfunctional ECs express adhesion molecules → promote leukocyte adhesion (monocytes and T cells)
- Cells migrate into intima under influence of locally produced chemokines

**Monocyte Role:**
- Differentiate into macrophages
- Avidly engulf lipoproteins including oxidized LDL and small cholesterol crystals
- Cholesterol crystals particularly important instigators of inflammation through activation of inflammasome and subsequent release of IL-1
- Activated macrophages produce toxic oxygen species → drive LDL oxidation
- Elaborate growth factors that stimulate SMC proliferation

**T Lymphocyte Role:**
- Recruited to intima → interact with macrophages
- Contribute to chronic inflammation
- Possibly responding to specific antigens (bacterial/viral antigens, heat-shock proteins, modified arterial wall constituents/lipoproteins) or nonspecifically activated by local inflammatory milieu
- Activated T cells in growing intimal lesions elaborate inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ) → stimulate macrophages, ECs, and SMCs

**Consequence of Chronic Inflammatory State:**
- Activated leukocytes and vascular wall cells release growth factors → promote SMC proliferation and matrix synthesis

#### SMC Proliferation and Matrix Synthesis

- Intimal SMC proliferation and ECM deposition convert fatty streak → mature atheroma
- Contributes to progressive growth of atherosclerotic lesions

**Growth Factors Implicated in SMC Proliferation and Matrix Synthesis:**
- **Platelet-derived growth factor** (released by adherent platelets, macrophages, ECs, SMCs)
- **Fibroblast growth factor**
- **TGF-α**

**Effects:**
- Recruited SMCs synthesize ECM (most notably collagen) → stabilizes atherosclerotic plaques
- Activated inflammatory cells in atheromas also can cause:
  - Intimal SMC apoptosis
  - Breakdown of matrix
  - Development of unstable plaques

### Morphology of Atherosclerosis

#### Fatty Streaks

- Begin as minute yellow, flat macules → coalesce into elongated lesions (1 cm or more in length)
- Composed of lipid-filled foamy macrophages
- Only minimally raised; do not cause significant flow disturbance
- Can appear in aortas of infants <1 year of age
- Present in virtually all children >10 years of age (regardless of genetic, clinical, or dietary risk factors)
- **Not all fatty streaks destined to progress to atherosclerotic plaques**
- Coronary fatty streaks form during adolescence at same anatomic sites prone to plaques later in life

#### Atherosclerotic Plaque

**Key Features:**
- Intimal thickening and lipid accumulation
- White to yellow raised lesions
- Range from 0.3 to 1.5 cm in diameter → can coalesce to form larger masses
- Thrombus superimposed on ulcerated plaques imparts red-brown color

**Focal Nature:**
- Atherosclerotic plaques are patchy; usually involving only a portion of arterial wall
- On cross-section, lesions appear "eccentric"
- Focal nature may be related to vagaries of vascular hemodynamics
- Local flow disturbances (turbulence at branch points) make certain parts of vessel wall especially susceptible to plaque formation

**Distribution (Descending Order of Severity):**
1. Infrarenal abdominal aorta
2. Coronary arteries
3. Popliteal arteries
4. Internal carotid arteries
5. Vessels of circle of Willis

**Note:**
- Atherosclerosis typically more severe in abdominal than thoracic aorta
- Vessels of upper extremities usually spared
- Mesenteric and renal arteries spared except at ostia
- Severity in one vascular location does not necessarily predict severity in another
- Any given patient tends to show comparable severity of disease throughout vasculature
- In any given vessel, lesions at various stages of severity often coexist

**Three Principal Components of Atherosclerotic Plaques:**
1. **Cells**: SMCs, macrophages, T cells
2. **ECM**: collagen, elastic fibers, proteoglycans
3. **Lipid**: intracellular and extracellular

**Plaque Architecture:**
- Most commonly have superficial **fibrous cap** composed of SMCs and relatively dense collagen
- **"Shoulder"** (where cap meets vessel wall) is more cellular area containing macrophages, T cells, and SMCs
- **Necrotic core** deep to fibrous cap contains:
  - Lipid (primarily cholesterol and cholesterol esters)
  - Necrotic debris
  - Lipid-laden macrophages and SMCs (foam cells)
  - Fibrin
  - Variably organized thrombus
  - Other plasma proteins
- Extracellular cholesterol frequently takes form of crystalline aggregates → washed out during tissue processing → leaving empty "cholesterol clefts"
- Periphery of lesions shows neovascularization (proliferating small blood vessels)
- Media deep to plaque may be attenuated with fibrosis secondary to smooth muscle atrophy and loss
- Some "fibrous plaques" composed almost exclusively of SMCs and fibrous tissue

**Plaque Progression:**
- Progressively enlarge over time through:
  - Cell death and degeneration
  - Synthesis and degradation of ECM (remodeling)
  - Thrombus organization
- Often undergo calcification

**Plaque Changes Susceptible to Clinically Important Consequences:**

| Change | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Rupture, ulceration, or erosion** of luminal surface | Exposes thrombogenic substances → induces thrombus formation → partial or complete occlusion → tissue ischemia |
| **Hemorrhage into plaque** | Rupture of overlying fibrous cap or neovascularization → rapid plaque expansion or rupture |
| **Atheroembolism** | Ruptured plaque discharges debris → microemboli composed of plaque contents |
| **Aneurysm formation** | Atherosclerosis-induced pressure or ischemic atrophy of media → loss of elastic tissue → structural weakening → aneurysmal dilation and rupture |

### Clinicopathologic Consequences of Atherosclerosis

**Most Commonly Involved Vessels:**
- Large elastic arteries (aorta, carotid, iliac arteries)
- Large- and medium-sized muscular arteries (coronary, renal, popliteal arteries)

**Major Clinical Consequences:**
- Myocardial infarction (heart attack)
- Cerebral infarction (stroke)
- Aortic aneurysm
- Peripheral vascular disease (gangrene of extremities)

### Atherosclerotic Stenosis

**Early Stages:**
- Remodeling of media tends to preserve luminal diameter by increasing overall vessel circumference
- Eventually expanding atheroma impinges on blood flow

**Critical Stenosis:**
- Tipping point at which chronic occlusion limits flow so severely that tissue demand exceeds supply
- In coronary artery (and other) circulations: typically when vessel ~70% occluded
- At rest: adequate cardiac perfusion
- With modest exertion: demand exceeds supply → chest pain (stable angina)

**Consequences of Chronic Arterial Hypoperfusion:**
- Bowel ischemia
- Sudden cardiac death
- Chronic IHD
- Ischemic encephalopathy
- Intermittent claudication (ischemic leg pain)

### Acute Plaque Change

Plaque erosion or rupture typically triggers thrombosis → partial or complete vascular obstruction → often tissue infarction.

**Three Categories of Plaque Changes:**
1. **Rupture/fissuring**: exposes highly thrombogenic plaque constituents
2. **Erosion/ulceration**: exposes thrombogenic subendothelial basement membrane to blood
3. **Hemorrhage into atheroma**: expands plaque volume

**Key Point:**
- Plaques responsible for myocardial infarctions and acute coronary syndromes often asymptomatic before acute event
- Symptoms triggered by thrombosis on lesion that previously did not produce significant luminal occlusion
- Large numbers of asymptomatic individuals at risk for catastrophic coronary event

**Causes of Acute Plaque Change:**
- Complex interplay of intrinsic factors (plaque structure and composition) and extrinsic factors (e.g., blood pressure)
- Factors combine to weaken plaque integrity → unable to withstand vascular shear forces

**Plaques at High Risk for Rupturing ("Vulnerable Plaques"):**
- Large numbers of foam cells and abundant extracellular lipid
- Thin fibrous caps containing few SMCs
- Clusters of inflammatory cells

**Features of Vulnerable vs. Stable Plaques:**

| Feature | Stable Plaque | Vulnerable Plaque |
|---|---|---|
| Fibrous cap | Densely collagenized, thickened | Thin |
| Lipid core | Minimal | Large |
| Inflammation | Negligible | Increased |

**Fibrous Cap Integrity:**
- Undergoes continuous remodeling
- Mechanical strength and stability proportional to collagen content
- Balance of collagen synthesis and degradation affects cap integrity
- Collagen in atherosclerotic plaques synthesized primarily by SMCs
- Loss of SMCs results in cap weakening
- Plaque inflammation increases collagen degradation and reduces collagen synthesis → destabilizes cap

**Plaque Stabilization:**
- Statins may have beneficial effect not only by reducing circulating cholesterol but also by stabilizing plaques through reduction in plaque inflammation

**Extrinsic Factors:**
- Adrenergic stimulation (intense emotions) can increase systemic blood pressure or induce local vasoconstriction → increases mechanical stress on plaque
- **Ciradian periodicity** in onset of heart attacks (peak 6 AM to 12 noon) → adrenergic surge associated with waking and rising → blood pressure spikes and heightened platelet reactivity

**Note:**
- Not all plaque ruptures result in occlusive thromboses with catastrophic consequences
- Silent plaque disruption and ensuing superficial platelet aggregation and thrombosis probably occur frequently and repeatedly
- Healing of these subclinical plaque disruptions (and overlying thromboses) is an important mechanism of atheroma enlargement

## Summary: Atherosclerosis

- Atherosclerosis is an intima-based lesion composed of a fibrous cap and an atheromatous (literally, "gruel-like") core; the constituents of the plaque include SMCs, ECM, inflammatory cells, lipids, and necrotic debris.
- Atherogenesis is driven by an interplay of vessel wall injury and inflammation. The multiple risk factors for atherosclerosis all cause EC dysfunction and influence SMC recruitment and stimulation.
- Major modifiable risk factors for atherosclerosis are hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, cigarette smoking, and diabetes mellitus.
- Atherosclerotic plaques develop and grow slowly over decades. Stable plaques can produce symptoms related to chronic ischemia by narrowing vessels, whereas unstable plaques can cause dramatic and potentially fatal ischemic complications related to acute plaque rupture, thrombosis, or embolization.
- Stable plaques tend to have a dense fibrous cap, minimal lipid accumulation, and little inflammation, whereas "vulnerable" unstable plaques have thin caps, large lipid cores, and relatively dense inflammatory infiltrates.

## Aneurysms and Dissections

**Aneurysms** are congenital or acquired dilations of blood vessels or heart.

**Types:**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **True aneurysm** | Involves all three layers of artery (intima, media, adventitia) or attenuated wall of heart. Includes atherosclerotic and congenital vascular aneurysms, ventricular aneurysms from transmural myocardial infarctions |
| **False aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm)** | Wall defect leads to formation of extravascular hematoma communicating with intravascular space ("pulsating hematoma"). Examples: ventricular ruptures contained by pericardial adhesions, leaks at junction of vascular graft with natural artery |
| **Dissection** | Pressurized blood gains entry to arterial wall through surface defect → pushes apart underlying layers |

**Shape Classification:**

| Shape | Features |
|---|---|
| **Saccular aneurysms** | Discrete outpouchings 5-20 cm in diameter; often with contained thrombus |
| **Fusiform aneurysms** | Circumferential dilations up to 20 cm in diameter; most commonly involve aortic arch, abdominal aorta, or iliac arteries |

**Clinical Significance:**
- Important causes of stasis and subsequent thrombosis
- Propensity to rupture — often with catastrophic results

### Pathogenesis of Aneurysms

Arteries must withstand constant mechanical stress of pulsatile blood flow.
- Aneurysms occur when alterations in SMCs or ECM compromise structural integrity of arterial media

**Implicated Factors:**

| Factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Inadequate/abnormal connective tissue synthesis** | Mutations in TGF-β receptors or downstream signaling pathways → defective elastin and collagen synthesis. In Marfan syndrome: defective fibrillin → increased TGF-β bioavailability → dysregulated signaling and progressive loss of elastic tissue. In Ehlers-Danlos syndrome type IV: defective type III collagen synthesis → aneurysm formation |
| **Excessive connective tissue degradation** | Increased matrix metalloprotease expression by macrophages in atherosclerotic plaque → degrades arterial ECM. Decreased tissue inhibitors of metalloprotease expression → net ECM degradation. Genetic predisposition in setting of inflammation may be related to polymorphisms in these factors |
| **Loss of SMCs or change in SMC synthetic phenotype** | Atherosclerotic thickening of intima → ischemia of inner media (increased diffusion distance). Systemic hypertension → luminal narrowing of aortic vasa vasorum → ischemia of outer media → SMC loss and aortic "degenerative changes": fibrosis (replacing distensible elastic tissue), inadequate ECM synthesis, accumulation of amorphous proteoglycans. Histologically called **cystic medial degeneration** (no true cysts formed) |

**Two Most Important Predisposing Conditions for Aortic Aneurysms:**
1. **Atherosclerosis** — dominant factor in abdominal aortic aneurysms
2. **Hypertension** — associated with ascending aortic aneurysms

**Other Conditions Weakening Vessel Walls:**
- Trauma
- Vasculitis
- Congenital defects
- Infections → "mycotic aneurysms"

**Mycotic Aneurysms May Result From:**
1. Embolization of septic embolus (usually as complication of infective endocarditis)
2. Extension of adjacent suppurative process
3. Direct infection of arterial wall by circulating organisms

**Tertiary Syphilis (Rare Cause of Aortic Aneurysms):**
- Spirochetes have predilection for vasa vasorum of ascending/thoracic aorta
- Subsequent immune response → obliterative endarteritis → compromises blood flow to media
- Ensuing ischemic injury leads to aneurysmal dilation
- Occasionally also involves aortic valve annulus

### Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

**Location:**
- Most commonly occur between renal arteries and aortic bifurcation
- Can be saccular or fusiform
- Up to 15 cm in diameter and 25 cm in length

**Epidemiology:**
- More frequent in men and smokers
- Rarely develop before 50 years of age
- Atherosclerosis is major cause but other factors contribute
- Incidence <5% in men >60 years despite almost universal presence of abdominal aortic atherosclerosis in this population

**Pathogenesis:**
- In majority of cases, results from ECM degradation mediated by proteolytic enzymes released from inflammatory infiltrates in atherosclerotic lesions
- Atherosclerotic plaques also compromise diffusion of nutrients and wastes between vascular lumen and arterial wall
- Deleterious effects on SMCs in media
- Media undergoes degeneration and necrosis → arterial wall thinning

**Familial Predisposition:**
- Independent of genetic predilections to atherosclerosis or hypertension

**Note:**
- Smokers who develop AAA tend to have more severe COPD (particularly emphysema)
- Suggests constitutional predisposition to disorders associated with ECM degradation

### Morphology of AAA

- Extensive atherosclerosis with thinning and focal destruction of underlying media
- Aneurysm sac usually contains bland, laminated, poorly organized mural thrombus (can fill much of dilated segment)
- Often accompanied by smaller iliac artery aneurysms

**Less Common Forms of Aortic Aneurysms:**

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Inflammatory AAAs** | Dense periaortic fibrosis containing abundant lymphoplasmacytic inflammation with many macrophages and giant cells |
| **IgG4-related disease-associated AAAs** | Subset of inflammatory AAAs. Marked by tissue fibrosis with frequent infiltrating IgG4-expressing plasma cells. Also affects pancreas, biliary system, thyroid gland, salivary gland. Patients have aortitis and periaortitis → can weaken wall sufficiently to give rise to aneurysms. Responds well to steroid therapy |
| **Mycotic AAAs** | Circulating microorganisms (e.g., bacteremia from infective endocarditis) seed aneurysm wall or associated thrombus → suppuration accelerates medial destruction → rapid dilation and rupture |

### Clinical Consequences of AAA

- **Obstruction** of vessel branching off aorta (renal, iliac, vertebral, mesenteric arteries) → ischemia of affected organs
- **Embolism** of atheromatous material (cholesterol crystals) or mural thrombus
- **Impingement** on adjacent structures (ureter compression, vertebral erosion)
- **Abdominal mass** (often palpably pulsating) simulating tumor
- **Rupture** into peritoneal cavity or retroperitoneal tissues → massive, often fatal hemorrhage

**Rupture Risk Related to Size:**

| Diameter | Annual Rupture Risk |
|---|---|
| ≤4 cm | Almost never |
| 4-5 cm | ~1% |
| 5-6 cm | ~11% |
| >6 cm | ~25% |

**Treatment:**
- Aneurysms ≥5 cm managed surgically
- Open placement of tubular prosthetic grafts OR endoluminal insertion of stented grafts
- **Mortality**: ~5% for elective procedures vs. ~50% for emergency surgery after rupture

**Note:** Patient with AAA very likely to have atherosclerosis in other vascular beds → significantly increased risk for ischemic heart disease and stroke

### Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm

**Most Commonly Associated With:**
- Hypertension
- Bicuspid aortic valves
- Marfan syndrome
- Less commonly: disorders caused by mutations in TGF-β signaling pathway

**Manifestations:**
- Respiratory or feeding difficulties (airway or esophageal compression from mediastinal encroachment)
- Persistent cough (irritation of recurrent laryngeal nerves)
- Pain from bone erosion (ribs and vertebral bodies)
- Cardiac disease (valvular insufficiency, narrowing of coronary ostia, heart failure from aortic valvular incompetence)
- Aortic dissection or rupture

### Aortic Dissection

**Definition:** Blood splays apart laminar planes of media → forms blood-filled channel within aortic wall
- Can be catastrophic if dissecting blood ruptures through adventitia into adjacent spaces

**Note:**
- Need not be associated with aortic dilation
- Older term "dissecting aneurysm" should be avoided

**Epidemiology:**
- Two main age groups:
  1. Men 40-60 years with antecedent hypertension (>90% of cases)
  2. Younger patients with connective tissue abnormalities affecting aorta (e.g., Marfan syndrome)
- Can be iatrogenic (complicating arterial cannulation during diagnostic catheterization or cardiopulmonary bypass)

**Pregnancy:**
- Rarely associated with aortic or other vessel dissection
- ~10-20 cases per 1 million births
- Typically occurs during or after third trimester
- May be related to hormone-induced vascular remodeling and hemodynamic stresses of perinatal period
- Unusual in presence of substantial atherosclerosis or other causes of medial scarring (medial fibrosis inhibits propagation of dissecting hematoma)

#### Pathogenesis of Aortic Dissection

**Major Risk Factor: Hypertension**
- Aortas in hypertensive patients show narrowing of vasa vasorum with degenerative changes in ECM and variable loss of medial SMCs
- Diminished flow through vasa vasorum contributory

**Other Causes:**
- Abrupt, transient increase in blood pressure (e.g., cocaine abuse)
- Heritable or acquired connective tissue disorders:
  - Marfan syndrome
  - Ehlers-Danlos syndrome type IV
  - Defects in copper metabolism

**Trigger:**
- Intimal tear and subsequent intramural hemorrhage — not known in most cases
- Once tear occurs, blood under systemic pressure dissects through media along laminar planes
- Aggressive pressure-reducing therapy may be effective in limiting evolving dissection
- Rarely: disruption of vasa vasorum can give rise to intramural hematoma without intimal tear

#### Morphology of Aortic Dissection

**Intimal Tear:**
- Usually found in ascending aorta within 10 cm of aortic valve
- Transverse or oblique orientation
- 1-5 cm long with sharp, jagged edges

**Dissection Plane:**
- Can extend retrograde toward heart or distally (occasionally as far as iliac and femoral arteries)
- Usually between middle and outer thirds of media

**Outcomes:**
- External rupture → massive hemorrhage or cardiac tamponade (if into pericardial sac)
- In some cases: dissecting hematoma reenters aorta through second distal intimal tear → "double-barreled aorta" (false channel becomes endothelialized → chronic dissection)

**Histology:**
- Most frequent detectable lesion = **cystic medial degeneration**
- Characterized by:
  - SMC dropout and necrosis
  - Elastic tissue fragmentation
  - Accumulations of amorphous proteoglycan-rich ECM
- Inflammation characteristically absent
- Recognizable medial damage neither prerequisite nor guarantee of dissection

#### Clinical Consequences of Aortic Dissection

**Classification:**

| Type | Description | DeBakey Classification |
|---|---|---|
| **Type A (Proximal)** | Involves ascending aorta, with or without descending aorta | Type I or Type II |
| **Type B (Distal)** | Usually beginning beyond subclavian artery | Type III |

**Classic Symptom:**
- Sudden onset of excruciating tearing or stabbing pain
- Begins in anterior chest → radiates to back between scapulae → moves downward as dissection progresses

**Most Common Cause of Death:**
- Rupture of dissection into pericardial, pleural, or peritoneal cavity

**Other Complications:**
- Retrograde dissection into aortic root → fatal disruption of aortic valvular apparatus or compression of coronary arteries
- Presentations: tamponade, aortic insufficiency, myocardial infarction
- Extension to great arteries of neck, renal, mesenteric, or iliac arteries → obstruction
- Compression of spinal arteries → transverse myelitis

**Treatment and Prognosis:**

| Type | Treatment | Survival |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | Rapid diagnosis + intensive anti-hypertensive therapy + surgical plication of aortic intimal tear | 65-85% saved; ~70% mortality if hemorrhage or distal ischemia symptoms; 40-60% 10-year survival |
| Type B | Conservative management | 75% survival (surgery or anti-hypertensive medication only) |

## Summary: Aneurysms and Dissections

- Aneurysms are congenital or acquired dilations of the heart or blood vessels that involve the entire wall thickness. Complications are related to rupture, thrombosis, and embolization.
- Dissections occur when blood enters the wall of a vessel and separates the various layers. Complications arise as a result of rupture or obstruction of vessels branching off the aorta.
- Aneurysms and dissections result from structural weakness of the vessel wall caused by loss of SMCs or weakening of the ECM, which can be a consequence of ischemia, genetic defects, or defective matrix remodeling.

## Vasculitis

**Definition:** Inflammation of vessel walls

**Two Most Common Pathogenic Mechanisms:**
1. Immune-mediated inflammation
2. Direct vascular invasion by infectious pathogens

**Other Precipitating Factors:**
- Infections can indirectly precipitate immune-mediated vasculitis (generating immune complexes or triggering cross-reactivity)
- Physical and chemical injury (radiation, mechanical trauma, toxins)

**Critical Distinction:**
- Must distinguish between infectious and immunologic mechanisms in each patient
- Immunosuppressive therapy appropriate for immune-mediated vasculitis but could exacerbate infectious vasculitis

**Classification Schemes Based On:**
- Vessel diameter
- Role of immune complexes
- Presence of specific autoantibodies
- Granuloma formation
- Organ specificity
- Population demographics

**~20 primary forms of vasculitis recognized**

**Clinical Manifestations:**
- Largely depend on specific vascular bed affected
- Usually signs and symptoms of systemic inflammation: fever, myalgia, arthralgia, malaise
- Considerable clinical and pathologic overlap among entities

### Noninfectious Vasculitis

**Main Immunologic Mechanisms:**
- Immune complex deposition
- Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs)
- Anti-EC antibodies
- Autoreactive T cells

#### Immune Complex–Associated Vasculitis

- Seen in immunologic disorders associated with autoantibody production (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, Chapter 5)
- Vascular lesions resemble those in experimental immune complex–mediated disorders (Arthus phenomenon, serum sickness)
- May contain identifiable antibody and complement
- Often diagnostic challenge
- Rarely is specific antigen for immune complex formation known
- Often unclear whether antigen-antibody complexes deposited from circulation or formed in situ
- In many suspected cases, antigen-antibody deposits scarce (possibly degraded by time of biopsy)

**Examples of Immune Complex–Associated Vasculitis:**

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Drug hypersensitivity vasculitis** | Drugs (e.g., penicillin) act as haptens by binding to host proteins; other agents are foreign proteins (e.g., streptokinase). Antibodies directed against drug-modified proteins → immune complex formation. Skin lesions most common. Discontinuation of offending agent usually leads to resolution |
| **Vasculitis secondary to infections** | Antibodies to microbial constituents form immune complexes that circulate and deposit in vascular lesions. In up to 30% of polyarteritis nodosa patients, vasculitis due to immune complexes composed of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-HBsAg antibody |

#### Anti-Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies (ANCAs)

**Definition:** Circulating antibodies that react with neutrophil cytoplasmic antigens

**Useful Diagnostic Markers:**
- Titers generally mirror clinical severity
- Rise in titers after periods of quiescence predictive of disease recurrence

**Two Most Important ANCAs (Classified by Antigen Specificity):**

| Type | Former Name | Antigen | Association |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PR3-ANCA** | c-ANCA | Proteinase-3 (PR3) — neutrophil azurophilic granule constituent; shares homology with numerous microbial peptides | Granulomatosis with polyangiitis |
| **MPO-ANCA** | p-ANCA | Myeloperoxidase (MPO) — lysosomal granule constituent involved in oxygen free radical generation | Microscopic polyangiitis, Churg-Strauss syndrome. Induced by therapeutic agents (especially propylthiouracil) |

**Mechanism of ANCA-Associated Vasculitis:**
- ANCAs directed against cellular constituents; do not form circulating immune complexes
- Vascular lesions typically do not contain demonstrable antibody and complement
- ANCA-associated vasculitides often described as "pauci-immune"

**Pathogenic Sequence:**
1. Drugs or cross-reactive microbial antigens induce ANCA formation; alternatively, leukocyte surface expression or release of PR3 and MPO (with infection) incites ANCA development in susceptible individual
2. Inflammatory stimuli elicit release of cytokines (e.g., TNF) → upregulate surface expression of PR3 and MPO on neutrophils and other cell types
3. ANCAs bind to cytokine-activated cells → further neutrophil activation
4. ANCA-activated neutrophils cause EC injury by releasing granule contents and elaborating reactive oxygen species

**Note:** ANCAs directed against proteins other than PR3 and MPO sometimes seen in patients with non-vasculitic inflammatory disorders (inflammatory bowel disease, sclerosing cholangitis, rheumatoid arthritis)

#### Anti-Endothelial Cell Antibodies and Autoreactive T Cells

- Antibodies to ECs underlie certain vasculitides (e.g., Kawasaki disease)
- Autoreactive T cells cause injury in forms of vasculitis characterized by granuloma formation

#### Giant Cell (Temporal) Arteritis

**Definition:** Chronic inflammatory disorder with granulomatous inflammation affecting large-to small-sized arteries in the head

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common form of vasculitis among older adults in developed countries
- Temporal arteries typically biopsied for diagnosis
- Vertebral and ophthalmic arteries, and aorta (giant cell aortitis) also commonly involved

**Clinical Urgency:**
- Ophthalmic artery vasculitis can lead to sudden and permanent blindness
- Must be promptly diagnosed and treated

##### Pathogenesis
- T-cell–mediated immune response to as-yet uncharacterized vessel wall antigen
- Pro-inflammatory cytokines (especially TNF) and anti-EC antibodies also contribute
- Granulomatous inflammation, association with certain MHC class II haplotypes, excellent therapeutic response to steroids → support immune etiology
- Predilection for vessels of head remains unexplained

##### Clinical Features
- Rare before 50 years of age
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, fatigue, weight loss
- Facial pain or headache (most intense along course of superficial temporal artery; painful to palpation)
- Ocular symptoms (ophthalmic artery involvement) in ~50% of patients: diplopia to complete vision loss

**Diagnosis:**
- Depends on biopsy and histology
- Vascular inflammation is patchy → negative biopsy does not exclude diagnosis

**Treatment:** Corticosteroid or anti-TNF therapies

##### Morphology
- Pathologic changes notoriously patchy along affected vessels
- Involved arterial segments exhibit nodular intimal thickening (occasionally thromboses) → reduce vessel diameter → distal ischemia
- Majority of lesions: granulomatous inflammation within inner media centered on internal elastic membrane
- Infiltrate of lymphocytes and macrophages with multinucleate giant cells
- Fragmentation of internal elastic lamina
- Up to 25% of cases: granulomas and giant cells absent; only nonspecific panarteritis with mixed infiltrate of acute and chronic inflammation
- Healing: intimal thickening, medial thinning and scarring, adventitial fibrosis
- Characteristically, lesions at different stages of development seen within same artery

#### Takayasu Arteritis

**Definition:** Granulomatous vasculitis of medium- and large-sized arteries characterized by ocular disturbances and marked weakening of pulses in upper extremities ("pulseless disease")

**Features:**
- Transmural scarring and thickening of aorta (particularly aortic arch and great vessels)
- Severe luminal narrowing of major branch vessels
- Shares clinical and histologic features with giant cell aortitis
- Distinction largely based on patient age:
  - >50 years → giant cell aortitis
  - <50 years → Takayasu aortitis
- Historically associated with Japanese ethnicity and certain HLA haplotypes
- Now has global distribution
- Autoimmune etiology likely

##### Morphology
- Classically affects aortic arch and arch vessels
- 1/3 of cases also involve remainder of aorta and branches
- Occasionally aortic root involvement → dilation and aortic valve insufficiency
- Pulmonary arteries involved in 50% of patients
- Renal and coronary arteries also can be affected
- Takeoffs of great vessels can be markedly narrowed and obliterated

**Histologic Spectrum:**
- Adventitial mononuclear infiltrates and perivascular cuffing of vasa vasorum
- Intense transmural mononuclear inflammation
- Granulomatous inflammation with giant cells and patchy medial necrosis
- Irregular thickening of vessel wall, intimal hyperplasia, adventitial fibrosis

##### Clinical Features

**Initial (Systemic) Phase:**
- Fatigue, weight loss, fever

**Vascular Phase (Progression):**
- Reduced upper-extremity blood pressure and pulse strength
- Neurologic deficits
- Ocular disturbances: visual field defects, retinal hemorrhages, total blindness
- Distal aorta disease → leg claudication
- Pulmonary artery involvement → pulmonary hypertension
- Narrowing of coronary ostia → myocardial infarction
- Renal artery involvement → systemic hypertension (~50% of patients)

**Course:** Variable — some rapidly progress, others become quiescent after 1-2 years. Long-term survival possible with quiescent disease, albeit with visual or neurologic deficits.

#### Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN)

**Definition:** Systemic vasculitis of small- or medium-sized muscular arteries
- Typically involves renal and visceral vessels
- Spares pulmonary circulation
- No association with ANCAs
- 1/3 of patients have chronic hepatitis B infection → immune complexes containing hepatitis B antigens deposit in affected vessels
- Cause unknown in remaining cases

##### Morphology
- Segmental transmural necrotizing inflammation of small- to medium-sized arteries
- Often with superimposed thrombosis
- Kidney, heart, liver, GI tract vessels affected (descending order of frequency)
- Lesions involve only part of vessel circumference; have predilection for branch points
- Impaired perfusion → ulcerations, infarcts, ischemic atrophy, hemorrhages
- Inflammatory process weakens arterial wall → aneurysms and rupture

**Acute Phase:**
- Transmural mixed inflammatory infiltrate (neutrophils and mononuclear cells)
- Fibrinoid necrosis and luminal thrombosis

**Older Lesions:**
- Fibrous thickening of vessel wall extending into adventitia
- All stages of activity (early to late) coexist in different vessels or even same vessel → ongoing and recurrent pathogenic insults

##### Clinical Features
- Primarily disease of young adults but can occur in all ages
- Episodic course with long symptom-free intervals
- Systemic findings: malaise, fever, weight loss (nonspecific)
- "Classic" presentation:
  - Rapidly accelerating hypertension (renal artery involvement)
  - Abdominal pain and bloody stools (GI lesions)
  - Diffuse muscular aches and pains
  - Peripheral neuritis (predominantly motor nerves)
- Renal involvement prominent; major cause of death
- **Untreated: typically fatal**
- **With immunosuppression: 5-year survival ~80%**
- Relapse in up to 25% (more often in non-HBV-associated cases)
- HBV-associated cases have better long-term prognosis

#### Kawasaki Disease

**Definition:** Acute, febrile, usually self-limited illness of infancy and childhood associated with arteritis of mainly large-to medium-sized vessels

**Epidemiology:**
- ~80% of patients <4 years of age
- Originally described in Japan; now recognized worldwide

**Clinical Significance:**
- Involvement of coronary arteries
- Coronary arteritis → aneurysms that rupture or thrombose → myocardial infarction

**Pathogenesis:**
- In genetically susceptible individuals, various infectious agents (mostly viral) trigger disease
- Vasculitis may result from delayed-type hypersensitivity response directed against cross-reactive or newly uncovered vascular antigen(s)
- Subsequent cytokine production and polyclonal B cell activation → auto-antibodies to ECs and SMCs → precipitate vasculitis

##### Clinical Features

**Presentation:**
- Conjunctival and oral erythema and blistering
- Edema of hands and feet
- Erythema of palms and soles
- Desquamative rash
- Cervical lymph node enlargement ("mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome")

**Cardiovascular Sequelae (20% of untreated patients):**
- Asymptomatic coronary arteritis
- Coronary artery ectasia
- Large coronary artery aneurysms (7-8 mm diameter) → associated with rupture, thrombosis, myocardial infarction, sudden death

**Treatment:**
- Intravenous immunoglobulin infusions + aspirin
- Given together → markedly decrease incidence of symptomatic coronary artery disease

#### Microscopic Polyangiitis

**Definition:** Necrotizing vasculitis affecting capillaries, small arterioles, and venules
- Also called hypersensitivity vasculitis or leukocytoclastic vasculitis
- All lesions tend to be same age in any given patient (unlike PAN)

**Involved Sites:**
- Skin, mucous membranes, lungs, brain, heart, GI tract, kidneys, muscle
- Necrotizing glomerulonephritis (90% of patients) and pulmonary capillaritis particularly common

**Association:**
- Can be feature of: Henoch-Schönlein purpura, essential mixed cryoglobulinemia, vasculitis associated with connective tissue disorders
- Antibody responses to antigens (drugs, microorganisms, heterologous proteins, tumor proteins) → immune complex deposition or trigger secondary immune responses (e.g., ANCAs)
- Most cases associated with MPO-ANCA
- Neutrophil recruitment and activation within vascular beds probably responsible for disease manifestations

##### Clinical Features
- Depending on vascular bed involved:
  - Hemoptysis, hematuria, proteinuria
  - Abdominal pain or bleeding
  - Muscle pain or weakness
  - Palpable cutaneous purpura
- With exception of widespread renal or CNS involvement: immunosuppression and removal of offending agent induce durable remissions

##### Morphology
- Segmental fibrinoid necrosis of media with focal transmural necrotizing lesions
- Granulomatous inflammation absent
- Resembles PAN but spares medium- and large-sized arteries → macroscopic infarcts uncommon
- In some areas (typically postcapillary venules): only infiltrating neutrophils undergoing fragmentation → "leukocytoclastic vasculitis"
- Most lesions "pauci-immune" (little or no antibody demonstrable)

#### Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA)

**Former Name:** Wegener granulomatosis

**Definition:** Necrotizing vasculitis characterized by triad of:
1. Necrotizing granulomas of upper-respiratory tract (ear, nose, sinuses, throat) or lower-respiratory tract (lung) or both
2. Necrotizing or granulomatous vasculitis affecting small- to medium-sized vessels (capillaries, venules, arterioles, arteries) — most prominently lungs and upper airways
3. Focal necrotizing, often crescentic, glomerulonephritis

**Variants:**
- "Limited" forms: restricted to respiratory tract
- Widespread disease: affects eyes, skin, other organs (notably heart); clinically resembles PAN with respiratory involvement

**Pathogenesis:**
- Likely initiated as cell-mediated hypersensitivity response to inhaled infectious or environmental antigens
- PR3-ANCAs present in almost 95% of cases → probably drive tissue injury
- ANCA level useful marker of disease activity; titers fall with effective immunosuppressive therapy and rise prior to relapse

##### Morphology

**Upper Respiratory Tract:**
- Granulomatous sinusitis to ulcerative lesions of nose, palate, or pharynx

**Lung:**
- Diffuse parenchymal infiltrates to granulomatous nodules
- Multifocal necrotizing granulomatous vasculitis with surrounding fibroblastic proliferation
- Multiple granulomata can coalesce → radiographically visible nodules with central cavitation
- Vessel destruction → hemorrhage and hemoptysis
- Progressive fibrosis and organization

**Kidney:**
- Mild: focal glomerular necrosis with thrombosis of isolated glomerular capillary loops (focal and segmental necrotizing glomerulonephritis)
- Advanced: diffuse necrosis with parietal cell proliferation forming epithelial crescents (crescentic glomerulonephritis)

##### Clinical Features
- Typical patient: middle-aged man (but women and other ages can be affected)

**Classic Presentations:**
- Bilateral pneumonitis with nodules and cavitary lesions (95%)
- Chronic sinusitis (90%)
- Mucosal ulcerations of nasopharynx (75%)
- Renal disease (80%)

**Other Features:**
- Rash, myalgias, articular involvement, neuritis, fever
- Mild renal involvement: hematuria and proteinuria
- Severe disease: rapidly progressive renal failure

**Course:**
- **Untreated: 80% mortality at 1 year**
- Treatment: steroids, cyclophosphamide, TNF inhibitors, rituximab → considerably improved
- Most patients survive but remain at high risk for relapses → may lead to renal failure

#### Churg-Strauss Syndrome

**Also Called:** Allergic granulomatosis and angiitis

**Definition:** Small-vessel necrotizing vasculitis associated with:
- Asthma
- Allergic rhinitis
- Lung infiltrates
- Peripheral eosinophilia
- Extravascular necrotizing granulomas
- Striking infiltration of vessels and perivascular tissues by eosinophils

**Epidemiology:** Rare — affects 1 in 1 million individuals

**Organ Involvement:**
- Cutaneous involvement (palpable purpura)
- Gastrointestinal bleeding
- Renal disease (primarily focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis)
- Cardiac involvement in 60% → major cause of morbidity and death (myocardial eosinophilic infiltrates → cardiomyopathy)

**Pathogenesis:**
- May stem from "hyperresponsiveness" to normally innocuous allergic stimulus
- MPO-ANCAs present in minority of cases → disorder pathogenically heterogeneous
- Vascular lesions differ from PAN or microscopic polyangiitis by presence of granulomas and eosinophils

#### Thromboangiitis Obliterans (Buerger Disease)

**Definition:** Segmental, thrombosing, acute and chronic inflammation of medium- and small-sized arteries (principally tibial and radial arteries)
- Occasional secondary extension into veins and nerves of extremities

**Epidemiology:**
- Occurs almost exclusively in heavy tobacco smokers
- Usually develops before 35 years of age

**Suspected Mechanisms:**
- Direct EC toxicity caused by some component of tobacco
- Reactive compound in tobacco may modify vessel wall components and induce immune response
- Most patients hypersensitive to tobacco extracts
- Genetic predilection suggested by increased prevalence in certain ethnic groups (Israeli, Indian subcontinent, Japanese) and association with certain HLA haplotypes

##### Morphology
- Sharply segmental acute and chronic transmural vasculitis of medium- and small-sized arteries (predominantly extremities)
- Early stages: mixed inflammatory infiltrates with luminal thrombosis
- Small microabscesses (occasionally rimmed by granulomatous inflammation)
- Inflammation extends into contiguous veins and nerves (rare in other forms of vasculitis)
- Over time: thrombi organize and recanalize; artery and adjacent structures become encased in fibrous tissue

##### Clinical Features

**Early Manifestations:**
- Cold-induced Raynaud phenomenon
- Instep foot pain induced by exercise (instep claudication)
- Superficial nodular phlebitis (venous inflammation)

**Progression:**
- Vascular insufficiency accompanied by severe pain (even at rest) — from neural involvement
- Chronic extremity ulcerations → may progress to frank gangrene

**Smoking:**
- Abstinence in early stages often ameliorates further attacks
- Once established, vascular lesions do not respond to smoking abstinence

#### Vasculitis Associated with Other Noninfectious Disorders

- May resemble hypersensitivity angiitis or classic PAN
- Associated with: malignancies, immunologic disorders (rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome, Henoch-Schönlein purpura)
- Rheumatoid vasculitis: occurs in patients with severe, long-standing rheumatoid arthritis; can cause clinically significant aortitis but more typically affects small- and medium-sized arteries → visceral infarction

**Therapeutic Implications:**
- Classic immune complex lupus vasculitis vs. anti-phospholipid antibody syndrome share morphologic features
- Former requires anti-inflammatory therapy
- Latter requires anticoagulation

### Infectious Vasculitis

**Definition:** Localized arteritis caused by direct invasion of arteries by infectious agents (usually bacteria or fungi — particularly Aspergillus and Mucor spp.)

**Modes of Vascular Invasion:**
- Part of nearby local tissue infection (bacterial pneumonia, adjacent abscess)
- Less commonly: hematogenous spread of bacteria or embolization from infective endocarditis

**Consequences:**
- May weaken arterial walls → mycotic aneurysms
- May induce thrombosis and infarction
- Inflammation of vessels in bacterial meningitis → thrombosis and infarction → extension of subarachnoid infection into brain parenchyma

---

## Summary: Vasculitis

- Vasculitis is defined as inflammation of vessel walls; it frequently is associated with systemic manifestations (including fever, malaise, myalgias, and arthralgias) and organ dysfunction that depends on the pattern of vascular involvement.
- Vasculitis can result from infections but more commonly has an immunologic basis such as immune complex deposition, anti-neutrophil antibodies (ANCAs), or anti-EC antibodies.
- Different forms of vasculitis tend to specifically affect vessels of a particular caliber and location.

## Disorders of Blood Vessel Hyperreactivity

Several disorders characterized by inappropriate or exaggerated vasoconstriction of blood vessels.

### Raynaud Phenomenon

**Definition:** Vasoconstriction of arteries and arterioles in extremities (particularly fingers and toes; sometimes nose, earlobes, lips)
- Restricted blood flow induces paroxysmal pallor or cyanosis
- Digits show "red-white-and-blue" color changes (proximal vasodilation, central vasoconstriction, more distal cyanosis)

**Classification:**
- Primary entity
- Secondary to other disorders

#### Primary Raynaud Phenomenon
(Formerly called Raynaud disease)

**Features:**
- Exaggerated central and local vasomotor responses to cold or emotion
- Affects 3-5% of general population
- Predilection for young women
- Structural changes in arterial walls absent (except late course when intimal thickening may appear)
- Usually benign course
- Chronic cases: atrophy of skin, subcutaneous tissues, and muscles
- Ulceration and ischemic gangrene rare

#### Secondary Raynaud Phenomenon

**Causes:**
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Scleroderma
- Buerger disease
- Atherosclerosis
- Other arterial diseases

**Clinical Importance:**
- May be first manifestation of such conditions
- Every patient with Raynaud phenomenon should be evaluated for secondary causes

### Myocardial Vessel Vasospasm

**Also Called:** Cardiac Raynaud

**Consequences:**
- Subset of cases leads to Prinzmetal angina (angina due to vasospasm)
- If spasm sufficient duration (20-30 minutes) → myocardial infarction
- Elevated catechol levels increase heart rate and myocardial contractility → exacerbate ischemia caused by vasospasm

**Outcomes:**
- Sudden cardiac death (fatal arrhythmia)
- Ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy ("Takotsubo cardiomyopathy" or "broken heart syndrome" — associated with emotional duress)

**Histologic Findings:**
- **Acute cases:** microscopic areas of necrosis characterized by myocyte hypercontraction (contraction band necrosis)
- **Subacute/chronic cases:** microscopic foci of granulation tissue and/or scar

## Veins and Lymphatics

Varicose veins and phlebothrombosis/thrombophlebitis account for at least 90% of clinically relevant venous disease.

### Varicose Veins of the Extremities

**Definition:** Abnormally dilated, tortuous veins produced by chronically increased intraluminal pressures and weakened vessel wall support

**Epidemiology:**
- Superficial veins of upper and lower leg typically involved
- Up to 1/5 of men and 1/3 of women develop lower-extremity varicose veins
- Obesity increases risk
- Higher incidence in women → prolonged elevation in venous pressure from compression of inferior vena cava by gravid uterus during pregnancy
- Familial tendency toward premature varicosities

#### Clinical Features

**Consequences of Varicose Dilation:**
- Venous valves become incompetent
- Lower-extremity stasis, congestion, edema, pain, thrombosis

**Most Disabling Sequelae:**
- Persistent edema in extremity
- Secondary ischemic skin changes: stasis dermatitis and ulcerations
- Chronic varicose ulcers (poor wound healing, superimposed infections)

**Note:** Embolism from superficial veins very rare (contrast with relatively frequent emboli from thrombosed deep veins)

### Varicosities of Other Sites

#### Esophageal Varices
- Liver cirrhosis (less frequently portal vein obstruction or hepatic vein thrombosis) → portal venous hypertension
- Opens porto-systemic shunts → increased blood flow into veins at:
  1. Gastroesophageal junction (esophageal varices)
  2. Rectum (hemorrhoids)
  3. Periumbilical veins of abdominal wall (caput medusae)
- Esophageal varices most important → prone to rupture → massive (even fatal) upper GI hemorrhage

#### Hemorrhoids
- Varicose dilations of venous plexus at anorectal junction
- Result from prolonged pelvic vascular congestion (pregnancy, straining to defecate)
- Source of bleeding; prone to thrombosis and painful ulceration

### Thrombophlebitis and Phlebothrombosis

**Definition:** Venous thrombosis accompanied by inflammation (terms largely interchangeable)

**Location:**
- Deep leg veins account for >90% of cases
- Other sites: prostatic venous plexus (males), pelvic venous plexus (females), large veins in skull, dural sinuses (especially with infection/inflammation)
- Peritoneal infections (peritonitis, appendicitis, salpingitis, pelvic abscesses), hypercoagulable conditions (polycythemia vera) → portal vein thrombosis

**Risk Factors for Deep Venous Thrombosis (DVT):**
- Prolonged immobilization (venous stasis) — extended bed rest, long plane/automobile trips
- Postoperative state
- Congestive heart failure
- Pregnancy
- Oral contraceptive use
- Malignancy
- Obesity
- Male sex
- Age >50 years
- Inherited defects in coagulation factors

**Note:** Venous thrombi also can result from procoagulant factors from cancers → hypercoagulable state → "migratory thrombophlebitis" or "Trousseau syndrome" (evanescent thromboses in different vascular beds)

**Clinical Signs:**
- Many DVTs asymptomatic
- When present: distal edema, cyanosis, superficial vein dilation, heat, tenderness, redness, swelling, pain
- Homan sign: pain elicited by pressure over affected veins, squeezing calf muscles, or forced dorsiflexion of foot
- Absence of findings does not exclude DVT

**Pulmonary Embolism:**
- Common and serious complication of DVT
- Fragmentation or detachment of venous thrombus
- First manifestation of thrombophlebitis in many cases
- Outcome ranges from resolution (no symptoms) to death

### Superior and Inferior Vena Cava Syndromes

#### Superior Vena Cava Syndrome
**Cause:** Neoplasms compressing/invading superior vena cava (bronchogenic carcinoma, mediastinal lymphoma)

**Presentation:**
- Marked dilation of veins of head, neck, arms
- Cyanosis
- Pulmonary vessel compression → respiratory distress

#### Inferior Vena Cava Syndrome
**Causes:**
- Neoplasms compressing/invading inferior vena cava
- Thrombus from hepatic, renal, or lower-extremity veins propagating cephalad

**Note:** Certain neoplasms (hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma) show striking tendency to grow within veins → may occlude inferior vena cava

**Presentation:**
- Marked lower-extremity edema
- Distention of superficial collateral veins of lower abdomen
- With renal vein involvement: marked proteinuria

### Lymphangitis and Lymphedema

#### Lymphangitis
**Definition:** Acute inflammation caused by bacterial seeding of lymphatic vessels

**Clinical Features:**
- Inflamed lymphatics appear as red, painful subcutaneous streaks
- Associated with tender enlargement of draining lymph nodes (acute lymphadenitis)
- If bacteria not contained → pass into venous circulation → bacteremia or sepsis

#### Lymphedema

**Primary Lymphedema:**
- Simple congenital lymphedema (isolated congenital defect)
- Milroy disease (heredofamilial congenital lymphedema) — agenesis or hypoplasia of lymphatics

**Secondary (Obstructive) Lymphedema:**
- Interstitial fluid accumulates behind obstructed previously normal lymphatic
- Causes:
  - Tumors involving lymphatic channels or regional lymph nodes
  - Surgical procedures severing lymphatic connections (e.g., axillary lymph nodes in radical mastectomy)
  - Postradiation fibrosis
  - Filariasis
  - Postinflammatory thrombosis and scarring

**Consequences:**
- Lymphedema → increased hydrostatic pressure in lymphatics distal to obstruction → edema
- Chronic edema → ECM deposition and fibrosis → brawny induration or peau d'orange appearance of overlying skin
- Inadequate tissue perfusion → skin ulceration
- Rupture of dilated lymphatics (typically following tumor infiltration) → milky accumulations:
  - Chylous ascites (abdomen)
  - Chylothorax
  - Chylopericardium

### Benign Tumors and Tumor-Like Conditions

#### Vascular Ectasias

**Ectasia:** Generic term for local dilation of a structure
**Telangiectasia:** Permanent dilation of preexisting small vessels (capillaries, venules, arterioles; usually skin or mucous membranes) forming discrete red lesion
- Can be congenital or acquired
- Not true neoplasms

**Types:**

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Nevus flammeus ("birthmark")** | Most common vascular ectasia; light pink to deep purple flat lesion on head/neck composed of dilated vessels; most ultimately regress spontaneously |
| **Port wine stain** | Special form of nevus flammeus; grows during childhood, thickens skin surface, does not fade; in trigeminal nerve distribution → associated with Sturge-Weber syndrome (facial port wine nevi, ipsilateral venous angiomas in cortical leptomeninges, mental retardation, seizures, hemiplegia, skull radiologic opacities) |
| **Spider telangiectasias** | Radial, often pulsatile arrays of dilated subcutaneous arteries/arterioles ("legs") about central core ("body"); blanch with pressure; face, neck, upper chest; associated with hyperestrogenic states (pregnancy, cirrhosis) |
| **Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler-Weber-Rendu disease)** | Autosomal dominant; mutations in genes encoding TGF-β signaling pathway components in ECs; telangiectasias = dilated capillaries and veins present at birth; widely distributed over skin, oral mucous membranes, respiratory/GI/urinary tracts; lesions can rupture → serious epistaxis, GI bleeding, hematuria |

#### Hemangiomas

**Definition:** Very common tumors composed of blood-filled vessels
- Constitute 7% of all benign tumors of infancy and childhood
- Most present from birth; initially increase in size but many eventually regress spontaneously
- Typically localized to head and neck
- Occasionally more extensive (angiomatosis) and can arise internally
- ~1/3 of internal lesions found in liver
- Malignant transformation rare

**Types:**

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Capillary hemangiomas** | Most common type; skin, subcutaneous tissues, mucous membranes of oral cavities/lips, liver, spleen, kidneys; composed of thin-walled capillaries with scant stroma |
| **Juvenile hemangiomas ("strawberry hemangiomas")** | Extremely common (1 in 200 births); can be multiple; grow rapidly for few months, then fade by 1-3 years; complete regression by 7 years in vast majority |
| **Pyogenic granulomas** | Capillary hemangiomas manifesting as rapidly growing red pedunculated lesions on skin, gingival, or oral mucosa; resemble exuberant granulation tissue; bleed easily and often ulcerate; ~1/4 develop after trauma; curettage and cautery usually curative; pregnancy tumor (granuloma gravidarum) occurs in gingiva of pregnant women |
| **Cavernous hemangiomas** | Large, dilated vascular channels; more infiltrative than capillary; frequently involve deep structures; do not spontaneously regress; sharply defined but unencapsulated; large blood-filled vascular spaces separated by connective tissue stroma; intravascular thrombosis and dystrophic calcification common; can be locally destructive; brain hemangiomas problematic (compression symptoms or rupture); component of von Hippel-Lindau disease (cerebellum, brain stem, retina, pancreas, liver); familial cerebral cavernous hemangiomas caused by mutations in CCM1, CCM2, CCM3 |

#### Lymphangiomas

**Definition:** Benign lymphatic counterpart of hemangiomas

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Simple (capillary) lymphangiomas** | Slightly elevated or pedunculated lesions (1-2 cm); predominantly head, neck, axillary subcutaneous tissues; composed of networks of endothelium-lined spaces distinguished from capillary channels only by absence of blood cells |
| **Cavernous lymphangiomas (cystic hygromas)** | Typically found in neck or axilla of children; more rarely retroperitoneum; can be large (up to 15 cm); massively dilated lymphatic spaces lined by ECs separated by connective tissue stroma containing lymphoid aggregates; margins indistinct and unencapsulated; difficult to excise completely; common in Turner syndrome |

#### Glomus Tumors (Glomangiomas)

**Definition:** Benign, exquisitely painful tumors arising from specialized SMCs of glomus bodies (arteriovenous structures involved in thermoregulation)
- Most commonly found in distal portion of digits (especially under fingernails)
- Distinction from cavernous hemangiomas based on clinical features and immunohistochemical staining for smooth muscle markers
- Excision curative

#### Bacillary Angiomatosis

**Definition:** Vascular proliferation in immunocompromised hosts (e.g., AIDS patients) caused by opportunistic gram-negative bacilli of Bartonella family

**Species:**
- **Bartonella henselae**: domestic cat reservoir; causes cat-scratch disease in immunocompetent hosts
- **Bartonella quintana**: transmitted by human body lice; caused "trench fever" in WWI

**Skin Lesions:** Red papules and nodules, or rounded subcutaneous masses

**Histology:**
- Proliferation of capillaries lined by prominent epithelioid ECs (nuclear atypia and mitoses)
- Infiltrating neutrophils, nuclear debris, purplish granular collections of bacteria

**Mechanism:**
- Bacteria induce host tissues to produce HIF-1α → drives VEGF production → vascular proliferation
- Infections and lesions cured by antibiotic treatment

### Intermediate-Grade (Borderline) Tumors

#### Kaposi Sarcoma (KS)

**Definition:** Vascular neoplasm caused by Kaposi sarcoma herpesvirus (KSHV/HHV-8)
- Most common in AIDS patients; used as criterion for diagnosis
- Four forms based on population demographics and risks:

| Form | Features |
|---|---|
| **Classic KS** | Older men of Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, Eastern European descent (especially Ashkenazi Jews); uncommon in US; associated with malignancy or altered immunity but not HIV; multiple red-purple skin plaques/nodules on distal lower extremities; progressively increase in size and spread proximally; usually asymptomatic, localized to skin/subcutaneous tissue |
| **Endemic African KS** | Younger (<40 years), HIV-seronegative; indolent or aggressive course; involves lymph nodes more frequently than classic variant; most common tumor in central Africa (with AIDS-associated KS); severe form in prepubertal children with prominent lymph node and visceral involvement; ~100% mortality within 3 years |
| **Transplantation-associated KS** | Solid organ transplant recipients with T-cell immunosuppression; risk increased 100-fold; aggressive course; often involves lymph nodes, mucosa, viscera; cutaneous lesions may be absent; lesions often regress with attenuation of immunosuppression (but risk of organ rejection) |
| **AIDS-associated (epidemic) KS** | AIDS-defining illness; most common HIV-related malignancy worldwide; incidence fallen >80% with antiretroviral therapy but still occurs in HIV-infected individuals (1000-fold higher incidence than general population; 2-3% of HIV-infected US population); often involves lymph nodes and disseminates widely to viscera early; most patients die of opportunistic infections rather than KS |

##### Pathogenesis of KS

- Virtually all KS lesions infected by KSHV (HHV-8)
- γ-herpesvirus transmitted through sexual contact and potentially oral secretions/cutaneous exposures
- KSHV and altered T-cell immunity required for KS development
- Acquired somatic mutations in cells of origin probably contribute to tumor development and progression

**KSHV Infection:**
- Causes lytic and latent infections in ECs
- **Lytic infection:** Virally encoded G protein induces VEGF → stimulates endothelial growth; cytokines from inflammatory cells at sites of lytic infection create local proliferative milieu
- **Latent infection:** KSHV-encoded proteins disrupt normal cellular proliferation controls (synthesis of viral cyclin D homologue) and prevent apoptosis (inhibit p53)
- Early stages: only a few cells KSHV-infected; with time, virtually all proliferating cells carry virus
- Like EBV infection: early lesions polyclonal → accumulation of mutations → monoclonal tumors

##### Morphology of KS

**Cutaneous Lesions Progress Through Three Stages:**

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **Patch** | Pink, red, or purple macules; typically confined to distal lower extremities; dilated, irregular, angulated blood vessels lined by ECs; interspersed infiltrate of chronic inflammatory cells (sometimes with hemosiderin); difficult to distinguish from granulation tissue |
| **Plaque** | Spread proximally, larger, violaceous, raised; dilated, jagged dermal vascular channels lined and surrounded by plump spindle cells; extravasated red cells, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, other mononuclear cells |
| **Nodule** | More overtly neoplastic; plump, proliferating spindle cells in dermis/subcutaneous tissues; often with interspersed slitlike spaces; express EC and SMC markers; hemorrhage and hemosiderin deposition more pronounced; mitotic figures common; nodal and visceral involvement (especially African and AIDS-associated variants) |

##### Clinical Features of KS

- Course varies widely according to clinical setting
- Most primary KSHV infections asymptomatic

**Treatment:**
- **Classic KS:** Surgical resection (excellent prognosis); radiation therapy for multiple lesions in restricted area; chemotherapy for disseminated disease
- **KS with immunosuppression:** Withdrawal of immunosuppressive therapy (with/without adjunct chemotherapy or radiotherapy) often effective
- **AIDS-associated KS:** HIV antiretroviral therapy generally beneficial; IFN-γ and angiogenesis inhibitors variably effective

#### Hemangioendotheliomas

**Definition:** Wide spectrum of borderline vascular neoplasms with clinical behaviors intermediate between benign hemangiomas and aggressively malignant angiosarcomas

**Epithelioid Hemangioendothelioma:**
- Tumor of adults arising in association with medium- to large-sized veins
- Highly variable clinical course
- Excision curative in majority
- ~40% recur; 20-30% metastasize; 15% die of tumors

### Malignant Tumors

#### Angiosarcomas

**Definition:** Malignant endothelial neoplasms ranging from highly differentiated (resembling hemangiomas) to wildly anaplastic lesions

**Epidemiology:**
- Older adults more commonly affected
- No gender predilection
- Most often involve skin, soft tissue, breast, liver
- Clinically aggressive → invade locally and metastasize
- 5-year survival rates only ~30%

**Associations:**
- **Lymphedema:** Classically in ipsilateral upper extremity years after radical mastectomy with lymph node resection for breast cancer (lymphangiosarcoma)
- **Radiation:** Can be induced by radiation
- **Foreign bodies:** Rarely associated with long-term indwelling foreign bodies (e.g., catheters)
- **Hepatic angiosarcomas:** Associated with carcinogens (arsenical pesticides, polyvinyl chloride)

##### Morphology of Angiosarcoma

**Skin:** Small, sharply demarcated, asymptomatic red nodules → large, fleshy red-tan to gray-white masses with margins blending imperceptibly with surrounding structures; necrosis and hemorrhage common

**Microscopy:**
- Extremely variable differentiation
- Plump atypical ECs forming vascular channels to undifferentiated spindle cell tumors without discernible blood vessels
- EC origin demonstrated by CD31 and von Willebrand factor staining

## Summary: Vascular Tumors

- Vascular ectasias are not neoplasms, but rather dilations of existing vessels.
- Vascular neoplasms can derive from either blood vessels or lymphatics, and can be composed of ECs (e.g., hemangioma, lymphangioma, angiosarcoma) or other cells of the vascular wall (e.g., glomus tumor).
- Most vascular tumors are benign (e.g., hemangioma); some have intermediate, locally aggressive behavior (e.g., Kaposi sarcoma); and others are highly malignant (e.g., angiosarcoma).
- Benign tumors typically form obvious vascular channels lined by normal-appearing ECs. Malignant tumors more often are solid and cellular, exhibit cytologic atypia, and lack well-defined vessels.

## Pathology of Vascular Intervention

### Endovascular Stenting

**Balloon Angioplasty:**
- Arterial stenoses (especially coronary and carotid arteries) dilated by transiently inflating balloon catheter to pressures sufficient to rupture occluding plaque
- Limited arterial dissection induced
- Complications: abrupt reclosure from compression by extensive circumferential/longitudinal dissection or thrombosis
- >90% of endovascular coronary procedures involve both angioplasty and concurrent coronary stent placement

**Coronary Stents:**
- Expandable tubes of metallic mesh
- Provide larger, more regular lumen
- "Tack down" intimal flaps and dissections from angioplasty
- Mechanically limit vascular spasm

**Complications:**
- **Immediate:** Thrombosis (from endothelial injury) → patients must receive potent anti-thrombotic agents (platelet antagonists) until stented region endothelializes
- **Long-term:** Proliferative in-stent restenosis → intimal thickening → clinically significant luminal occlusion in up to 1/3 of patients within 6-12 months

**Drug-Eluting Stents:**
- Release anti-proliferative drugs (paclitaxel, sirolimus) into adjacent vessel wall → block SMC activation
- Reduce incidence of restenosis at 1 year by 50-80%

### Vascular Replacement

**Synthetic Grafts:**
- Large-bore (12-18 mm diameter) conduits function well in high-flow locations (aorta)
- Small-bore artificial grafts (≤8 mm) generally fail from acute thrombosis or late intimal hyperplasia (primarily at junction of graft and native vasculature)

**Autologous Grafts for Small-Bore Replacement (e.g., coronary artery bypass):**
- Saphenous vein (from patient's leg) or left internal mammary artery
- **Saphenous vein grafts:** 50% patency at 10 years; occlude through thrombosis (early), intimal thickening (months to years postop), vein graft atherosclerosis
- **Internal mammary artery grafts:** >90% patent after 10 years
