# Chapter 3: Inflammation and Repair

## Overview of Inflammation

### Definitions and General Features

**Inflammation** is a response of vascularized tissues to infections and tissue damage. Its purpose is to bring cells and molecules of host defense from the circulation to the sites where they are needed, to eliminate offending agents.

Although inflammation often suggests a harmful reaction, it is actually a **protective response essential for survival**. It serves to:

- Rid the host of the initial cause of cell injury (e.g., microbes, toxins)
- Remove the consequences of such injury (e.g., necrotic cells and tissues)

The mediators of defense include **phagocytic leukocytes**, **antibodies**, and **complement proteins** (Fig. 3.1). Most of these circulate in the blood, where they are sequestered so they cannot damage normal tissues but can be rapidly recruited to any site in the body. Some cells involved in inflammatory responses also reside in tissues as sentinels on the lookout for threats.

Without inflammation, infections would go unchecked, wounds would never heal, and injured tissues might remain permanent festering sores.

#### Steps of the Inflammatory Reaction

The typical inflammatory reaction develops through sequential steps:

1. The offending agent (located in extravascular tissues) is recognized by host cells and molecules
2. Leukocytes and plasma proteins are recruited from the circulation to the site of the offending agent
3. Leukocytes and proteins are activated and work together to destroy and eliminate the offending substance
4. The reaction is controlled and terminated
5. The damaged tissue is repaired

#### Types of Inflammation

Inflammation may be of two types: **acute** and **chronic** (Table 3.1).

**Acute inflammation** is the initial, rapid response to infections and tissue damage:
- Develops within minutes or hours
- Short duration: several hours or a few days
- Main characteristics: exudation of fluid and plasma proteins (edema) and emigration of leukocytes, predominantly **neutrophils** (also called polymorphonuclear leukocytes)

When acute inflammation achieves its goal of eliminating the offenders, the reaction subsides and residual injury is repaired. But if the initial response fails to clear the stimulus, the reaction progresses to **chronic inflammation**.

**Chronic inflammation** may follow acute inflammation or arise de novo:
- Longer duration
- Associated with more tissue destruction
- Characterized by the presence of **lymphocytes** and **macrophages**
- Involves proliferation of blood vessels and **fibrosis**

#### Chemical Mediators of Inflammation

Inflammation is induced by **chemical mediators** produced by host cells in response to injurious stimuli. When a microbe enters a tissue or the tissue is injured, the presence of infection or damage is sensed by resident cells (macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, and other cell types). These cells secrete molecules (cytokines and other mediators) that induce and regulate the subsequent inflammatory response.

Inflammatory mediators are also produced from plasma proteins that react to the microbes or to products of necrotic cells. Mediators:
- Promote the efflux of plasma and recruitment of circulating leukocytes to the site
- Activate recruited leukocytes, enhancing their ability to destroy and remove the offending agent

Most anti-inflammatory drugs target specific mediators.

#### Cardinal Signs of Inflammation

The external manifestations of inflammation (cardinal signs) are:
- **Heat** (calor)
- **Redness** (rubor)
- **Swelling** (tumor)
- **Pain** (dolor)
- **Loss of function** (functio laesa)

The first four were described more than 2000 years ago by Celsus (Roman encyclopedist); the fifth was added in the late 19th century by **Rudolf Virchow**, known as the "father of modern pathology."

#### Inflammation as a Cause of Disease

Although normally protective, in some situations the inflammatory reaction becomes the cause of disease, and the damage it produces is its dominant feature. Examples:

- Inflammatory reactions to infections are often accompanied by local tissue damage (e.g., pain and functional impairment)
- In **autoimmune diseases**, inflammatory reactions are misdirected against self tissues
- In **allergies**, reactions occur against normally harmless environmental substances
- In some infections by microbes that resist eradication, inflammation is excessively prolonged

Inflammatory reactions underlie common chronic diseases such as **rheumatoid arthritis**, **atherosclerosis**, and **lung fibrosis**, as well as life-threatening hypersensitivity reactions to insect bites, drugs, and toxins (Table 3.2).

Inflammation may also contribute to diseases thought to be primarily metabolic, degenerative, or genetic disorders, such as **type 2 diabetes**, **Alzheimer disease**, and **cancer**.

#### Defective Inflammation

Not only excessive inflammation but also **defective inflammation** is responsible for serious illness. Too little inflammation—typically manifested by increased susceptibility to infections—is most often caused by:
- Reduced number of leukocytes resulting from replacement of the bone marrow by cancers
- Suppression of the marrow by therapies for cancer and graft rejection

Leukocytes arise from progenitors in the bone marrow; any compromise of marrow function diminishes the generation of mature leukocytes. Inherited genetic abnormalities of leukocyte function are rare but provide valuable information about leukocyte response mechanisms.

#### Termination of Inflammation

Inflammation is terminated when the offending agent is eliminated. The reaction resolves because:
- Mediators are broken down and dissipated
- Leukocytes have short life spans in tissues
- Anti-inflammatory mechanisms are activated

After inflammation has achieved its goal, it sets into motion the process of **tissue repair**, which consists of healing damaged tissue by replacing injured tissue through **regeneration of surviving cells** and filling residual defects with **connective tissue (scarring)**.

### Causes of Inflammation

Inflammatory reactions may be triggered by:

- **Infections** (bacterial, viral, fungal, parasitic) and microbial toxins — Among the most common and medically important causes. Different infectious pathogens elicit distinct inflammatory responses.
- **Tissue necrosis** — Elicits inflammation regardless of cause of cell death (ischemia, trauma, physical and chemical injury). Several molecules released from necrotic cells trigger inflammation.
- **Foreign bodies** — Splinters, dirt, sutures may cause traumatic tissue injury or carry microbes. Some endogenous substances also stimulate inflammation if large amounts are deposited (e.g., urate crystals in gout, cholesterol crystals in atherosclerosis).
- **Immune reactions (hypersensitivity)** — Reactions in which the normally protective immune system damages the individual's own tissues. May be directed against self antigens (autoimmune diseases) or environmental substances (allergies).

Because the stimuli in autoimmune and allergic diseases cannot be eliminated, these reactions tend to be persistent and difficult to cure.

### Recognition of Microbes and Damaged Cells

The first step in inflammatory responses is recognition of microbes and necrotic cells by **cellular receptors** and **circulating proteins**.

#### Cellular Receptors for Microbes

Phagocytes, **dendritic cells**, and many other cells express receptors that detect infectious pathogens:

- **Toll-like receptors (TLRs)** — Located in plasma membranes and endosomes; detect extracellular and ingested microbes. Recognize motifs common to many microbes called **pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)**.
- Other microbial sensors are present in the **cytoplasm** of cells.

Recognition by these receptors stimulates production of cytokines (inducing inflammation and anti-viral interferons) and membrane proteins that promote lymphocyte activation.

#### Sensors of Cell Damage

All cells have cytosolic receptors that recognize molecules liberated or altered as a consequence of cell damage — called **damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)**:

- **Uric acid** (product of DNA breakdown)
- **ATP** (released from damaged mitochondria)
- **Reduced intracellular K⁺** (reflecting loss of ions due to plasma membrane injury)
- **DNA** (released into cytoplasm)

These receptors activate a multiprotein cytosolic complex called the **inflammasome**, which induces production of **interleukin-1 (IL-1)**. IL-1 recruits leukocytes and induces inflammation.

Gain-of-function mutations in cytosolic receptors cause **autoinflammatory syndromes** (characterized by spontaneous inflammation); IL-1 antagonists are effective treatments.

The inflammasome has also been implicated in reactions to urate crystals (gout), cholesterol crystals (atherosclerosis), lipids (metabolic syndrome, obesity-associated diabetes), and amyloid deposits (Alzheimer disease).

#### Circulating Proteins

Several plasma proteins recognize microbes and function to destroy blood-borne microbes and stimulate inflammation at tissue sites:

- **Complement system** — Reacts against microbes and produces mediators of inflammation
- **Mannose-binding lectin** — Recognizes microbial sugars; promotes ingestion of microbes and complement activation
- **Collectins** — Bind to microbes and promote phagocytosis

### Summary: General Features and Causes of Inflammation

- **Inflammation** is a beneficial host response to foreign invaders and necrotic tissue, but may also cause tissue damage
- Main components are a **vascular reaction** and a **cellular response**; both are activated by mediators from plasma proteins and various cells
- Steps of the inflammatory response (five Rs):
  1. Recognition of the injurious agent
  2. Recruitment of leukocytes
  3. Removal of the agent
  4. Regulation (control) of the response
  5. Resolution (repair)
- Causes of inflammation include infections, tissue necrosis, foreign bodies, trauma, and immune responses
- Epithelial cells, tissue macrophages and dendritic cells, leukocytes, and other cell types express receptors that sense the presence of microbes and necrotic cells
- Outcome of acute inflammation is either elimination of the noxious stimulus followed by decline and repair, or persistent injury resulting in chronic inflammation

## Acute Inflammation

Acute inflammation has three major components:

1. **Dilation of small vessels** — Leading to increased blood flow
2. **Increased permeability of the microvasculature** — Enabling plasma proteins and leukocytes to leave the circulation
3. **Emigration of leukocytes** — From the microcirculation, their accumulation at the focus of injury, and their activation to eliminate the offending agent

### Reactions of Blood Vessels in Acute Inflammation

The vascular reactions of acute inflammation consist of changes in blood flow and vessel permeability, designed to maximize movement of plasma proteins and leukocytes out of the circulation and into the site of infection or injury.

#### Exudation

The escape of fluid, proteins, and blood cells from the vascular system into interstitial tissues or body cavities is known as **exudation**:

- **Exudate** — Extravascular fluid with high protein concentration and cellular debris; implies increased permeability of small blood vessels during an inflammatory reaction
- **Transudate** — Fluid with low protein content, little or no cellular material; essentially an ultrafiltrate of blood plasma produced as a result of osmotic or hydrostatic imbalance across vessels with normal vascular permeability
- **Edema** — Excess fluid in interstitial tissue or serous cavities; can be either an exudate or a transudate
- **Pus** (purulent exudate) — Inflammatory exudate rich in leukocytes (mostly neutrophils), debris of dead cells, and often microbes

#### Changes in Vascular Flow and Caliber

- **Vasodilation** — Induced by mediators (notably **histamine**) acting on vascular smooth muscle; one of the earliest manifestations of acute inflammation. May be preceded by transient vasoconstriction. Vasodilation first involves arterioles, then opens new capillary beds, resulting in increased blood flow (cause of heat and redness/erythema).

- **Increased permeability** — Quickly follows vasodilation, with outpouring of protein-rich fluid (exudate) into extravascular tissues.

- **Stasis** — Loss of fluid and increased vessel diameter lead to slower blood flow, concentration of red cells in small vessels, and increased blood viscosity. Histologically: vascular congestion. Externally: localized redness (erythema).

- **Leukocyte accumulation** — As stasis develops, blood leukocytes (principally neutrophils) accumulate along the vascular endothelium. Endothelial cells are activated by mediators produced at sites of infection and tissue damage, expressing increased levels of adhesion molecules. Leukocytes adhere to endothelium and migrate through the vascular wall into interstitial tissue.

#### Increased Vascular Permeability (Vascular Leakage)

Several mechanisms are responsible (Fig. 3.3):

1. **Retraction of endothelial cells** (most common mechanism)
   - Elicited by histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes, and other chemical mediators
   - Occurs rapidly after exposure (15-30 minutes); short-lived
   - Called the **immediate transient response**
   - Main sites: **postcapillary venules**

2. **Endothelial injury** (direct or leukocyte-induced)
   - Direct damage in severe injuries (e.g., burns)
   - Induced by microbes and microbial toxins targeting endothelial cells
   - Neutrophils adhering to endothelium may also injure endothelial cells
   - Leakage starts immediately after injury and is sustained for hours until damaged vessels are thrombosed or repaired

3. **Increased transcytosis**
   - Transport of fluids and proteins through endothelial cells
   - May involve intracellular channels that open in response to factors such as **vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)**
   - Contribution to acute inflammation in humans is unclear

#### Responses of Lymphatic Vessels and Lymph Nodes

In addition to blood vessels, **lymphatic vessels** also participate in acute inflammation:

- Lymphatics drain extravascular fluid that seeps out of capillaries
- In inflammation, lymph flow increases to help drain accumulated edema fluid
- Leukocytes, cell debris, and microbes may enter lymph
- Lymphatic vessels proliferate during inflammatory reactions

**Lymphangitis** — Lymphatic vessels may become secondarily inflamed

**Lymphadenitis** — Draining lymph nodes may become inflamed and enlarged (reactive or inflammatory lymphadenitis)

For clinicians, red streaks near a skin wound indicate infection in the wound, following the course of lymphatic channels (lymphangitis), possibly accompanied by painful enlargement of draining lymph nodes (lymphadenitis).

### Leukocyte Recruitment to Sites of Inflammation

Leukocytes recruited to sites of inflammation perform the key function of eliminating offending agents. The most important leukocytes capable of phagocytosis are **neutrophils** and **macrophages**:

- **Neutrophils** — Produced in bone marrow; rapidly recruited
- **Macrophages** — Slower responders

Principal functions:
- Neutrophils use cytoskeletal rearrangements and enzyme assembly for rapid, transient responses
- Macrophages (long-lived) make slower but more prolonged responses that often rely on new gene transcription

Both cell types ingest and destroy bacteria, necrotic tissue, and foreign substances. Macrophages also produce growth factors that aid in repair.

A price paid for the defensive potency of leukocytes is that strongly activated leukocytes may induce tissue damage and prolong inflammation (collateral damage of normal host tissues).

#### The Multistep Process of Leukocyte Recruitment

The journey of leukocytes from vessel lumen to tissue involves:

1. **Margination** — As blood flow slows early in inflammation, leukocytes assume a peripheral position along the endothelial surface
2. **Rolling** — Leukocytes bind and detach, tumbling on the endothelial surface
3. **Firm adhesion** — Mediated by integrins
4. **Transmigration** — Leukocytes migrate through the vessel wall
5. **Chemotaxis** — Movement toward the offending agent

#### Leukocyte Adhesion to Endothelium

Two major families of adhesion molecules mediate leukocyte adhesion and migration:

**Selectins** — Mediate initial weak interactions:
- **L-selectin (CD62L)** — On neutrophils, monocytes, T cells
- **E-selectin (CD62E)** — On endothelium activated by cytokines (TNF, IL-1)
- **P-selectin (CD62P)** — On platelets and endothelium; rapidly mobilized to surface after mediator exposure

Selectins bind sialic acid-containing oligosaccharides on glycoprotein backbones. The endothelial selectins are typically low or absent on unactivated endothelium and are upregulated after stimulation by cytokines. Thus, binding of leukocytes is largely restricted to endothelium at sites of infection or tissue injury.

**Integrins** — Mediate firm adhesion:
- Normally expressed in low-affinity form on leukocyte plasma membranes
- Do not adhere to specific ligands until leukocytes are activated by **chemokines**
- Chemokines bind to endothelial cell proteoglycans and are displayed at high concentrations on the endothelial surface
- When rolling leukocytes encounter displayed chemokines, integrins undergo conformational changes (high-affinity form)
- Cytokines (TNF, IL-1) activate endothelial cells to increase expression of integrin ligands:
  - **ICAM-1** (binds LFA-1/MAC-1)
  - **VCAM-1** (binds VLA-4)

The combination of cytokine-induced expression of integrin ligands on endothelium and increased affinity of integrins on leukocytes results in firm integrin-mediated binding.

Genetic deficiencies in adhesion molecules result in recurrent bacterial infections due to impaired leukocyte adhesion (leukocyte adhesion deficiencies).

#### Leukocyte Migration Through Endothelium

After arrest on the endothelial surface, leukocytes migrate through the vessel wall by squeezing between cells at intercellular junctions — called **transmigration**:
- Occurs mainly in postcapillary venules
- Driven by chemokines produced in extravascular tissues
- **PECAM-1 (CD31)** mediates binding events needed for leukocytes to traverse the endothelium
- After traversing endothelium, leukocytes pierce the basement membrane (probably by secreting collagenases) and enter extravascular tissue

#### Chemotaxis of Leukocytes

After exiting the circulation, leukocytes move toward the site of injury by **chemotaxis** — locomotion along a chemical gradient.

Chemoattractants include:
- **Bacterial products** (particularly peptides with N-formylmethionine termini)
- **Cytokines** (especially the chemokine family)
- **Complement components** (particularly **C5a**)
- **Leukotriene B4 (LTB4)** (product of lipoxygenase pathway of arachidonic acid metabolism)

All act by binding to **seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors** on leukocyte surfaces. Signals from these receptors activate second messengers that induce **actin polymerization**, resulting in leukocyte migration toward the inflammatory stimulus.

#### Nature of the Leukocyte Infiltrate

- In most forms of acute inflammation, **neutrophils predominate** during the first 6-24 hours
- **Monocyte-derived macrophages** gradually replace neutrophils over 24-48 hours

Reasons for early preponderance of neutrophils:
- More numerous in blood than other leukocytes
- Respond more rapidly to chemokines
- Attach more firmly to adhesion molecules rapidly induced on endothelium (P- and E-selectins)

Neutrophils are short-lived in tissues (undergo apoptosis within 24-48 hours). Macrophages survive longer and may proliferate, becoming dominant in prolonged inflammatory reactions.

Exceptions to this pattern:
- **Pseudomonas infections** — Neutrophil infiltrate for several days
- **Viral infections** — Lymphocytes may be first cells to arrive
- **Hypersensitivity reactions** — Activated lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells
- **Allergic reactions** — Eosinophils may be prominent

Agents that block TNF are among the most successful therapeutics for chronic inflammatory diseases. Antagonists of leukocyte integrins are approved for inflammatory diseases.

### Phagocytosis and Clearance of the Offending Agent

Recognition of microbes or dead cells induces **leukocyte activation**. After neutrophils and monocytes have been recruited to a site of infection or tissue injury, they must be activated to perform their functions.

The most important functional responses for destruction of microbes and other offenders are:
- **Phagocytosis**
- **Intracellular killing**

#### Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis involves three sequential steps:

**1. Recognition and Attachment**
- Mannose receptors, scavenger receptors, and receptors for various **opsonins** bind and ingest microbes
- **Mannose receptor** — Lectin that binds terminal mannose and fucose residues of glycoproteins and glycolipids (typically on microbial cell walls, not host cells)
- **Scavenger receptors** — Bind LDL particles and variety of microbes
- **Opsonization** — Efficiency of phagocytosis is greatly enhanced when microbes are coated by specific proteins (opsonins) for which phagocytes express high-affinity receptors
  - Major opsonins: **IgG antibodies**, **C3b** (complement), **mannose-binding lectin**

**2. Engulfment**
- After particle is bound to phagocyte receptors, pseudopods flow around it
- Plasma membrane pinches off to form a cytosolic vesicle (phagosome) enclosing the particle
- Phagosome fuses with lysosomes, discharging lysosomal contents into the phagolysosome
- During this process, phagocyte may release some granule contents extracellularly (damaging bystander cells)

**3. Killing or Degradation**
- Accomplished by **reactive oxygen species (ROS)**, **reactive nitrogen species** (mainly nitric oxide, NO), and **lysosomal enzymes**
- The final step in elimination of infectious agents and necrotic cells
- All killing mechanisms are normally sequestered in lysosomes

#### Intracellular Destruction of Microbes and Debris

**Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)**
- Produced by rapid assembly and activation of **phagocyte oxidase** (also called NADPH oxidase)
- Oxidizes NADPH and reduces oxygen to **superoxide anion (O₂⁻)**
- In neutrophils, this oxidative reaction is tightly linked to phagocytosis — called the **respiratory burst**
- Phagocyte oxidase components translocate from plasma membrane and cytoplasm to form the functional enzyme complex in the phagosomal membrane
- ROS are produced within the phagolysosome (acting on ingested particles without damaging the host cell)
- Superoxide is converted to **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)** by spontaneous dismutation
- **Myeloperoxidase (MPO)** in azurophilic granules of neutrophils converts H₂O₂ to **hypochlorite (OCl⁻)** in the presence of Cl⁻
- The H₂O₂-MPO-halide system is the most efficient bactericidal system of neutrophils
- H₂O₂ is also converted to **hydroxyl radical (OH•)**

Serum, tissue fluids, and host cells possess **anti-oxidant mechanisms**:
- **Superoxide dismutase** — Found in or activated in various cell types
- **Catalase** — Detoxifies H₂O₂
- **Glutathione peroxidase** — Powerful H₂O₂ detoxifier

Genetic defects in ROS generation cause **chronic granulomatous disease**.

**Nitric Oxide (NO)**
- Produced from arginine by **nitric oxide synthase (NOS)**
- Three types: **eNOS** (endothelial), **nNOS** (neuronal), **iNOS** (inducible)
- **iNOS** — Involved in microbial killing; expressed when macrophages are activated by cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ) or microbial products
- In macrophages, NO reacts with superoxide to generate **peroxynitrite (ONOO•)**
- NO produced by endothelial cells also relaxes vascular smooth muscle and promotes vasodilation

**Granule Enzymes and Other Proteins**
Neutrophils and monocytes contain granules packed with enzymes and anti-microbial proteins:

- **Specific (secondary) granules** — Lysozyme, collagenase, gelatinase, lactoferrin, plasminogen activator, histaminase, alkaline phosphatase
- **Azurophil (primary) granules** — MPO, bactericidal factors (defensins), acid hydrolases, neutral proteases (elastase, cathepsin G, proteinase 3)

Different granule enzymes serve different functions:
- **Acid proteases** — Degrade bacteria and debris within phagolysosomes
- **Neutral proteases** — Degrade extracellular components (collagen, basement membrane, fibrin, elastin, cartilage), causing tissue destruction in inflammatory processes
- **Neutrophil elastase** — Degrades virulence factors of bacteria

Anti-proteases in serum and tissue fluids control harmful proteases:
- **α1-anti-trypsin** — Major inhibitor of neutrophil elastase
- Deficiency of these inhibitors leads to sustained action of leukocyte proteases (e.g., α1-anti-trypsin deficiency)

#### Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)

NETs are extracellular fibrillar networks that:
- Concentrate anti-microbial substances at sites of infection
- Prevent spread of microbes by trapping them in fibrils
- Produced by neutrophils in response to infectious pathogens (mainly bacteria and fungi) and inflammatory mediators

Extracellular traps consist of:
- Viscous meshwork of nuclear chromatin
- Binds and concentrates granule proteins (anti-microbial peptides and enzymes)

Provides an additional mechanism of killing microbes **without phagocytosis**. In NET formation (NETosis), nuclei of neutrophils are lost, leading to cell death.

NETs may be a source of nuclear antigens in systemic autoimmune diseases (particularly **lupus**), in which individuals react against their own DNA and nucleoproteins.

### Leukocyte-Mediated Tissue Injury

Leukocytes are important mediators of injury to normal cells and tissues:

- **As part of normal defense** — Tissues at or near the site of infection suffer collateral damage
- **Inappropriately directed against host tissues** — As in certain autoimmune diseases
- **When host "hyper-reacts"** — Against usually harmless environmental substances, as in allergic diseases including asthma

Leukocytes damage tissues by releasing injurious molecules:
- **Degranulation** — Controlled secretion of granule contents following normal response of activated leukocytes
- **Frustrated phagocytosis** — When leukocytes encounter materials that cannot be easily ingested (e.g., immune complexes deposited on immovable flat surfaces such as glomerular basement membrane), triggering strong activation and release of large amounts of granule enzymes
- **Damage to phagolysosome membrane** — By phagocytosed substances (urate, silica crystals), leading to release of damaging contents

### Other Functional Responses of Activated Leukocytes

In addition to eliminating microbes and dead cells, activated leukocytes play several other roles in host defense:

- **Cytokines** — Amplify or limit inflammatory reactions
- **Growth factors** — Stimulate proliferation of endothelial cells and fibroblasts and synthesis of collagen
- **Enzymes** — Remodel connective tissues

Macrophages have central roles in orchestrating chronic inflammation and tissue repair.

Some **T lymphocytes** (particularly **TH17 cells** producing **IL-17**) also contribute to acute inflammation. IL-17 induces secretion of chemokines that recruit other leukocytes. Absence of effective TH17 responses leads to susceptibility to fungal and bacterial infections and "cold abscesses" lacking classic features of acute inflammation.

### Termination of the Acute Inflammatory Response

A powerful system of host defense with inherent capacity to cause tissue injury needs tight controls:

- **Mediators are produced in rapid bursts** — Only as long as the stimulus persists; short half-lives; degraded after release
- **Neutrophils have short half-lives** — Die by apoptosis within hours to a day or two after leaving the blood
- **Active termination mechanisms** are triggered as inflammation develops:
  - Switch in arachidonic acid metabolite production — From proinflammatory leukotrienes to anti-inflammatory **lipoxins**
  - Liberation of anti-inflammatory cytokines — **TGF-β** and **IL-10** from macrophages and other cells
  - Neural impulses (cholinergic discharge) — Inhibit TNF production in macrophages

---

### Summary: Leukocyte Recruitment, Activation, and Removal

- **Leukocytes** are recruited from blood into extravascular tissue, migrate to the site of infection or tissue injury, and are activated to perform their functions
- Leukocyte recruitment is a **multistep process**:
  - Loose attachment and rolling on endothelium (selectins)
  - Firm attachment (integrins)
  - Migration through interendothelial gaps
- Cytokines promote:
  - Expression of selectins and integrin ligands on endothelium (TNF, IL-1)
  - Avidity of integrins for their ligands (chemokines)
  - Directional migration of leukocytes (chemokines)
- **Neutrophils** predominate in early inflammatory infiltrate and are later replaced by **monocytes and macrophages**

## Mediators of Inflammation

**Mediators of inflammation** are substances that initiate and regulate inflammatory reactions.

Important mediators of acute inflammation:
- **Vasoactive amines**
- **Lipid products** (prostaglandins and leukotrienes)
- **Cytokines** (including chemokines)
- **Complement products**

### General Properties of Inflammatory Mediators

- **May be produced locally by cells** at the site of inflammation, or derived from **circulating inactive precursors** activated at the site
  - **Cell-derived mediators** — Rapidly released from granules (amines) or synthesized de novo (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, cytokines)
  - Major cell types producing mediators: tissue macrophages, dendritic cells, mast cells, platelets, neutrophils, endothelial cells, most epithelia
  - **Plasma-derived mediators** — Present in circulation as inactive precursors; activated by proteolytic cleavages; produced mainly in the liver

- **Active mediators are produced only in response** to molecules that stimulate inflammation (microbial products, substances released from necrotic cells)

- **Most mediators are short-lived** — Quickly decay, inactivated by enzymes, or scavenged/inhibited

- **One mediator can stimulate the release of others** — Providing mechanisms for amplifying or counteracting the initial action

### Vasoactive Amines: Histamine and Serotonin

**Histamine** and **serotonin** are the two major vasoactive amines:
- Stored as preformed molecules; among the first mediators released during inflammation
- Richest source of histamine: **mast cells** (present in connective tissue adjacent to blood vessels); also in blood basophils and platelets

**Histamine release** is triggered by:
- Physical injury (trauma, cold, heat)
- Binding of antibodies to mast cells (immediate hypersensitivity reactions)
- **Anaphylatoxins** (C3a and C5a)
- Neuropeptides (substance P) and cytokines (IL-1, IL-8)

**Histamine effects:**
- Causes **dilation of arterioles** and **increases permeability of venules**
- Principal mediator of the **immediate transient phase** of increased vascular permeability (interendothelial gaps in postcapillary venules)
- Acts via **H1 receptors** on microvascular endothelial cells
- H1 receptor antagonists (antihistamine drugs) block these effects
- Also causes contraction of some smooth muscles (but leukotrienes are more potent for bronchial muscles)

**Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine):**
- Present in platelets, certain neuroendocrine cells (gastrointestinal tract), mast cells (rodents, not humans)
- Primary function: neurotransmitter in GI tract
- Also a vasoconstrictor (importance in inflammation unclear)

### Arachidonic Acid Metabolites

**Prostaglandins** and **leukotrienes** are produced from **arachidonic acid** (20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acid) in membrane phospholipids:
- Synthesized by **cyclooxygenases** (prostaglandins) and **lipoxygenases** (leukotrienes and lipoxins)
- Also called **eicosanoids** (Greek eicosa = 20)

#### Prostaglandins

Produced by mast cells, macrophages, endothelial cells, and many other cell types:
- Generated by **COX-1** (constitutively expressed in most tissues; homeostatic function) and **COX-2** (induced by inflammatory stimuli; involved in inflammatory reactions)

Important prostaglandins in inflammation:
- **PGD₂** — Major prostaglandin made by mast cells; causes vasodilation, increases permeability of postcapillary venules; chemoattractant for neutrophils
- **PGE₂** — More widely distributed; causes vasodilation, increases permeability; involved in pain and fever
- **TXA₂ (Thromboxane A₂)** — Produced by platelets; potent platelet-aggregating agent and vasoconstrictor; promotes thrombosis
- **PGI₂ (Prostacyclin)** — Produced by vascular endothelium; vasodilator; potent inhibitor of platelet aggregation; prevents thrombus formation on normal endothelial cells

#### Leukotrienes

Produced in leukocytes and mast cells by lipoxygenase:
- **LTB₄** — Potent chemotactic agent and activator of neutrophils; causes aggregation, adhesion to venular endothelium, generation of ROS, release of lysosomal enzymes
- **LTC₄, LTD₄, LTE₄** (cysteinyl-containing leukotrienes) — Produced mainly in mast cells; cause intense vasoconstriction, **bronchospasm** (important in asthma), increased permeability of venules

#### Lipoxins

Generated from arachidonic acid by lipoxygenase pathway:
- **Suppress inflammation** by inhibiting recruitment of leukocytes
- Inhibit neutrophil chemotaxis and adhesion to endothelium
- Require two cell populations for transcellular biosynthesis (neutrophils produce intermediates; platelets convert to lipoxins)

#### Pharmacologic Inhibitors of Prostaglandins and Leukotrienes

- **Cyclooxygenase inhibitors** — Aspirin and other NSAIDs (ibuprofen); inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2; block all prostaglandin synthesis
  - **Selective COX-2 inhibitors** — More potent in blocking COX-2 than COX-1; may increase cardiovascular/cerebrovascular risk
- **Lipoxygenase inhibitors** — e.g., zileuton (useful in asthma)
- **Corticosteroids** — Broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory agents; reduce transcription of genes encoding COX-2, phospholipase A₂, proinflammatory cytokines, iNOS
- **Leukotriene receptor antagonists** — e.g., Montelukast (useful in asthma)

### Cytokines and Chemokines

**Cytokines** are proteins secreted by many cell types (activated lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, endothelial, epithelial, and connective tissue cells) that mediate and regulate immune and inflammatory reactions.

#### Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF) and Interleukin-1 (IL-1)

TNF and IL-1 serve critical roles in leukocyte recruitment:
- Produced mainly by **activated macrophages** and **dendritic cells** (TNF also by T lymphocytes and mast cells)
- Produced in response to microbial products, foreign bodies, necrotic cells, and other inflammatory stimuli

**Actions of TNF and IL-1:**

1. **Endothelial activation**
   - Increased expression of endothelial adhesion molecules (E- and P-selectins, integrin ligands)
   - Increased production of other mediators (cytokines, chemokines, eicosanoids)
   - Increased procoagulant activity

2. **Activation of leukocytes and other cells**
   - TNF augments neutrophil responses to other stimuli and stimulates macrophage microbicidal activity
   - IL-1 activates fibroblasts to synthesize collagen and stimulates proliferation of synovial cells
   - IL-1 and IL-6 stimulate generation of **TH17 cells**

3. **Systemic acute-phase response**
   - Fever (IL-1, TNF, IL-6)
   - Pathogenesis of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS)

4. **Regulation of energy balance**
   - TNF promotes lipid and protein catabolism and suppresses appetite
   - Sustained TNF production contributes to **cachexia** (weight loss, muscle atrophy, anorexia)

**TNF antagonists** are remarkably effective in treating chronic inflammatory diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease). Complications include increased susceptibility to mycobacterial infection.

#### Chemokines

A family of small (8-10 kD) proteins that act primarily as **chemoattractants** for specific types of leukocytes. About 40 different chemokines and 20 different receptors have been identified.

**Classification (four major groups based on cysteine arrangement):**

- **C-X-C chemokines** — One amino acid separating first two cysteines; act primarily on neutrophils
  - **IL-8 (CXCL8)** — Secreted by activated macrophages, endothelial cells; causes activation and chemotaxis of neutrophils
  - Inducers: microbial products and cytokines (IL-1, TNF)

- **C-C chemokines** — First two cysteines adjacent
  - **MCP-1 (CCL2)** — Monocyte chemoattractant
  - **Eotaxin (CCL11)** — Selective eosinophil recruitment
  - **MIP-1α (CCL3)** — Monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes

- **C chemokines** — Lack first and third cysteines; relatively specific for lymphocytes (e.g., lymphotactin, XCL1)

- **CX3C chemokines** — Three amino acids between first two cysteines
  - **Fractalkine (CX3CL1)** — Cell surface-bound on endothelial cells (promotes adhesion of monocytes and T cells) and soluble form (potent chemoattractant)

**Chemokine functions:**

1. **Acute inflammation** — Stimulate leukocyte attachment to endothelium (increase integrin affinity) and serve as chemoattractants (guiding leukocytes to sites of infection or tissue damage)

2. **Maintenance of tissue architecture** — Constitutively produced by stromal cells; organize various cell types in different anatomic regions (homeostatic chemokines)

Chemokines bind to **seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptors**. Developing chemokine antagonists has proved difficult (possibly due to functional redundancy).

### Complement System

The complement system is a collection of **soluble proteins** and **membrane receptors** that function mainly in host defense against microbes and in pathologic inflammatory reactions.

**More than 20 complement proteins** (numbered C1 through C9); function in both innate and adaptive immunity.

#### Pathways of Complement Activation

Cleavage of **C3** is the critical step. Three pathways lead to C3 cleavage:

1. **Classical pathway** — Triggered by fixation of C1 to antibody (IgM or IgG) combined with antigen
2. **Alternative pathway** — Triggered by microbial surface molecules (endotoxin, complex polysaccharides) in the absence of antibody
3. **Lectin pathway** — Plasma mannose-binding lectin binds to carbohydrates on microbes and directly activates C1

All three pathways lead to formation of **C3 convertase**, which splits C3 into **C3a** and **C3b**. C3b becomes covalently attached to the cell or molecule where complement is being activated. More C3b binds to form **C5 convertase**, which cleaves C5 to release **C5a** and leave **C5b** attached to the cell surface. C5b binds C6-C9, culminating in formation of the **membrane attack complex (MAC)**.

#### Functions of Complement

1. **Inflammation** — C5a, C4a, C3a stimulate histamine release from mast cells (increased vascular permeability, vasodilation). Called **anaphylatoxins**. C5a is also a chemotactic agent for neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils.

2. **Opsonization and phagocytosis** — C3b and iC3b (inactive C3b), when fixed to a microbial cell wall, act as **opsonins** and promote phagocytosis.

3. **Cell lysis** — MAC deposits on cells and creates holes in the cell membrane, making cells permeable to water and ions (osmotic death). Important for killing microbes with thin cell walls, such as **Neisseria bacteria**. Deficiency of terminal complement components predisposes to Neisseria infections.

#### Regulation of Complement

Complement activation is tightly controlled by cell-associated and circulating regulatory proteins:

- **C1 inhibitor** — Blocks activation of C1. Inherited deficiency causes **hereditary angioedema**.
- **Decay accelerating factor (DAF)** and **CD59** — Membrane-associated (GPI anchor); DAF prevents formation of C3 convertases; CD59 inhibits MAC formation. Deficiency causes **paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)**.
- **Factor H** — Plasma protein; cofactor for proteolysis of C3 convertase. Deficiency results in excessive complement activation.

Complement contributes to disease when activated by antibodies or antigen-antibody complexes deposited on host cells and tissues (important mechanism of cell and tissue injury in autoimmune diseases).

### Other Mediators of Inflammation

#### Platelet-Activating Factor (PAF)

A phospholipid-derived mediator with multiple inflammatory effects:
- Produced by platelets, basophils, mast cells, neutrophils, macrophages, endothelial cells
- Effects: platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction, bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, increased vascular permeability
- Trials of PAF antagonists have been disappointing

#### Products of Coagulation

Coagulation and inflammation are linked processes:
- **Protease-activated receptors (PARs)** — Activated by thrombin
- PARs expressed on leukocytes; roles in inflammation
- Thrombin activation of PAR on platelets is a potent trigger of platelet aggregation

#### Kinins

Vasoactive peptides derived from plasma proteins (kininogens) by specific proteases (kallikreins):
- **Bradykinin** — Produced from high-molecular-weight kininogen by kallikrein
- Effects: increases vascular permeability, causes smooth muscle contraction, dilates blood vessels, causes pain
- Short-lived; inactivated by kininase
- Implicated in some allergic reactions such as anaphylaxis

#### Neuropeptides

Secreted by sensory nerves and leukocytes:
- **Substance P** and **neurokinin A** — Produced in central and peripheral nervous systems
- Substance P functions: pain transmission, regulation of blood pressure, stimulation of hormone secretion, increasing vascular permeability

## Morphologic Patterns of Acute Inflammation

The morphologic hallmarks of acute inflammatory reactions are:
- **Dilation of small blood vessels**
- **Accumulation of leukocytes and fluid** in the extravascular tissue

Increased blood flow leads to redness (rubor) and warmth (calor). Increased vascular permeability leads to swelling (tumor). Leukocytes may release toxic metabolites and proteases extracellularly, causing tissue damage and loss of function (functio laesa). Pain results from damage and liberation of prostaglandins, neuropeptides, and cytokines.

### Serous Inflammation

Marked by exudation of **cell-poor fluid** into spaces created by injury to surface epithelia or into body cavities lined by peritoneum, pleura, or pericardium:
- Fluid is not infected by destructive organisms
- Does not contain large numbers of leukocytes
- In body cavities, fluid may be derived from plasma (increased vascular permeability) or from mesothelial cell secretions (local irritation)
- Accumulation of fluid in cavities is called an **effusion**
- Skin blister from burn or viral infection — Accumulation of serous fluid within or beneath damaged epidermis

### Fibrinous Inflammation

A **fibrinous exudate** develops when vascular leaks are large or there is a local procoagulant stimulus:
- Higher-molecular-weight proteins (fibrinogen) pass out of blood
- Fibrin is formed and deposited in extracellular space
- Characteristic of inflammation in linings of body cavities (meninges, pericardium, pleura)
- Histologically: eosinophilic meshwork of threads or amorphous coagulum
- May be dissolved by fibrinolysis and cleared by macrophages
- If fibrin is not removed, may stimulate ingrowth of fibroblasts and blood vessels → scarring
- **Organization** — Conversion of fibrinous exudate to scar tissue within pericardial sac → opaque fibrous thickening of pericardium and epicardium

### Purulent (Suppurative) Inflammation, Abscess

**Purulent inflammation** is characterized by production of **pus** — an exudate consisting of neutrophils, liquefied debris of necrotic cells, and edema fluid:
- Most frequently caused by infection with bacteria that cause liquefactive tissue necrosis (e.g., staphylococci) — called **pyogenic (pus-producing) bacteria**
- Example: acute appendicitis

**Abscess** — Localized collection of pus caused by suppuration buried in a tissue, organ, or confined space:
- Produced by seeding of pyogenic bacteria into a tissue
- Central region: necrotic leukocytes and tissue cells
- Usually a zone of preserved neutrophils around the necrotic focus
- Outside: vascular dilation and parenchymal and fibroblastic proliferation (chronic inflammation and repair)
- May become walled off and replaced by connective tissue
- When persistent or at critical locations (e.g., brain), may require surgical drainage

### Ulcers

An **ulcer** is a local defect or excavation of the surface of an organ or tissue produced by the **sloughing (shedding) of inflamed necrotic tissue**:
- Occurs only when tissue necrosis and resultant inflammation exist on or near a surface
- Most commonly encountered in:
  1. Mucosa of mouth, stomach, intestines, or genitourinary tract
  2. Skin and subcutaneous tissue of lower extremities in older persons with circulatory disturbances and extensive ischemic necrosis

Acute and chronic inflammation often coexist in ulcers (e.g., peptic ulcers, diabetic leg ulcers):
- **Acute stage** — Intense polymorphonuclear infiltration and vascular dilation in margins
- **Chronicity** — Margins and base develop fibroblast proliferation, scarring, and accumulation of lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells

---

## Outcomes of Acute Inflammation

Acute inflammatory reactions typically have one of three outcomes:

1. **Complete resolution** — Restoration of the site to normal after elimination of the offending agent
   - Occurs when injury is limited or short-lived with little tissue destruction and damaged parenchymal cells can regenerate
   - Involves removal of cellular debris and microbes by macrophages and resorption of edema fluid by lymphatics

2. **Healing by connective tissue replacement (scarring, or fibrosis)** — When:
   - Tissue injury is substantial
   - Injured tissues are incapable of regeneration
   - Abundant fibrin exudation in tissue or serous cavities cannot be adequately cleared
   - Connective tissue grows into the area, converting it into a mass of fibrous tissue

3. **Progression to chronic inflammation** — When the acute inflammatory response cannot be resolved due to:
   - Persistence of the injurious agent
   - Interference with the normal process of healing

## Chronic Inflammation

**Chronic inflammation** is a response of prolonged duration (weeks or months) in which inflammation, tissue injury, and attempts at repair coexist in varying combinations. It may follow acute inflammation or begin insidiously without signs of a preceding acute reaction.

### Causes of Chronic Inflammation

- **Persistent infections** by microorganisms difficult to eradicate (mycobacteria, certain viruses, fungi, parasites) — Often evoke delayed-type hypersensitivity; may produce **granulomatous inflammation**
- **Hypersensitivity diseases** — Excessive and inappropriate activation of the immune system
  - **Autoimmune diseases** — Self antigens evoke self-perpetuating immune reaction (rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis)
  - **Allergic diseases** — Excessive immune responses against environmental substances (bronchial asthma)
- **Prolonged exposure to potentially toxic agents** — Exogenous (e.g., **silica** in silicosis) or endogenous (e.g., cholesterol in atherosclerosis)
- **Role in pathogenesis of non-conventional inflammatory diseases** — Neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer), metabolic syndrome/type 2 diabetes, certain cancers

### Morphologic Features

In contrast to acute inflammation, chronic inflammation is characterized by:

- **Infiltration with mononuclear cells** — Macrophages, lymphocytes, and plasma cells
- **Tissue destruction** — Induced by persistent offending agent or by inflammatory cells
- **Attempts at healing** by connective tissue replacement — Angiogenesis and fibrosis

### Cells and Mediators of Chronic Inflammation

#### Role of Macrophages

**Macrophages** are the dominant cells in most chronic inflammatory reactions:
- Secrete cytokines and growth factors
- Destroy foreign invaders and tissues
- Activate other cells (notably T lymphocytes)

**Development:**
- Derived from hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow and from progenitors in embryonic yolk sac and fetal liver
- Circulating cells are **monocytes**
- Tissue macrophages include: Kupffer cells (liver), sinus histiocytes (spleen, lymph nodes), microglia (CNS), alveolar macrophages (lungs)

**In inflammatory reactions:**
- Bone marrow progenitors give rise to monocytes → blood → migrate into tissues → differentiate into macrophages
- Entry governed by same factors as neutrophil emigration (adhesion molecules, chemokines)
- Blood monocyte half-life: ~1 day
- Tissue macrophage life span: several months or years
- Become dominant cell population in inflammatory reactions within 48 hours

#### Two Major Pathways of Macrophage Activation

**1. Classical activation (M1)**
- Induced by microbial products (endotoxin engaging TLRs) and T cell-derived signals, particularly **IFN-γ**
- Produce NO and ROS, upregulate lysosomal enzymes
- Enhanced ability to kill ingested organisms
- Secrete cytokines that stimulate inflammation
- Important in host defense against microbes and in many inflammatory reactions

**2. Alternative activation (M2)**
- Induced by cytokines other than IFN-γ, such as **IL-4** and **IL-13** (produced by T lymphocytes)
- Not actively microbicidal
- Principal function: **tissue repair**
- Secrete growth factors that promote angiogenesis, activate fibroblasts, stimulate collagen synthesis

It seems plausible that the first activation pathway is classical (destroy offending agents), followed by alternative activation (initiate tissue repair).

**Functions of macrophages central to chronic inflammation:**
- **Secrete mediators of inflammation** — TNF, IL-1, chemokines, eicosanoids
- **Display antigens to T lymphocytes** and respond to T cell signals — Setting up feedback loop essential for cell-mediated immune responses

Their arsenal of mediators makes macrophages powerful allies but also causes considerable tissue destruction when inappropriately or excessively activated.

#### Role of Lymphocytes

**T and B lymphocytes** amplify and propagate chronic inflammation:
- **CD4+ T lymphocytes** — By virtue of cytokine secretion, promote inflammation and influence the nature of the inflammatory reaction
  - **TH1 cells** — Produce IFN-γ → activate macrophages by classical pathway
  - **TH2 cells** — Secrete IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 → recruit and activate eosinophils; responsible for alternative macrophage activation
  - **TH17 cells** — Secrete IL-17 and other cytokines → recruit neutrophils

**Macrophage-Lymphocyte Interactions:**
- Macrophages display antigens to T cells, express costimulators, produce cytokines (IL-12) that stimulate T cell responses
- Activated T lymphocytes produce cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF, IL-17) that recruit and activate macrophages
- Result: cycle of cellular reactions that fuel and sustain chronic inflammation

**B lymphocytes** and **plasma cells** (antibody-producing) are often present at sites of chronic inflammation. In some reactions, accumulated lymphocytes, antigen-presenting cells, and plasma cells cluster to form **tertiary lymphoid organs** (seen in rheumatoid arthritis synovium, Hashimoto thyroiditis, H. pylori infection gastric mucosa).

#### Other Cells in Chronic Inflammation

- **Eosinophils** — Abundant in IgE-mediated immune reactions and parasitic infections; recruited by adhesion molecules and chemokines (e.g., eotaxin); contain granules with **major basic protein** (toxic to parasites but also injures host epithelial cells)
- **Mast cells** — Widely distributed; participate in both acute and chronic inflammatory reactions; arise from bone marrow precursors; express FcεRI receptor for IgE; degranulate in immediate hypersensitivity reactions; secrete cytokines that promote inflammation
- **Neutrophils** — Characteristic of acute inflammation but many forms of chronic inflammation continue to show large numbers of neutrophils (induced by persistent microbes or cytokines from activated macrophages and T lymphocytes). Seen in chronic bacterial infection of bone (osteomyelitis), chronic lung damage from smoking

#### Granulomatous Inflammation

A form of chronic inflammation characterized by:
- **Collections of activated macrophages** (often with T lymphocytes)
- Sometimes associated with **central necrosis**

Activated macrophages may:
- Develop abundant cytoplasm and resemble epithelial cells → **epithelioid cells**
- Fuse forming **multinucleate giant cells**

Granuloma formation is a cellular attempt to contain an offending agent that is difficult to eradicate.

**Two types of granulomas:**

1. **Immune granulomas** — Caused by agents capable of inducing persistent T cell-mediated immune response (persistent microbe or self antigen). Macrophages activate T cells → produce cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ) → perpetuates response and activates macrophages.

2. **Foreign body granulomas** — Response to relatively inert foreign bodies in absence of T cell-mediated immune response (talc, sutures, fibers too large to phagocytose). Epithelioid cells and giant cells apposed to foreign body surface.

**Morphology:**
- Activated macrophages (epithelioid cells) have pink granular cytoplasm with indistinct cell boundaries
- Collar of lymphocytes surrounds aggregates of epithelioid macrophages
- Older granulomas may have rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue
- **Langhans giant cells** — Multinucleate (40-50 µm) with many nuclei
- **Caseous necrosis** — Central zone of necrosis with granular, cheesy appearance (classically in M. tuberculosis); amorphous, structureless, eosinophilic, granular debris
- Noncaseating granulomas — Without necrotic centers (Crohn disease, sarcoidosis, foreign body reactions)

### Summary: Chronic Inflammation

- **Chronic inflammation** is a prolonged host response to persistent stimuli that may follow unresolved acute inflammation or be chronic from the outset
- Caused by microbes that resist elimination, immune responses against self and environmental antigens, and some toxic substances
- Characterized by coexisting inflammation, tissue injury, attempted repair by scarring, and immune response
- Cellular infiltrate consists of **macrophages**, **lymphocytes**, **plasma cells**, and other leukocytes
- Mediated by cytokines produced by macrophages and lymphocytes (notably T lymphocytes); bidirectional interactions amplify and prolong the reaction
- **Granulomatous inflammation** is a morphologically specific pattern of chronic inflammation induced by T cell and macrophage activation in response to an agent resistant to eradication

## Systemic Effects of Inflammation

Inflammation, even if localized, is associated with **cytokine-induced systemic reactions** collectively called the **acute-phase response**.

The cytokines **TNF**, **IL-1**, and **IL-6** are important mediators; other cytokines (type I interferons) also contribute.

### Clinical and Pathologic Changes of the Acute-Phase Response

- **Fever** — Elevation of body temperature (usually 1°-4°C)
  - **Pyrogens** — Substances that induce fever
  - Bacterial products (e.g., LPS, called **exogenous pyrogens**) stimulate leukocytes to release cytokines (IL-1, TNF — **endogenous pyrogens**) → increase cyclooxygenases → convert arachidonic acid to prostaglandins → in hypothalamus, prostaglandins (especially PGE₂) stimulate production of neurotransmitters that reset the temperature set point higher
  - NSAIDs (including aspirin) reduce fever by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis

- **Acute-phase proteins** — Plasma proteins (mostly synthesized in liver) whose concentrations increase several hundred-fold
  - **C-reactive protein (CRP)**, **fibrinogen**, **serum amyloid A (SAA) protein**
  - Synthesis stimulated by cytokines
  - Many acute-phase proteins bind to microbial cell walls (act as opsonins and fix complement)
  - Fibrinogen causes red cells to form stacks (rouleaux) → basis for **erythrocyte sedimentation rate** as a test for inflammation
  - Prolonged production of acute-phase proteins (especially SAA) in chronic inflammation can cause secondary amyloidosis
  - Elevated CRP serves as a marker for increased risk of myocardial infarction in coronary artery disease
  - **Hepcidin** — Iron-regulating peptide increased in acute-phase response; chronically elevated levels reduce iron availability → anemia of chronic inflammation

- **Leukocytosis** — Common in inflammatory reactions (especially bacterial infections)
  - Leukocyte count usually 15,000-20,000 cells/mL; can reach 40,000-100,000 cells/mL (called **leukemoid reactions**)
  - Initially caused by accelerated release from bone marrow postmitotic reserve pool (cytokines including TNF and IL-1) → rise in immature neutrophils in blood ("shift to the left")
  - Prolonged infection induces proliferation of precursors (increased colony-stimulating factors, CSFs)
  - Most bacterial infections → **neutrophilia**
  - Viral infections → **lymphocytosis**
  - Allergies and parasitic infestations → **eosinophilia**
  - Some infections (typhoid fever, certain viruses, rickettsiae, some protozoa) → **leukopenia**

- **Other manifestations** — Increased heart rate and blood pressure; decreased sweating (blood flow redirected from cutaneous to deep vascular beds); rigors, chills, anorexia, somnolence, malaise

### Septic Shock

In severe bacterial infections (sepsis), large amounts of bacteria and their products in the blood stimulate enormous quantities of cytokines (notably TNF and IL-1). High blood levels cause:
- **Disseminated intravascular coagulation**
- **Hypotensive shock**
- **Metabolic disturbances** (insulin resistance, hyperglycemia)

This triad is known as **septic shock**.

A similar syndrome may occur in noninfectious disorders (severe burns, trauma, pancreatitis) — collectively called **systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS)**.

---

### Summary: Systemic Effects of Inflammation

- **Fever** — Cytokines (TNF, IL-1) stimulate production of prostaglandins in hypothalamus
- **Acute-phase proteins** — CRP and others; synthesis stimulated by cytokines (IL-6) acting on liver cells
- **Leukocytosis** — CSFs stimulate production of leukocytes from bone marrow precursors
- **Septic shock** (severe infections) — Fall in blood pressure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, metabolic abnormalities; induced by high levels of TNF and other cytokines

## Tissue Repair

**Tissue repair** is the process of healing damaged tissue resulting from inflammation or other causes. The inflammatory response to microbes and injured tissues sets into motion the process of repair.

Repair occurs by two types of reactions:
1. **Regeneration** — Proliferation of residual (uninjured) cells and maturation of tissue stem cells
2. **Scarring** — Deposition of connective tissue to form a scar

### Overview of Tissue Repair

- **Regeneration** — Some tissues can replace damaged components and return to a normal state
  - Occurs by proliferation of cells that survive injury and retain proliferative capacity
  - In other cases, tissue stem cells contribute to restoration
  - Mammals have limited capacity to regenerate damaged tissues

- **Connective tissue deposition (scar formation)** — When injured tissues cannot completely restitute or supporting structures are severely damaged
  - Laying down of connective (fibrous) tissue → scar formation
  - Fibrous scar provides structural stability even though not normal
  - **Fibrosis** — Extensive deposition of collagen in lungs, liver, kidney, other organs from chronic inflammation; or in myocardium after infarction
  - **Organization** — Fibrosis developing in tissue space occupied by inflammatory exudate

Both regeneration and scar formation contribute to ultimate repair after many common types of injury. Both involve proliferation of various cells and close interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM).

### Cell and Tissue Regeneration

**Regeneration** involves cell proliferation driven by growth factors and dependent on integrity of the extracellular matrix, and development of mature cells from stem cells.

#### Cell Proliferation: Signals and Control Mechanisms

Several cell types proliferate during tissue repair:
- Remnants of injured tissue (attempting to restore normal structure)
- **Vascular endothelial cells** (create new vessels for nutrients)
- **Fibroblasts** (source of fibrous tissue for the scar)

**Intrinsic proliferative capacity of tissues:**

- **Labile tissues** — Cells constantly being lost and replaced by new cells from stem cells and rapidly proliferating immature progenitors
  - Hematopoietic cells in bone marrow
  - Surface epithelia (skin, oral cavity, vagina, cervix)
  - Duct epithelia (salivary glands, pancreas, biliary tract)
  - Columnar epithelium (GI tract, uterus, fallopian tubes)
  - Transitional epithelium (urinary tract)
  - These tissues can readily regenerate after injury if the pool of stem cells is preserved

- **Stable tissues** — Normally in G₀ stage of cell cycle but capable of dividing in response to injury
  - Parenchyma of most solid organs (liver, kidney, pancreas)
  - Endothelial cells, fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells

- **Permanent tissues** — Terminally differentiated nonproliferative cells
  - Most neurons and cardiac muscle cells
  - Injury is irreversible → scar
  - Skeletal muscle has satellite cells providing some regenerative capacity

**Cell proliferation is driven by:**
- **Growth factors** — Produced by cells near the site of damage (mainly activated macrophages); bind to ECM proteins and are displayed at high concentrations
- **Extracellular matrix interactions** — Cells use integrins to bind ECM proteins; signals from integrins can also stimulate proliferation
- **Tissue stem cells** — Live in specialized niches; injury triggers signals that activate quiescent stem cells to proliferate and differentiate into mature cells

### Mechanisms of Tissue Regeneration

The importance of regeneration varies with tissue type and severity of injury:

- **Intestinal tract and skin epithelia** — Injured cells rapidly replaced by proliferation of residual cells and differentiation of cells derived from tissue stem cells (provided the underlying basement membrane is intact)
- **Parenchymal organs** — Cells capable of proliferation; with exception of liver, usually limited process
  - Pancreas, adrenal, thyroid, lung have some regenerative capacity
  - Surgical removal of kidney → compensatory response in remaining kidney (hypertrophy and hyperplasia)
  - **Liver** has extraordinary capacity to regenerate

**Liver Regeneration:**
- Demonstrated by growth after partial hepatectomy (for tumor resection or living-donor hepatic transplantation)

Two major mechanisms:
1. **Proliferation of remaining hepatocytes** following partial hepatectomy
   - In humans, resection of up to 90% of liver can be corrected by hepatocyte proliferation
   - Driven by cytokines (IL-6 from Kupffer cells) and growth factors (HGF from many cell types)

2. **Repopulation from progenitor cells** when hepatocyte proliferative capacity is impaired (chronic liver injury/inflammation)
   - Progenitor cells reside in specialized niches called **canals of Hering** (where bile canaliculi connect with larger bile ducts)
   - In rodents called **oval cells**

**Restoration of normal tissue architecture** can occur only when residual tissue is structurally intact (e.g., after partial surgical resection of liver). If entire tissue is damaged by infection or inflammation, regeneration is incomplete and accompanied by scarring.

---

### Summary: Repair by Regeneration

- **Labile tissues** (epithelia, hematopoietic) — Continuously dividing cells; readily regenerate
- **Stable tissues** (most parenchymal organs) — Normally quiescent but capable of proliferation
- **Permanent tissues** (neurons, cardiac muscle) — Non-dividing cells; cannot regenerate
- Cell proliferation is controlled by the cell cycle and stimulated by growth factors and ECM interactions
- **Liver regeneration** is a classic example: occurs by proliferation of surviving hepatocytes or repopulation from progenitor cells, triggered by cytokines and growth factors

### Repair by Scarring

When repair cannot be accomplished by regeneration alone, it occurs by **replacement of injured cells with connective tissue**, leading to scar formation (or by a combination of regeneration and scar formation).

Scarring happens when:
- Tissue injury is severe or chronic
- Damage involves parenchymal cells, epithelia, and connective tissue framework
- Nondividing cells are injured

Scarring is a response that "patches" rather than restores tissue.

#### Steps in Scar Formation

**1. Hemostatic plug formation** (within minutes)
- Platelet plug stops bleeding and provides scaffold for infiltrating inflammatory cells

**2. Inflammation**
- Typical acute and chronic inflammatory responses
- Complement activation breakdown products, chemokines from activated platelets, and other mediators recruit neutrophils (first 6-48 hours) then monocytes
- **Macrophages** are the central cellular players in repair:
  - **M1 macrophages** — Clear microbes and necrotic tissue, promote inflammation
  - **M2 macrophages** — Produce growth factors that stimulate proliferation in the next stage
- As injurious agents and necrotic cells are cleared, inflammation resolves

**3. Cell proliferation** (up to 10 days)
- Several cell types proliferate and migrate to close the wound:
  - **Epithelial cells** — Respond to locally produced growth factors; migrate to cover the wound
  - **Endothelial and other vascular cells** — Proliferate to form new blood vessels (**angiogenesis**)
  - **Fibroblasts** — Proliferate and migrate into site of injury; lay down collagen fibers forming the scar

- **Granulation tissue** — Combination of proliferating fibroblasts, loose connective tissue, new blood vessels, and scattered chronic inflammatory cells
  - Pink, soft, granular gross appearance (seen beneath the scab of a skin wound)

**4. Remodeling** (begins 2-3 weeks after injury; continues for months or years)
- Deposited connective tissue reorganized to produce stable fibrous scar
- Increased strength through collagen cross-linking and increased collagen fiber size
- Shift from type III collagen (early) to more resilient type I collagen
- In well-sutured skin wounds, strength may recover to 70-80% of normal skin by 3 months
- Wound contraction: initially by myofibroblasts; later by cross-linking of collagen fibers

#### Angiogenesis

**Angiogenesis** is the process of new blood vessel development from existing vessels. Critical in healing at sites of injury, development of collateral circulations at sites of ischemia, and allowing tumors to grow beyond their original blood supply.

**Steps in angiogenesis:**
1. Vasodilation in response to NO and increased permeability induced by VEGF
2. Separation of pericytes from abluminal surface and breakdown of basement membrane
3. Migration of endothelial cells toward tissue injury
4. Proliferation of endothelial cells behind the leading front ("tip") of migrating cells
5. Remodeling into capillary tubes
6. Recruitment of periendothelial cells (pericytes for capillaries; smooth muscle cells for larger vessels)
7. Suppression of endothelial proliferation/migration and deposition of basement membrane

**Signaling pathways involved:**
- **VEGF-A** — Stimulates migration and proliferation of endothelial cells; promotes vasodilation via NO production
- **FGF-2** — Stimulates proliferation of endothelial cells; promotes migration of macrophages, fibroblasts; stimulates epithelial cell migration
- **PDGF and TGF-β** — Recruit smooth muscle cells; stabilize new vessels; TGF-β suppresses endothelial proliferation/migration and enhances ECM production
- **Notch signaling** — Regulates sprouting and branching of new vessels (ensures proper spacing)

**ECM proteins** — Participate through integrin receptors of endothelial cells and provide scaffold for vessel growth

**Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)** — Degrade ECM to permit remodeling and extension of vascular tube

Newly formed vessels are leaky (incomplete interendothelial junctions; VEGF increases permeability) — accounts for edema that may persist in healing wounds.

#### Activation of Fibroblasts and Deposition of Connective Tissue

Two steps:
1. **Migration and proliferation of fibroblasts** into the site of injury
2. **Deposition of ECM proteins** produced by fibroblasts

Orchestrated by cytokines and growth factors (PDGF, FGF-2, TGF-β) from inflammatory cells (particularly M2 macrophages).

In response to cytokines and growth factors:
- Fibroblasts enter wound from edges and migrate toward center
- Some differentiate into **myofibroblasts** (contain smooth muscle actin; have increased contractile activity) → close the wound by pulling margins toward center
- Activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts produce **connective tissue proteins** (mainly collagen)

**TGF-β** is the most important cytokine for synthesis and deposition of connective tissue proteins:
- Produced by most cells in granulation tissue (including M2 macrophages)
- Regulated by post-transcriptional activation of latent TGF-β, rate of secretion, and ECM factors (integrins)
- Stimulates fibroblast migration and proliferation; increases collagen and fibronectin synthesis; decreases ECM degradation by inhibiting metalloproteinases
- Involved in scar formation after injury and development of fibrosis in lung, liver, kidneys from chronic inflammation
- Has anti-inflammatory effects that limit and terminate inflammatory responses

As healing progresses:
- Number of proliferating fibroblasts and new vessels decreases
- Fibroblasts assume more synthetic phenotype → increased deposition of ECM
- **Collagen synthesis** begins early (days 3-5) and continues for several weeks
- Progressive vascular regression → transforms highly vascularized granulation tissue into pale, largely avascular scar

#### Remodeling of Connective Tissue

After scar formation, it is remodeled to increase strength and contract it:

- **Wound strength increases** because of:
  - Cross-linking of collagen
  - Increased size of collagen fibers
  - Shift from type III to type I collagen

- **Wound contraction** — Initially by myofibroblasts; later by cross-linking of collagen fibers

**Degradation of collagens and ECM components** is accomplished by **matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)**:
- Dependent on metal ions (e.g., zinc) for enzymatic activity
- Produced by fibroblasts, macrophages, neutrophils, synovial cells, some epithelial cells
- Include:
  - **Interstitial collagenases** (MMP-1, -2, -3) — Cleave fibrillar collagen
  - **Gelatinases** (MMP-2, -9) — Degrade amorphous collagen and fibronectin
  - **Stromelysins** (MMP-3, -10, -11) — Degrade proteoglycans, laminin, fibronectin, amorphous collagen
- Neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, plasmin can also degrade ECM but are less important than MMPs
- **Tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs)** — Produced by most mesenchymal cells; rapidly inhibit activated collagenases

Balance of MMPs and TIMPs regulates the size and nature of the scar.

**Morphology:**
- **Granulation tissue** — Proliferation of fibroblasts and new thin-walled, delicate capillaries in loose extracellular matrix, often with admixed inflammatory cells (mainly macrophages)
- **Scar** — Largely inactive, spindle-shaped fibroblasts; dense collagen; fragments of elastic tissue; other ECM components
- Special stains: **Trichrome** (detects collagen — stains blue); **Elastin stain** (identifies elastin fibers)

---

### Summary: Repair by Scar Formation

- Repair occurs by **deposition of connective tissue and scar formation** when injured tissue is not capable of regeneration or structural framework cannot support regeneration
- Main steps: clot formation → inflammation → angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation → fibroblast migration and proliferation → collagen synthesis → connective tissue remodeling
- **Macrophages** are critical for orchestrating repair (eliminate offending agents; produce cytokines and growth factors that stimulate proliferation of repair cell types)
- **TGF-β** is a potent fibrogenic agent; ECM deposition depends on balance among fibrogenic agents, **MMPs**, and **TIMPs**

---

### Factors That Impair Tissue Repair

Factors that reduce quality or adequacy of reparative process:

**Extrinsic/Intrinsic factors:**

- **Infection** — Most important cause of delayed healing; prolongs inflammation and may increase local tissue injury
- **Diabetes** — Compromises tissue repair for many reasons; important cause of abnormal wound healing
- **Nutritional status** — Protein malnutrition and vitamin C deficiency inhibit collagen synthesis and retard healing
- **Glucocorticoids** — Anti-inflammatory effects; may result in weak scars (inhibit TGF-β production, diminish fibrosis)
- **Mechanical factors** — Increased local pressure or torsion may cause wound dehiscence
- **Poor perfusion** — Arteriosclerosis, diabetes (arterial), or obstructed venous drainage (varicose veins) impair healing
- **Foreign bodies** — Fragments of steel, glass, bone impede healing
- **Type and extent of tissue injury** — Complete restoration possible only in tissues with proliferating cells; extensive injury results in incomplete regeneration and at least partial loss of function; injury to tissues with nondividing cells must result in scarring
- **Location and character of tissue** — In tissue spaces (pleural, peritoneal, synovial cavities), small exudates may be resorbed and digested (resolution); large exudates undergo organization → granulation tissue → fibrous scar

### Clinical Examples of Abnormal Wound Healing and Scarring

#### Defects in Healing: Chronic Wounds

- **Venous leg ulcers** — From chronic venous hypertension (varicose veins, congestive heart failure); deposits of iron pigment (hemosiderin); poor oxygen delivery; fails to heal
- **Arterial ulcers** — From atherosclerosis of peripheral arteries (especially with diabetes); ischemia → atrophy → necrosis of skin and underlying tissues; can be quite painful
- **Pressure sores** — Areas of ulceration and necrosis from prolonged compression against bone (bedridden, immobile elderly); caused by mechanical pressure and local ischemia
- **Diabetic ulcers** — Lower extremities (particularly feet); tissue necrosis and failure to heal from small vessel disease (ischemia), neuropathy, metabolic abnormalities, secondary infections

**Dehiscence** (wound rupture) — May occur after abdominal surgery from increased abdominal pressure (vomiting, coughing, ileus)

#### Excessive Scarring

**Hypertrophic scar** — Raised scar with excessive collagen accumulation:
- Grow rapidly; contain abundant myofibroblasts
- Tend to regress over several months
- Develop after thermal or traumatic injury involving deep layers of dermis

**Keloid** — Scar tissue that grows beyond boundaries of original wound and does not regress:
- Certain individuals predisposed (particularly those of African descent)
- Does not regress

**Exuberant granulation** — Excessive amounts of granulation tissue protruding above skin level, blocking reepithelialization ("proud flesh")
- Must be removed by cautery or surgical excision

**Desmoids** (aggressive fibromatoses) — Exuberant proliferation of fibroblasts and connective tissue elements after incisional scars or traumatic injuries; may recur after excision; lie at the gray zone of benign and malignant low-grade tumors

**Contractures** — Exaggeration of normal wound contraction:
- Results in deformities of wound and surrounding tissues
- Particularly prone to develop on palms, soles, anterior thorax
- Commonly seen after serious burns; can compromise joint movement

### Fibrosis in Parenchymal Organs

**Fibrosis** — Excessive deposition of collagen and other ECM components in a tissue:
- Often refers to abnormal deposition in internal organs in chronic diseases
- Basic mechanisms same as scar formation in skin
- Pathologic process induced by persistent injurious stimuli (chronic infections, immunologic reactions)
- Associated with loss of tissue
- May cause substantial organ dysfunction and organ failure

**Major cytokine involved in fibrosis: TGF-β**
- Mechanisms leading to increased TGF-β activity not precisely known
- Cell death (necrosis or apoptosis) and ROS production seem important triggers
- Cells producing collagen in response to TGF-β vary by tissue:
  - In lung and kidney: **myofibroblasts** are main source
  - In liver: **stellate cells** are major collagen producers

**Fibrotic disorders** include:
- Liver cirrhosis
- Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma)
- Fibrosing diseases of lung (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, pneumoconioses, drug- or radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis)
- End-stage kidney disease
- Constrictive pericarditis

---

### Summary: Cutaneous Wound Healing and Pathologic Aspects of Repair

- Main phases of cutaneous wound healing: **inflammation** → **formation of granulation tissue** → **ECM remodeling**
- Cutaneous wounds can heal by **primary union (first intention)** or **secondary union (second intention)**; secondary healing involves more extensive scarring and wound contraction
- Wound healing can be altered by many conditions (especially infection and diabetes); type, volume, and location of injury are important factors
- **Excessive production of ECM** can cause keloids in the skin
- Persistent stimulation of collagen synthesis in chronic inflammatory diseases leads to tissue fibrosis, often with extensive loss of tissue and functional impairment
