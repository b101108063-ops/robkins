# Chapter 7: Genetic and Pediatric Diseases

## Nature of Genetic Abnormalities Contributing to Human Disease

Several types of genetic abnormalities affect the structure and function of proteins, disrupting cellular homeostasis and contributing to disease.

### Mutations in Protein-Coding Genes

The term **mutation** refers to permanent changes in DNA. Those that affect germ cells are transmitted to progeny and may give rise to inherited diseases. Mutations in somatic cells are not transmitted to progeny but are important in the causation of cancers and some congenital malformations.

**Common examples of gene mutations and their effects:**

- **Point mutations**: Substitution of a single nucleotide base by a different base, resulting in replacement of one amino acid by another in the protein product
  - The mutation in the β-globin chain of hemoglobin giving rise to **sickle cell anemia** is an excellent example
  - Sometimes called **missense mutations**
  - Certain point mutations may change an amino acid codon to a chain termination codon ("**nonsense**" mutations), causing interruption of translation and nonsense-mediated decay

- **Frameshift mutations**: Insertion or deletion of one or two base pairs alters the reading frame of the DNA strand

- **Trinucleotide repeat mutations**: Characterized by amplification of a sequence of three nucleotides
  - Example: In **fragile X syndrome**, there are 200 to 4000 tandem repeats of the sequence CGG within the FMR1 gene (normal populations average 29 repeats)
  - These mutations are **dynamic** (degree of amplification increases during gametogenesis)
  - They influence the pattern of inheritance and phenotypic manifestations

### Alterations in Protein-Coding Genes Other Than Mutations

In addition to alterations in DNA sequence, coding genes can undergo structural variations:

- **Copy number changes**: Amplifications or deletions
- **Translocations**: Result in aberrant gain or loss of protein function
- Structural changes may occur in the germline or be acquired in somatic tissues
- Pathogenic germline alterations often involve a contiguous portion of a chromosome (e.g., **22q microdeletion syndrome**)
- Cancers often contain somatically acquired structural alterations, including amplifications, deletions, and translocations
- Classic example: **Philadelphia chromosome** — translocation t(9;22) between BCR and ABL genes in **chronic myeloid leukemia**

### Alterations in Non-Coding RNAs

A very large number of genes do not encode proteins. The non-encoded products—**non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)**—play important regulatory functions.

Key examples:
- **MicroRNAs (miRNAs)**: Small RNA molecules
- **Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs)**: Longer regulatory RNAs

### Categories of Genetic Disorders

The three major categories of genetic disorders:

1. **Mendelian disorders**: Resulting from mutations in single genes
   - High penetrance (most individuals who inherit the anomaly show phenotypic effects)
   - Hereditary and familial
   - Include storage diseases and inborn errors of metabolism

2. **Complex disorders**: Involving multiple genes and environmental influences (multifactorial diseases)
   - Include hypertension, diabetes, and allergic/autoimmune diseases

3. **Chromosomal abnormalities**: Changes in number or structure of chromosomes
   - Several rare developmental abnormalities are attributable to chromosomal alterations

4. **Other genetic diseases**: Single-gene mutations that do not follow simple Mendelian rules
   - Include triplet repeat mutations, mutations in mitochondrial DNA, and those influenced by genomic imprinting

## Mendelian Disorders: Diseases Caused by Single-Gene Defects

Single-gene defects follow well-known Mendelian patterns of inheritance. Individually rare, together they account for approximately:
- **1%** of all adult hospital admissions
- **6% to 8%** of all pediatric hospital admissions

### Important Tenets and Caveats for Mendelian Disorders

- Mutations involving single genes follow one of three patterns: **autosomal dominant**, **autosomal recessive**, or **X-linked**
- A single-gene mutation may have many phenotypic effects (**pleiotropy**)
- Mutations at several genetic loci may produce the same trait (**genetic heterogeneity**)
  - Example: Marfan syndrome affects skeleton, eyes, and cardiovascular system (mutation in fibrillin gene)
  - Example: Several different types of mutations can cause retinitis pigmentosa
- Phenotypic manifestations of mutations are influenced by **modifier genes**
- Proactive prenatal genetic screening in high-risk populations has significantly reduced incidence of certain disorders

### Transmission Patterns of Single-Gene Disorders

#### Disorders of Autosomal Dominant Inheritance

- Manifested in the heterozygous state
- At least one parent in an index case usually is affected
- Both males and females can be affected; both sexes can transmit the condition
- Each child has one chance in two of having the disease when an affected person marries an unaffected one

**Key features:**
- Some patients do not have affected parents (new mutations in egg or sperm)
- **Reduced penetrance**: Some persons inherit the mutant gene but are phenotypically normal
- **Variable expressivity**: Trait is consistently associated with mutant gene but expressed differently among persons (e.g., neurofibromatosis 1 manifestations range from mild to severe)
- Age at onset is often delayed (e.g., Huntington disease)
- A 50% reduction in normal gene product is associated with clinical signs
- Involved genes usually encode:
  - **Receptors and transport proteins** (e.g., LDL receptor in familial hypercholesterolemia)
  - **Structural proteins** (e.g., collagen, spectrin)
- **Dominant negative effect**: Mutant allele impairs function of normal allele (seen in some forms of osteogenesis imperfecta)

#### Disorders of Autosomal Recessive Inheritance

- Manifested in the homozygous state
- Both alleles at a given gene locus are mutants

**Key features:**
- Parents are usually unaffected (carriers)
- Multiple siblings may be affected
- Each sibling has a 25% chance of being affected (recurrence risk)
- Strong likelihood of consanguineous marriage when mutant gene frequency is low
- Expression tends to be more uniform
- Complete penetrance is common
- Onset is frequently early in life
- Enzymes are often affected
- Heterozygotes usually function normally due to natural "margin of safety"

#### X-Linked Disorders

- Y chromosome carries SRY (testes-determining gene) but no Y-linked Mendelian disorders exist
- Sex-linked disorders are primarily X-linked
- Most X-linked disorders are X-linked recessive

**Key features:**
- Heterozygous female carriers transmit to sons (hemizygous for X chromosome)
- Heterozygous females rarely express full phenotypic change (random X inactivation allows sufficient normal cells)
- Affected male does not transmit to sons; all daughters are carriers
- Sons of heterozygous women have one chance in two of receiving the mutant gene

### Summary: Transmission Patterns of Single-Gene Disorders

- **Autosomal dominant disorders**: Expressed in heterozygous state; affects males and females equally; involves dysfunctional receptors and structural proteins
- **Autosomal recessive diseases**: Both copies of gene are mutated; frequently involves enzymes; males and females equally affected
- **X-linked disorders**: Transmitted by heterozygous females to sons; female carriers usually unaffected due to random X chromosome inactivation

## Diseases Caused by Mutations in Genes Encoding Structural Proteins

### Marfan Syndrome

An autosomal dominant disorder of connective tissues, manifested principally by changes in:
- **Skeleton**
- **Eyes**
- **Cardiovascular system**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Caused by inherited defect in extracellular glycoprotein called **fibrillin-1**
- Fibrillin is secreted by fibroblasts and is the major component of microfibrils in extracellular matrix
- Microfibrils serve as scaffolding for deposition of tropoelastin (component of elastic fibers)
- Particularly abundant in aorta, ligaments, and ciliary zonules of ocular lens
- Fibrillin is encoded by **FBN1 gene** at chromosomal locus 15q21
- More than 1800 distinct causative mutations have been found
- Diagnosis is mainly clinical because of mutational complexity
- Prevalence: 1 in 5000
- 70% to 85% of cases are familial; rest are sporadic (de novo FBN1 mutations)

**Pathophysiology:**
- Loss of microfibrils gives rise to abnormal and excessive activation of **TGF-β**
- Normal microfibrils sequester TGF-β, controlling bioavailability
- Excessive TGF-β signaling has deleterious effects on vascular smooth muscle development and extracellular matrix integrity
- Mutations in TGF-β type II receptor give rise to **Marfan syndrome type 2 (MFS2)**
- Angiotensin receptor blockers (which inhibit TGF-β activity) improve aortic and cardiac function in mouse models

**Treatment:**
- Beta-adrenergic blocking agents (lower blood pressure)
- Angiotensin receptor inhibitors (lower blood pressure and interfere with TGF-β activity)

**Morphology:**
- **Skeletal abnormalities**: Slender, elongated habitus; long legs, arms, and fingers (**arachnodactyly**); high-arched palate; hyperextensible joints; spinal deformities (kyphoscoliosis); chest deformities (pectus excavatum or pigeon-breast)
- **Eyes**: Bilateral dislocation or subluxation of lens (**ectopia lentis**) — highly specific for Marfan syndrome
- **Cardiovascular system**: 
  - Fragmentation of elastic fibers in tunica media of aorta → aneurysmal dilation and aortic dissection (**cystic medionecrosis**)
  - Dilation of aortic valve ring → aortic incompetence
  - Mitral valve may be excessively distensible → **floppy valve syndrome** with mitral valve prolapse
  - Aortic rupture is most common cause of death

### Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes (EDSs)

A group of diseases characterized by defects in collagen synthesis or structure. All are single-gene disorders with autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance.

Approximately 30 distinct types of collagen exist; each has characteristic tissue distributions.

**Clinical features common to all variants:**

- **Tissues rich in collagen** (skin, ligaments, joints) are frequently affected
- **Joint hypermobility**: Permits grotesque contortions; predisposition to joint dislocation
- **Skin fragility**: Extraordinary stretchability; extremely fragile; gaping defects with minor injuries; difficult surgical repair
- **Structural failure of organs/tissues**:
  - Vascular EDS: Rupture of colon and large arteries
  - Kyphoscoliotic EDS: Ocular fragility, rupture of cornea, retinal detachment
  - Classical EDS: Diaphragmatic hernias

**Molecular bases for common variants:**

- **Vascular EDS**: Deficient synthesis of type III collagen (COL3A1 mutations) → autosomal dominant; weakness of tissues rich in type III collagen (blood vessels, bowel wall)
- **Kyphoscoliotic EDS**: Deficiency of lysyl hydroxylase → autosomal recessive; decreased hydroxylation of lysyl residues interferes with crosslink formation
- **Classical EDS**: Deficient synthesis of type V collagen (COL5A1 and COL5A2 mutations) → autosomal dominant

### Summary: Marfan Syndrome and Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes

**Marfan Syndrome:**
- Caused by mutation in FBN1 gene encoding fibrillin
- Major tissues affected: skeleton, eyes, cardiovascular system
- Clinical features: tall stature, long fingers, bilateral subluxation of lens, mitral valve prolapse, aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection
- Prevention: drugs that lower blood pressure and inhibit TGF-β signaling

**Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes:**
- Six variants, all characterized by defects in collagen synthesis or assembly
- Clinical features: fragile, hyperextensible skin; hypermobile joints; ruptures involving colon, cornea, or large arteries; poor wound healing

## Diseases Caused by Mutations in Genes Encoding Receptor Proteins or Channels

### Familial Hypercholesterolemia

A "receptor disease" caused by **loss-of-function mutations in the gene encoding the LDL receptor**.

**Pathophysiology:**
- Impaired transport and catabolism of LDL → accumulation of LDL cholesterol in plasma
- Absence of LDL receptors on liver cells impairs transport of IDL into liver
- Greater proportion of plasma IDL is converted to LDL
- Marked increase of cholesterol uptake by monocyte-macrophages and vascular walls through scavenger receptor
- Results in skin xanthomas and premature atherosclerosis

**Inheritance and Prevalence:**
- **Autosomal dominant**
- Heterozygous frequency: 1 in 500
- Heterozygotes: two-fold to three-fold elevation of plasma cholesterol
- Homozygotes: more than five-fold elevation
- Heterozygotes develop xanthomas along tendon sheaths and premature atherosclerosis in adult life
- Homozygotes develop cutaneous xanthomas in childhood and often die of myocardial infarction before age 20

**LDL Receptor Mutations (five categories):**
- **Class I**: Complete loss of receptor synthesis (uncommon)
- **Class II**: Receptor synthesized but transport from ER to Golgi impaired due to protein folding defects (most prevalent)
- **Class III**: Receptors transported to cell surface but fail to bind LDL normally
- **Class IV**: Receptors fail to internalize within clathrin pits after binding LDL
- **Class V**: Receptors can bind and internalize LDL but are trapped in endosomes

**PCSK9 Regulation:**
- Enzyme PCSK9 causes intracellular degradation of LDL receptors in hepatocytes before they reach cell surface
- Activating mutations of PCSK9 cause similar phenotype to classic familial hypercholesterolemia
- Statins upregulate LDL receptors but increase PCSK9 levels, dampening effects
- PCSK9 inhibitors have been developed for refractory hypercholesterolemia

**Normal Cholesterol Metabolism:**
- Cholesterol derived from diet or endogenous synthesis
- Dietary triglycerides and cholesterol incorporated into chylomicrons
- Chylomicrons are hydrolyzed by lipoprotein lipase in capillaries
- Chylomicron remnants delivered to liver
- Liver synthesizes VLDL → converted to IDL → converted to cholesterol-rich LDL
- LDL receptor pathway metabolizes two-thirds of LDL particles
- LDL receptors are mostly located on hepatocytes (75%)
- Intracellular cholesterol homeostasis:
  1. Suppresses cholesterol synthesis (inhibits HMG-CoA reductase)
  2. Stimulates formation of cholesterol esters for storage
  3. Downregulates synthesis of cell surface LDL receptors

### Cystic Fibrosis (CF)

A disorder of **epithelial ion transport** affecting fluid secretion in:
- Exocrine glands
- Epithelial linings of respiratory, gastrointestinal, and reproductive tracts

**Key clinical manifestations:**
- Recurrent and chronic pulmonary infections
- Pancreatic insufficiency
- High level of sodium chloride in sweat (consistent and characteristic)

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence: 1 in 2500 live births in the United States
- Most common lethal genetic disease affecting white populations
- Carrier frequency: 1 in 20 among Caucasians

**Inheritance:** Simple autosomal recessive transmission

**Pathogenesis:**
- Primary defect: Reduced production or abnormal function of epithelial chloride channel protein encoded by **CFTR gene**
- Disruptive mutations render epithelial membranes relatively impermeable to chloride ions
- In sweat glands: CFTR reabsorbs luminal chloride and augments sodium reabsorption through ENaC → loss leads to decreased reabsorption of sodium chloride and hypotonic sweat
- In respiratory/intestinal epithelium: CFTR is major avenue for luminal chloride secretion → mutations result in loss/reduction of chloride secretion into lumen → increased sodium absorption → increased water reabsorption → dehydration of surface fluid layer → defective mucociliary action and viscous secretions
- CFTR also regulates bicarbonate secretion; certain variants manifest only with abnormal bicarbonate transport

**CFTR Mutations:**
- More than 1800 disease-causing mutations identified
- Can be classified by clinical course or nature of underlying defect
- Severe mutations: Complete loss of CFTR protein function (e.g., **ΔF508** — deletion of three nucleotides coding phenylalanine at position 508)
- Mild mutations: Some residual function
- ΔF508 found in approximately 70% of CF patients worldwide

**Modifier Genes:**
- CF phenotype is modified by other genes (e.g., **MBL2**, **TGF-β1**)
- Influence ability of lungs to tolerate infections with virulent microbes

**Morphology:**

*Pancreatic abnormalities (85-90% of patients):*
- Mild cases: Mucus accumulation in small ducts with some dilation of exocrine glands
- Advanced cases: Ducts totally plugged → atrophy of exocrine glands and progressive fibrosis
- Total loss of pancreatic exocrine secretion impairs fat absorption → vitamin A deficiency → squamous metaplasia of ductal epithelium

*Pulmonary changes (most serious complications):*
- Obstruction of air passages by viscous mucus secretions
- Bronchioles distended with thick mucus
- Marked hyperplasia and hypertrophy of mucus-secreting cells
- Superimposed infections → severe chronic bronchitis and bronchiectasis
- Common organisms: Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Burkholderia cepacia
- Burkholderia cepacia causes "cepacia syndrome" (fulminant illness)

*Liver involvement:*
- Bile canaliculi plugged by mucinous material
- Ductular proliferation and portal inflammation
- Hepatic steatosis ("fatty liver")
- Cirrhosis develops in less than 10% of patients

*Male reproductive tract:*
- Azoospermia and infertility in 95% of affected males who survive to adulthood
- Bilateral absence of vas deferens

**Clinical Course:**

- 5-10% present at birth with meconium ileus
- **Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency** (85-90%): Associated with "severe" CFTR mutations on both alleles
- **Pancreatic-sufficient phenotype** (10-15%): Have one severe and one mild mutation, or two mild mutations; enzyme supplementation not required
- Malabsorption: Large, foul-smelling stools; abdominal distention; poor weight gain; deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, K); hypoproteinemia; rectal prolapse in 10%
- Cardiorespiratory complications: Most common cause of death (approximately 80%)
- By 18 years of age, 80% of patients harbor P. aeruginosa, 3.5% harbor B. cepacia
- Liver disease: Third most common cause of death (after cardiopulmonary and transplant-related complications)

**Diagnosis:**
- Persistently elevated sweat electrolyte concentrations
- Characteristic clinical findings
- Family history
- Sequencing CFTR gene is gold standard

**Treatment:**
- More potent antimicrobial therapies
- Pancreatic enzyme replacement
- Bilateral lung transplantation
- **CFTR potentiators**: For patients with G155D mutation
- **CFTR modulators**: Combination of potentiator and drug that increases transport of defective Δ508 CFTR to cell membrane

### Summary: Familial Hypercholesterolemia and Cystic Fibrosis

**Familial Hypercholesterolemia:**
- Autosomal dominant disorder caused by LDL receptor gene mutations
- Patients develop hypercholesterolemia as a consequence of impaired LDL transport into cells
- Heterozygotes: Elevated serum cholesterol → increased risk of atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease; xanthomas
- Homozygotes: Even greater increase in serum cholesterol and higher frequency of ischemic heart disease

**Cystic Fibrosis:**
- Autosomal recessive disease caused by CFTR gene mutations
- Principal defect: Chloride ion transport → high salt in sweat; viscous luminal secretions in respiratory and GI tracts
- CFTR mutations can be severe (ΔF508) or mild
- Cardiopulmonary complications most common cause of death
- Pancreatic insufficiency common; infertility due to bilateral absence of vas deferens characteristic
- Liver disease including cirrhosis increasing as life expectancy increases
- Molecular therapies enhancing CFTR transport/stability useful for certain alleles

## Diseases Caused by Mutations in Genes Encoding Enzyme Proteins

### Phenylketonuria (PKU)

Results from mutations causing severe lack of **phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH)**.

**Epidemiology:**
- 1 in 10,000 live-born white infants
- Most common form ("classic phenylketonuria") common in Scandinavian descent
- Uncommon in African-American and Jewish populations

**Pathophysiology:**
- Autosomal recessive disorder
- Severe lack of PAH → hyperphenylalaninemia
- Inability to convert phenylalanine into tyrosine
- Normally less than 50% of dietary phenylalanine is needed for protein synthesis; remainder converted to tyrosine by PAH system
- When metabolism is blocked, shunt pathways produce intermediates excreted in urine and sweat → musty/mousy odor
- Excess phenylalanine or metabolites contribute to brain damage
- Lack of tyrosine (melanin precursor) → light hair and skin color

**Clinical Features (untreated):**
- Normal at birth
- Rising plasma phenylalanine level within weeks → impairs brain development
- Severe mental retardation by 6 months (less than 4% have IQ > 60)
- One-third never walk; two-thirds never talk
- Seizures, neurologic abnormalities, decreased pigmentation of hair and skin, eczema

**Prevention:**
- Hyperphenylalaninemia and mental retardation can be avoided by restricting phenylalanine intake early in life
- Newborn screening procedures performed routinely

**Maternal PKU:**
- Female patients with PKU who received dietary treatment may reach childbearing age clinically normal
- 75-90% of children born to these women are mentally retarded and microcephalic
- 15% have congenital heart disease (even though infants are heterozygotes)
- Caused by teratogenic effects of phenylalanine or metabolites crossing placenta
- Dietary restriction of phenylalanine must be initiated before conception and continued throughout pregnancy

**Molecular and Diagnosis:**
- Approximately 500 mutant alleles of PAH gene identified
- Infants with severe lack of PAH present with classic PKU features
- Those with some residual activity may have milder disease or benign hyperphenylalaninemia
- Measurement of serum phenylalanine levels differentiates benign hyperphenylalaninemia from PKU
- Specific mutation can be determined for carrier testing
- Enzyme replacement therapy (phenylalanine ammonia lyase/PAL) being tried

**Variants:**
- 98% of hyperphenylalaninemia cases are from PAH mutations
- Approximately 2% arise from abnormalities in synthesis or recycling of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4)
- These variants require dietary restriction plus supplementation with BH4, neurotransmitter precursors, and folic acid

### Galactosemia

An autosomal recessive disorder caused by lack of **galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT)**.

**Pathophysiology:**
- Deficiency of GALT leads to accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate and its metabolites in tissues
- Galactose is derived from lactose (glucose + galactose) in milk
- Galactose-1-phosphate accumulates in liver → liver damage (jaundice, hepatomegaly)
- Galactitol accumulates in lenses → cataracts
- Brain damage from elevated galactitol levels in neuronal tissues

**Clinical Features:**
- Jaundice and hepatomegaly (first week of life)
- Vomiting and diarrhea within days of milk ingestion
- Failure to thrive from birth
- Accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate in kidney impairs amino acid transport → aminoaciduria
- Fulminant E. coli septicemia (increased frequency)
- Newborn screening: Fluorometric assay of GALT enzyme activity on dried blood spot; confirmed by GALT levels in RBC

**Treatment and Prognosis:**
- Removal of galactose from diet for at least first 2 years prevents cataracts and liver damage; permits almost normal development
- Older patients frequently affected by speech disorder and gonadal failure (especially premature ovarian failure)
- Ataxic condition may occur

## Lysosomal Storage Diseases

Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes for breakdown of complex substrates (sphingolipids, mucopolysaccharides).

**Pathogenesis:**
- Inherited lack of lysosomal enzyme → incomplete catabolism of substrate → accumulation of partially degraded insoluble metabolites within lysosomes (**primary storage**)
- Impaired autophagy → **secondary storage** of autophagic substrates (polyubiquitinated proteins, old mitochondria)
- Accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria → free radical generation and apoptosis

**General Features:**
- Approximately 60 lysosomal storage diseases identified
- Combined frequency: about 1 in 5000 live births
- Autosomal recessive transmission
- Affects infants and young children
- Storage in mononuclear phagocyte system → hepatosplenomegaly
- Frequent CNS involvement → neuronal damage
- Cellular dysfunction from storage material and cascade of secondary events (macrophage activation, cytokine release)
- Most are fatal with profound neurologic impairment beginning in childhood

**Connection to Common Diseases:**
- Carrier state for Gaucher disease: Important genetic risk factor for Parkinson disease
- Nearly all Gaucher patients develop Parkinson disease
- Niemann-Pick C: Connected to risk for Alzheimer disease

### Tay-Sachs Disease (GM2 Gangliosidosis)

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common gangliosidosis
- Most common among Ashkenazi Jews
- Heterozygous carrier frequency: 1 in 30

**Pathogenesis:**
- **Loss-of-function mutations of the β subunit of hexosaminidase A** → inability to degrade GM2 ganglioside
- More than 100 mutations described; most disrupt protein folding or intracellular transport
- GM2 ganglioside accumulates in neurons, axon cylinders, and glial cells throughout CNS
- Neurons appear swollen and foamy
- Electron microscopy: Whorled onionskin-like configurations within lysosomes (membranous layers)

**Morphology:**
- Accumulation in neurons, axon cylinders of nerves, and glial cells throughout CNS (including spinal cord), peripheral nerves, and autonomic nervous system
- Retina involved → swollen ganglion cells in peripheral retina → "cherry red" spot in central macula
- Molecular basis for neuronal injury: Misfolded mutant protein induces "unfolded protein" response → proteasomal degradation → accumulation of toxic substrates

**Clinical Features (acute infantile variant):**
- Normal at birth
- Motor weakness at 3-6 months
- Neurologic impairment, blindness, progressively severe neurologic dysfunction
- Death within 2-3 years

**Treatment:**
- Molecular chaperone therapy trials: Synthetic chaperones cross blood-brain barrier, bind to mutated protein, enable proper folding

### Niemann-Pick Disease Types A and B

**Epidemiology:**
- Common in Ashkenazi Jews
- Gene for acid sphingomyelinase maps to chromosome 11p15.4 (one of imprinted genes)

**Type A:**
- Severe deficiency of sphingomyelinase
- Breakdown of sphingomyelin into ceramide and phosphorylcholine impaired
- Excess sphingomyelin accumulates in phagocytic cells and neurons
- Macrophages become stuffed with lipid droplets → foamy cytoplasm
- Electron microscopy: Engorged secondary lysosomes with membranous cytoplasmic bodies ("zebra" bodies)
- Most severely affected organs: Spleen, liver, bone marrow, lymph nodes, lungs
- Splenic enlargement may be striking
- CNS involved (including spinal cord and ganglia)
- Affected neurons enlarged and vacuolated
- Manifests in infancy with massive organomegaly and severe neurologic deterioration
- Death within first 3 years of life

**Type B:**
- Mutant sphingomyelinase with some residual activity
- Organomegaly but no neurologic manifestations

**Diagnosis:**
- Sphingomyelinase activity in leukocytes for diagnosis and carrier detection
- Molecular genetic tests available

### Niemann-Pick Disease Type C

**Distinct from Types A and B:**
- More common than types A and B combined
- Primary defect in **lipid transport** (not sphingomyelinase deficiency)
- Mutations in **NPC1** (majority) or **NPC2** genes
- Both involved in transport of free cholesterol from lysosomes to cytoplasm
- Accumulate cholesterol and gangliosides (GM1 and GM2)

**Clinical Features:**
- Most common form manifests in childhood
- Ataxia, vertical supranuclear gaze palsy, dystonia, dysarthria, psychomotor regression

### Gaucher Disease

**Pathogenesis:**
- Mutation in gene encoding **glucocerebrosidase**
- Deficiency of glucocerebrosidase → accumulation of glucocerebroside in mononuclear phagocytic cells
- Three autosomal recessive variants from distinct allelic mutations

**Morphology:**
- Macrophages ("Gaucher cells") become enlarged (up to 100 μm diameter)
- Distended lysosomes with pathognomonic "wrinkled tissue paper" cytoplasmic appearance
- Disease caused by storage burden AND macrophage activation
- High levels of macrophage-derived cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF) in affected tissues

**Clinical Features:**

*Type 1 (chronic nonneuronopathic — 99% of cases):*
- Most common in Ashkenazi Jews
- Clinical or radiographic bone involvement (osteopenia, focal lytic lesions, osteonecrosis) in 70-100%
- Hepatomegaly and absence of CNS involvement
- Spleen often enlarges to massive proportions
- Gaucher cells in liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow
- Marrow replacement and cortical erosion → skeletal lesions; peripheral blood cytopenias
- Compatible with long life

*Type 2 (acute infantile neuronopathic):*
- Neurologic manifestations in infancy
- More severe

*Type 3 (chronic neuronopathic):*
- Neurologic manifestations emerge later and are milder
- Liver and spleen also involved; clinical features dominated by neurologic disturbances (convulsions, progressive mental deterioration)

**Connection to Parkinson Disease:**
- Mutation of glucocerebrosidase gene: Very important risk factor for Parkinson disease
- Gaucher patients have 20-fold higher risk of developing Parkinson disease
- 5-10% of Parkinson patients have glucocerebrosidase mutations
- Reciprocal relationship between enzyme level and alpha synuclein

**Diagnosis:**
- Glucocerebroside levels in leukocytes or cultured fibroblasts
- DNA testing available in select populations

**Treatment:**
- Lifelong enzyme replacement therapy (infusion of recombinant glucocerebrosidase)
- **Substrate reduction therapy**: Oral inhibitor of glucosylceramide synthase → reduced systemic levels of glucocerebroside
- Gene therapy through transplant of hematopoietic stem cells containing corrected enzyme

### Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPSs)

Characterized by defective degradation and excessive storage of **mucopolysaccharides** in various tissues.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Mucopolysaccharides are part of extracellular matrix
- Synthesized by connective tissue fibroblasts
- Most is secreted; a fraction degraded within lysosomes through catabolic pathway involving multiple enzymes
- Several clinical variants (MPS I to MPS VII), each from deficiency of one specific enzyme
- Accumulated mucopolysaccharides: dermatan sulfate, heparan sulfate, keratan sulfate, chondroitin sulfate

**Clinical Features (all MPSs):**
- Hepatosplenomegaly
- Skeletal deformities
- Heart valve lesions
- Subendothelial arterial deposits (especially coronary arteries)
- Brain lesions
- Coronary subendothelial lesions → myocardial ischemia → myocardial infarction and cardiac decompensation
- Coarse facial features
- Corneal clouding
- Joint stiffness
- Mental retardation
- Increased urinary excretion of accumulated mucopolysaccharides
- Most are autosomal recessive; Hunter syndrome (MPS II) is X-linked recessive

**MPS Type I (Hurler Syndrome):**
- Deficiency of α-L-iduronidase
- Life expectancy: 6-10 years; death often from cardiac complications
- Accumulation of dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate in mononuclear phagocytes, fibroblasts, endothelium, smooth muscle
- Swollen cells with clear cytoplasm; material positive for periodic acid–Schiff staining within vacuolated lysosomes
- Neuronal lysosomal inclusions → mental retardation

**MPS Type II (Hunter Syndrome):**
- Deficiency of L-iduronate sulfatase
- X-linked inheritance
- Absence of corneal clouding
- Often milder clinical course
- Same accumulated substrates (heparan sulfate and dermatan sulfate)

### Summary: Lysosomal Storage Diseases

**Tay-Sachs Disease:**
- Inability to metabolize GM2 gangliosides due to lack of β subunit of hexosaminidase
- GM2 gangliosides accumulate in CNS → severe mental retardation, blindness, motor weakness, death by 2-3 years

**Niemann-Pick Disease Types A and B:**
- Deficiency of sphingomyelinase
- Type A: Accumulation of sphingomyelin in nervous system → neuronal damage; also stored in phagocytes in liver, spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes → enlargement
- Type B: No neuronal damage

**Niemann-Pick Disease Type C:**
- Defect in cholesterol transport → accumulation of cholesterol and gangliosides
- Ataxia, dysarthria, psychomotor regression

**Gaucher Disease:**
- Lack of glucocerebrosidase → accumulation of glucocerebroside in mononuclear phagocytic cells
- Type 1: Gaucher cells in liver, spleen, bone marrow; hepatosplenomegaly, bone erosion
- Types 2 and 3: Variable neuronal involvement

**MPSs:**
- Accumulation of mucopolysaccharides in liver, spleen, heart, blood vessels, brain, cornea, joints
- Coarse facial features in all forms
- Hurler: Corneal clouding, coronary arterial and valvular deposits, death in childhood
- Hunter: Milder clinical course

## Glycogen Storage Diseases (Glycogenoses)

Inherited deficiency of any enzyme involved in glycogen synthesis or degradation → excessive accumulation of glycogen or abnormal glycogen in various tissues.

**Inheritance:** Most are autosomal recessive

**Categories:**

### Hepatic Type

- Liver contains enzymes to synthesize glycogen and break it down into free glucose
- Deficiency of hepatic enzymes → hepatomegaly (glycogen storage) and hypoglycemia (failure of glucose production)
- **Von Gierke disease (type I glycogenosis)**: Lack of glucose-6-phosphatase — most important example

### Myopathic Type

- In striated muscle, glycogen is important energy source
- Deficiency of glycolysis enzymes → glycogen storage in muscles → muscle weakness from impaired energy production
- Muscle cramps after exercise, myoglobinuria, failure of exercise to raise blood lactate
- **McArdle disease (type V glycogenosis)**: Deficiency of muscle phosphorylase — prototype of myopathic glycogenoses

### Type II (Pompe Disease)

- Deficiency of lysosomal acid maltase
- Glycogen deposition in virtually every organ; cardiomegaly most prominent
- Death within 2 years from cardiorespiratory failure
- Enzyme replacement therapy (glucosidase) can reverse cardiac damage and modestly increase longevity

## Diseases Caused by Mutations in Genes Encoding Proteins That Regulate Cell Growth

Two classes of genes regulate normal cell growth:
- **Proto-oncogenes**
- **Tumor suppressor genes**

Mutations affecting these genes (most often somatic) are involved in tumor pathogenesis.

Approximately 5-10% of all cancers have mutations affecting tumor suppressor genes present in all cells (including germ cells) → can be transmitted to offspring → predispose to hereditary tumors.

## Complex Multigenic Disorders

Also called "**multifactorial**" or "**polygenic disorders**" — caused by interactions between genetic variants and environmental factors.

**Key concepts:**
- **Polymorphism**: Genetic variant occurring in at least 1% of population
- **Common disease–common variant hypothesis**: Complex disorders occur when many polymorphisms (each with modest effect and low penetrance) are coinherited
- Different polymorphisms vary in significance
- Some polymorphisms are common to multiple diseases of the same type; others are disease-specific
- Many disease-associated polymorphisms are in noncoding regions → affect epigenetic regulation
- Environmental influences significantly modify phenotypic expression

**Examples of multigenic inheritance:**
- Hair color, eye color, skin color, height, intelligence (quantitative trait loci/QTLs)
- Type 2 diabetes mellitus: Many features of complex multigenic disorder; weight gain unmasks diabetic genetic trait

**Diagnosis:**
- Familial clustering
- Exclusion of Mendelian and chromosomal modes of transmission
- Range of severity levels (but variable expressivity and reduced penetrance of single mutant genes may also account for this)

## Cytogenetic Disorders

Chromosomal abnormalities occur in approximately 1 in 200 newborn infants; much higher in fetuses that do not survive (50% of first-trimester spontaneous abortions).

Cytogenetic disorders result from alterations in number or structure of chromosomes; may affect autosomes or sex chromosomes.

### Karyotyping

**Karyotype**: Photographic representation of stained metaphase spread with chromosomes arranged in order of decreasing length.

**G banding technique**: Each chromosome set has distinctive pattern of alternating light and dark bands of variable widths.

Band notation: **2q34** = chromosome 2, long arm (q), region 3, band 4

### Numeric Abnormalities

**Normal human chromosome count**: 46 (2n = 46)

**Terms:**
- **Euploid**: Exact multiple of haploid number (n) — e.g., 3n, 4n (polyploidy — usually results in spontaneous abortion)
- **Aneuploid**: Any number that is not exact multiple of n

**Cause of aneuploidy**: **Nondisjunction**
- Failure of homologous pair to separate during first meiotic division, or
- Failure of sister chromatids to separate during second meiotic division
- Also: Failure of pairing followed by random assortment (anaphase lag)

**Results:**
- Gametes with extra chromosome (n + 1) or one less chromosome (n − 1)
- Fertilization → trisomic (2n + 1) or monosomic (2n − 1)
- Monosomy involving autosome: Incompatible with life
- Trisomies of certain autosomes and monosomy of sex chromosomes: Compatible with life

**Mosaicism**: Presence of two or more populations of cells with different chromosome complements in same individual (from postzygotic mitotic nondisjunction)

### Structural Abnormalities

Results from chromosomal breakage followed by loss or rearrangement of material.

**Designations:**
- **p**: Short arm of chromosome (French, "petit")
- **q**: Long arm

**Types of rearrangements:**

- **Translocation**: Transfer of part of one chromosome to another
  - Usually reciprocal (fragments exchanged)
  - Example: 46,XX,t(2;5)(q31;p14) — reciprocal translocation involving long arm of chromosome 2 and short arm of chromosome 5
  - **Balanced reciprocal translocation**: Full complement of genetic material; carrier not harmed but may produce unbalanced gametes
  - **Robertsonian translocation**: Centric fusion type involving two acrocentric chromosomes; breaks near centromere → one large chromosome, one very small (lost) chromosome; carrier has 45 chromosomes

- **Isochromosomes**: Centromere divides horizontally (not vertically)
  - One arm lost, remaining arm duplicated
  - Most common: i(Xq) — long arm of X chromosome

- **Deletion**: Loss of portion of chromosome
  - Single break: Terminal segment lost
  - Two interstitial breaks: Internal segment lost

- **Inversions**: Two interstitial breaks with segment reuniting after complete turnaround

- **Ring chromosome**: Loss of segments from each end; arms unite to form ring (variant of deletion)

### General Features of Chromosomal Disorders

- May be associated with absence (deletion, monosomy), excess (trisomy), or abnormal rearrangements (translocations)
- **Loss of chromosomal material produces more severe defects than gain**
- Excess chromosomal material: Complete chromosome (trisomy) or part of chromosome (Robertsonian translocation)
- **Sex chromosome imbalances are tolerated better than autosomal imbalances**
- Sex chromosomal disorders often produce subtle abnormalities (sometimes not detected at birth); infertility cannot be diagnosed until adolescence
- Most chromosomal disorders result from de novo changes (parents normal; low recurrence risk)
- Exception: Translocation form of Down syndrome

## Cytogenetic Disorders Involving Autosomes

**First identified:** Three autosomal trisomies (21, 18, 13) and cri du chat syndrome (partial deletion of short arm of chromosome 5)

**Most common:** Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) and 22q11.2 deletions

### Trisomy 21 (Down Syndrome)

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common chromosomal disorder
- 95% have trisomy 21 (47 chromosomes)
- Most common cause: Meiotic nondisjunction
- Parents of such children are normal
- **Maternal age has strong influence:**
  - 1 in 1550 live births (women < 20 years)
  - 1 in 25 live births (women > 45 years)
- 95% of cases: Extra chromosome of maternal origin

**Karyotypes:**
- **Trisomy 21 type**: 47,XX,+21 or 47,XY,+21 (95%)
- **Translocation type**: 46,XX,der(14;21)(q10;q10),+21 (4%) — often familial; inherited from parent carrier
- **Mosaic type**: 46,XX/47,XX,+21 (1%) — from mitotic nondisjunction during early embryogenesis; variable and milder manifestations

**Clinical Features:**
- Flat facial profile, oblique palpebral fissures, epicanthic folds
- Leading cause of severe mental retardation (approximately 80% have IQ of 25-50)
- Some mosaics have mild phenotypic changes and near-normal intelligence

**Associated Conditions:**
- **Congenital heart disease** (40%): Endocardial cushion defects, atrial septal defects, atrioventricular valve malformations, ventricular septal defects — majority of deaths in infancy/early childhood
- Atresias of esophagus and small bowel
- **10- to 20-fold increased risk of acute leukemia** (both ALL and AML)
- Virtually all patients > 40 years develop neuropathologic changes of Alzheimer disease
- Abnormal immune responses → serious infections, thyroid autoimmunity

**Longevity:**
- Improved medical care increased median age at death from 25 years (1983) to 60 years currently

**Molecular Basis:**
- Molecular basis remains elusive despite decades of known karyotype
- Studies of humans with partial trisomy 21 and mouse models identify critical region of chromosome 21
- Several gene clusters implicated: 16 genes in mitochondrial energy pathway, several influencing CNS development, one group involved in folate metabolism
- Several miRNA genes on chromosome 21 can shut down translation of genes mapping elsewhere

**Prenatal Diagnosis:**
- Cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood (approximately 5-10% of total)
- Next-generation sequencing determines gene dosage of chromosome 21-linked genes
- "Liquid biopsy" — sensitive and specific noninvasive prenatal diagnosis
- Confirmed by conventional cytogenetics on fetal cells from amniocentesis

### 22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome

**Spectrum of disorders** from small interstitial deletion of band 11 on long arm of chromosome 22.

**Clinical Features:**
- Congenital heart disease (outflow tracts)
- Palate abnormalities
- Facial dysmorphism
- Developmental delay
- Thymic hypoplasia → impaired T cell immunity
- Parathyroid hypoplasia → hypocalcemia

**Previously Recognized as Two Disorders:**
- **DiGeorge syndrome**: T cell immunodeficiency and hypocalcemia dominant
- **Velocardiofacial syndrome**: Mild immunodeficiency, pronounced dysmorphology and cardiac defects

**Additional Risk:**
- High risk for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (~25% of adults with syndrome)
- 2-3% of individuals with childhood-onset schizophrenia have deletions

**Molecular Basis:**
- Affected region encodes many genes
- **TBX1** (transcription factor gene) suspected as responsible candidate — loss correlates with DiGeorge syndrome
- Other genes contribute to behavioral and psychiatric disorders

**Diagnosis:**
- Clinical suspicion → confirmed by detection of deletion (typically by FISH)

### Summary: Cytogenetic Disorders Involving Autosomes

**Down Syndrome:**
- Extra copy of genes on chromosome 21 (most commonly trisomy 21)
- Severe mental retardation, flat facial profile, epicanthic folds, cardiac malformations, higher risk of leukemia and infections, premature Alzheimer disease

**22q11.2 Deletion Syndrome:**
- Deletion of genes at chromosomal locus 22q11.2
- Results in malformations affecting face, heart, thymus, parathyroids
- DiGeorge syndrome: Thymic hypoplasia with diminished T-cell immunity; parathyroid hypoplasia with hypocalcemia
- Velocardiofacial syndrome: Congenital heart disease (outflow tracts), facial dysmorphism, developmental delay

## Cytogenetic Disorders Involving Sex Chromosomes

Several abnormal karyotypes involving sex chromosomes are compatible with life (45,X to 49,XXXXY).

**Tolerance factors:**
1. X inactivation (Lyon hypothesis) — only one X chromosome genetically active in females
2. Small amount of genetic information on Y chromosome

**Lyon Hypothesis:**
- Mary Lyon (1962): In females, only one X chromosome is genetically active
- X inactivation occurs early in fetal life (about 16 days after conception)
- Either paternal or maternal X randomly inactivated in each cell
- Once inactivated, same X remains inactive in all progeny
- All but one X is inactivated
- Explains why normal females do not have double dose of X-encoded phenotypic attributes
- Normal females are mosaics (two cell populations: one with active maternal X, other with active paternal X)

**Molecular Basis:**
- XIST gene encodes long non-coding RNA
- This RNA "coats" X chromosome it is transcribed from and silences genes
- Other XIST allele is switched off in active X

**Modified Lyon Hypothesis:**
- Not all genes on inactive X are "switched off"
- Approximately 21% of genes on Xp and 3% on Xq escape X inactivation
- Has implications for monosomic X chromosome disorders (Turner syndrome)

**Y Chromosome:**
- Extra Y chromosomes readily tolerated
- Only information relates to male differentiation
- **SRY** (sex-determining region of Y chromosome) located on short arm of Y
- Presence of Y invariably dictates male phenotype

### Klinefelter Syndrome

**Definition:** Male hypogonadism with at least two X chromosomes and one or more Y chromosomes.

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common cause of hypogonadism in males
- Most patients: 47,XXY karyotype
- Results from nondisjunction of sex chromosomes during meiosis
- Extra X may be maternal or paternal in origin
- Advanced maternal age and irradiation may contribute
- Approximately 15% show mosaic patterns (46,XY/47,XXY; 47,XXY/48,XXXY)
- Presence of 46,XY line in mosaics usually associated with milder condition

**Clinical Features:**
- Wide range of manifestations
- May be expressed only as hypogonadism
- **Distinctive body habitus**: Increased length between soles and pubic bone → elongated body appearance
- **Eunuchoid body habitus**
- Reduced facial, body, and pubic hair
- Gynecomastia frequently seen
- **Testes markedly reduced in size** (sometimes only 2 cm)
- Lower serum testosterone levels; elevated urinary gonadotropins

**Fertility:**
- Only rarely fertile (presumably mosaics with large proportion of 46,XY cells)
- Sterility from impaired spermatogenesis (sometimes total azoospermia)
- Histology: Hyalinization of tubules ("ghostlike" structures); Leydig cells prominent

**Neurologic:**
- May be associated with mental retardation (typically mild)
- Reduction in intelligence correlated with number of extra X chromosomes

**Associated Disorders:**
- Breast cancer (20 times more common than in normal males)
- Extragonadal germ-cell tumors
- Autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus)

### Turner Syndrome

**Definition:** Primary hypogonadism in phenotypic females from partial or complete monosomy of short arm of X chromosome.

**Karyotypes:**
- **45,X** (57%): Entire X chromosome missing — most severely affected; diagnosis often at birth or early childhood
- Mosaics or structural abnormalities of X chromosome (43%)

**Clinical Features (45,X Turner Syndrome):**
- Growth retardation → abnormally short stature (below third percentile)
- Swelling of nape of neck in infancy (distended lymphatic channels) → webbing of neck in older children
- Low posterior hairline
- **Cubitus valgus** (increased carrying angle of arms)
- Shieldlike chest with widely spaced nipples
- High-arched palate
- Lymphedema of hands and feet
- Congenital malformations: Horseshoe kidney, bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation of aorta
- Cardiovascular abnormalities: Most common cause of death in childhood

**Adolescence:**
- Fail to develop normal secondary sex characteristics
- Genitalia remain infantile; minimal breast development; little pubic hair
- Primary amenorrhea
- Ovaries transformed into white streaks of fibrous stroma devoid of follicles (**streak ovaries**)
- Mental status usually normal but subtle defects in visual-spatial information processing
- Hypothyroidism caused by autoantibodies (especially in women with isochromosome Xp) — up to 50% develop clinical hypothyroidism

**Molecular Pathogenesis:**
- Both X chromosomes active during oogenesis (essential for normal ovarian development)
- Fetal ovaries contain up to 7 million oocytes → dwindled to 400,000 by menarche → fewer than 10,000 at menopause
- In Turner syndrome: Absence of second X chromosome → accelerated oocyte loss → complete by age 2 years
- "Menopause occurs before menarche" → streak ovaries
- Other (nongonadal) abnormalities indicate genes required for normal growth and development also on X chromosome

**SHOX Gene:**
- **Short stature homeobox (SHOX) gene** at Xp22.33
- One of genes that remain active on both X chromosomes
- Has active homologue on short arm of Y chromosome
- Both normal males and females have two active copies
- Loss of one copy of SHOX → short stature
- Deletions of SHOX gene noted in 2-5% of otherwise normal children with short stature
- Loss of SHOX explains growth deficit but not cardiac malformations and endocrine abnormalities

**Variability:**
- Isochrome Xq: Most common structural abnormality → partial monosomy of X chromosome
- Mosaics or deletion variants may have almost normal appearance; may present only with primary amenorrhea

### Summary: Cytogenetic Disorders Involving Sex Chromosomes

**Klinefelter Syndrome:**
- Two or more X chromosomes with one Y chromosome (nondisjunction)
- Testicular atrophy, sterility, reduced body hair, gynecomastia, eunuchoid body habitus
- Most common cause of male sterility

**Turner Syndrome:**
- Partial or complete monosomy of short arm of X chromosome (most commonly 45,X)
- Also mosaicism or deletions involving short arm of X chromosome
- Short stature, webbing of neck, cubitus valgus, cardiovascular malformations, amenorrhea, lack of secondary sex characteristics, fibrotic ovaries

## Single-Gene Disorders With Atypical Patterns of Inheritance

Three groups of diseases resulting from mutations affecting single genes that do not follow Mendelian rules:

1. **Diseases caused by triplet repeat mutations**
2. **Diseases caused by mutations in mitochondrial genes**
3. **Diseases associated with alteration of imprinted regions of the genome**

### Triplet Repeat Mutations

#### Fragile X Syndrome

**Prototype** of diseases where causative mutation occurs in a long repeating sequence of three nucleotides.

**Gene:** FMR1 gene at Xq27.3

**Epidemiology:**
- Frequency: 1 in 1550 males; 1 in 8000 females
- Second most common genetic cause of mental retardation (after Down syndrome)

**Clinical Features:**
- Moderate to severe mental retardation in males (highly variable)
- Some children initially diagnosed with autism-like symptoms
- **Physical phenotype**: Long face with large mandible, large everted ears, large testicles (**macroorchidism**) — postpubertal males
- Macroorchidism detectable in at least 90% of postpubertal males

**Inheritance Patterns (Unusual):**
- **Carrier males**: Approximately 20% of males who carry fragile X mutation do not manifest typical symptoms in childhood ("transmitting males") — harbor detectable molecular abnormality at FMR1 locus but not full-fledged mutation
- **Affected females**: 30-50% of carrier women show features of mild cognitive impairment or behavioral disturbances; 20% develop premature reproductive failure — unusual for X-linked recessive disease
- **Anticipation**: Clinical features worsen with each successive generation

**Molecular Basis:**
- Normal FMR1 gene contains CGG repeats in 5' untranslated region
- Normal population: Average around 29 repeats
- Affected persons: 200 to 4000 repeats ("**full mutations**")
- **Premutations**: 52 to 200 CGG repeats (carrier males and females)
- Full mutations arise through premutation stage
- During oogenesis (but not spermatogenesis), premutations convert to full mutations by further amplification
- When trinucleotide repeats exceed approximately 230: DNA of entire 5' region becomes abnormally methylated → methylation extends to promoter region → transcriptional suppression of FMR1
- Resulting absence of **FMRP** (familial mental retardation protein) causes phenotypic changes
- FMRP is RNA-binding protein transported from cytoplasm to nucleus → binds specific mRNAs → transports to axons and dendrites
- FMRP-mRNA complexes regulate translation of specific mRNAs involved in synaptic functions
- Absence of this "shuttle" function underlies fragile X syndrome

**Fragile X Tremor/Ataxia:**
- **CGG premutations can cause disease through distinct mechanism** (toxic gain-of-function)
- **20% of carrier females**: Mild cognitive impairment and premature ovarian failure (before age 40)
- **>50% of premutation-carrying males**: Progressive neurodegenerative syndrome starting in sixth decade
- **Syndrome**: Intention tremors and cerebellar ataxia → may progress to parkinsonism
- **Mechanism**: FMR1 gene continues to be transcribed; CGG-containing mRNAs are "toxic" → accumulate in nucleus → form intranuclear inclusions → recruit RNA-binding proteins → sequestration at abnormal locations → toxic effects

**Other Trinucleotide Repeat Diseases:**
- Huntington disease
- Myotonic dystrophy
- Friedreich ataxia

**General Principles:**
- In all cases, gene functions altered by expansion of repeats; threshold for premutation-to-full mutation conversion differs with each disorder
- Expansion may occur during oogenesis (fragile X) or spermatogenesis (Huntington disease)
- May affect untranslated regions (loss of function) or coding regions (misfolded proteins)
- **Polyglutamine diseases**: CAG repeats encode polyglutamine tracts → "toxic gain-of-function" mutations → neurodegenerative

### Summary: Fragile X Syndrome and Fragile X Tremor/Ataxia

**Fragile X Syndrome:**
- Pathologic amplification of trinucleotide repeats causes loss-of-function (fragile X) or gain-of-function mutations (Huntington)
- Results from loss of FMR1 gene function → mental retardation, macroorchidism, abnormal facial features
- Normal population: ~29 CGG repeats; carriers: 52-200 repeats (premutations); full mutations: up to 4000 repeats
- Premutations expand to full mutations during oogenesis

**Fragile X Tremor/Ataxia:**
- Results from expression of FMR1 gene bearing premutation
- Accumulation of corresponding mRNA in nucleus binds and sequesters essential neuronal proteins

### Diseases Caused by Mutations in Mitochondrial Genes

Mitochondria contain genes encoding enzymes involved in oxidative phosphorylation.

**Inheritance:** **Maternal inheritance** — ova contain mitochondria in cytoplasm; sperm contain few/no mitochondria; zygote's mitochondrial DNA derived entirely from ovum

**Affected organs:** Those most dependent on oxidative phosphorylation — CNS, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, liver, kidney

**Prototype:** **Leber hereditary optic neuropathy** — progressive bilateral loss of central vision → blindness

### Diseases Caused by Alterations of Imprinted Regions: Prader-Willi and Angelman Syndromes

**Genomic imprinting**: Epigenetic process whereby certain homologous genes are differentially "inactivated" during paternal and maternal gametogenesis.
- **Maternal imprinting**: Transcriptional silencing of maternal allele
- **Paternal imprinting**: Paternal allele inactivated
- Associated with methylation of gene promoter and modification of DNA-binding histone proteins

#### Prader-Willi Syndrome

**Clinical Features:** Mental retardation, short stature, hypotonia, obesity, small hands and feet, hypogonadism

**Genetic Basis:**
- 60-75% of cases: Interstitial deletion of band q12 in long arm of chromosome 15 — del(15)(q11;q13)
- In all cases, deletion affects **paternally derived** chromosome 15

#### Angelman Syndrome

**Clinical Features:** Mental retardation, ataxic gait, seizures, inappropriate laughter ("happy puppet syndrome")

**Genetic Basis:**
- Deletion of same chromosomal region (15q12) but derived from **mother**

#### Molecular Basis

**Imprinting context:**
- Set of genes on maternal chromosome at 15q12 is imprinted (silenced) → paternal chromosome provides only functional alleles
- Deletion of paternal genes → **Prader-Willi syndrome**
- Most likely culprit: Gene cluster encoding multiple distinct small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) involved in mRNA processing

**For Angelman syndrome:**
- **UBE3A** gene (also maps to same region) is imprinted on paternal chromosome
- Encodes ubiquitin ligase → targets cellular proteins for proteasomal degradation
- Only maternally derived allele normally active
- Deletion of maternal gene → **Angelman syndrome**
- Neurologic manifestations from lack of UBE3A expression in specific brain regions

**Uniparental Disomy:**
- Both structurally normal copies of chromosome 15 derived from mother → Prader-Willi syndrome
- **Uniparental disomy**: Inheritance of both chromosomes of a pair from one parent
- Angelman syndrome can also result from uniparental disomy of paternal chromosome 15

### Summary: Genomic Imprinting

- Imprinting involves transcriptional silencing of paternal or maternal copies of certain genes during gametogenesis
- Only one functional copy exists; loss of functional allele by deletions gives rise to diseases
- **Prader-Willi syndrome**: Deletion of paternal chromosomal region 15q12 → mental retardation, short stature, hypotonia, obesity, hypogonadism
- **Angelman syndrome**: Deletion of maternal chromosomal region 15q12 → mental retardation, ataxia, seizures, inappropriate laughter

## Congenital Anomalies

### Definition and Epidemiology

**Congenital anomalies**: Structural defects present at birth (some may not become clinically apparent for years — e.g., cardiac defects, renal anomalies).

**Incidence:** Approximately 1 in 33 births in the United States (approximately 120,000 babies per year with birth defect).

Important cause of infant mortality and source of illness, disability, and death throughout early years of life.

### Terminology

**Malformations:**
- Primary errors of morphogenesis
- Intrinsically abnormal developmental process
- Usually multifactorial (not single gene or chromosomal defect)
- May affect single body systems (e.g., congenital heart diseases) or multiple malformations involving many organs/tissues

**Disruptions:**
- Secondary destruction of previously normal organ or body region
- **Extrinsic** disturbance in morphogenesis
- Example: **Amniotic bands** from rupture of amnion → "bands" encircle, compress, or attach to developing fetus
- Not heritable; no recurrence risk

**Deformations:**
- Extrinsic disturbance of development (not intrinsic error)
- Common (approximately 2% of newborn infants)
- Caused by localized or generalized compression of growing fetus by abnormal biomechanical forces
- Most common cause: **Uterine constraint**
- Between weeks 35-38: Rapid fetal growth outpaces uterine growth; amniotic fluid decreases
- Variables increasing excessive compression: Maternal (first pregnancy, small uterus, bicornuate uterus, leiomyomas); fetal (multiple gestations, oligohydramnios, abnormal fetal presentation)

**Sequence:**
- Multiple congenital anomalies resulting from secondary effects of single localized aberration in organogenesis
- Initiating event may be malformation, deformation, or disruption
- Example: **Oligohydramnios (Potter) sequence**
  - Oligohydramnios from various causes (amniotic fluid leakage, uteroplacental insufficiency, fetal renal agenesis)
  - Fetal compression → classic phenotype: flattened faces, positional abnormalities of hands and feet, hip dislocation, pulmonary hypoplasia

**Malformation Syndrome:**
- Presence of several defects that cannot be explained by single localizing initiating error in morphogenesis
- Usually arises from single causative condition (e.g., viral infection, chromosomal abnormality) simultaneously affecting several tissues

**Organ-Specific Terms:**
- **Agenesis**: Complete absence of organ or anlage
- **Aplasia**: Incomplete development
- **Hypoplasia**: Underdevelopment
- **Atresia**: Absence of opening (usually of hollow visceral organ or duct — intestines, bile ducts)

### Causes of Congenital Malformations

Three major categories:

**1. Genetic:**
- All previously discussed mechanisms of genetic disease
- Virtually all chromosomal syndromes associated with congenital malformations (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome)
- Most chromosomal disorders arise during gametogenesis (not familial)
- Single-gene mutations may underlie major malformations (e.g., holoprosencephaly — Hedgehog signaling pathway mutations)

**2. Environmental:**
- Viral infections, drugs, radiation to which mother exposed during pregnancy
- "Malformation" used loosely here (technically, these are disruptions)

*Viral infections (TORCH):*
- Rubella: Major scourge; virtually eliminated in developed countries due to vaccination
- Zika virus: Severe malformations of CNS (microcephaly, brain damage)

*Drugs and chemicals:*
- **Thalidomide**: 50-80% incidence of limb malformations; used as tranquilizer in Europe; currently used for certain cancers
- **Alcohol**: Fetal alcohol syndrome — prenatal/postnatal growth retardation, facial anomalies (microcephaly, short palpebral fissures, maxillary hypoplasia), psychomotor disturbances
- **Anticonvulsants** (e.g., phenytoin): Teratogenic
- **Warfarin**: Oral anticoagulant
- **13-cis-retinoic acid**: Used for severe acne; predictable phenotype (CNS, cardiac, craniofacial defects, cleft lip/palate)
- Nicotine: Not convincingly demonstrated as teratogen, but associated with spontaneous abortions, premature labor, placental abnormalities, low birth weight, SIDS

*Maternal conditions:*
- **Diabetes mellitus**: Major malformations in 6-10% of infants
  - Maternal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia → fetal macrosomia
  - Cardiac anomalies, neural tube defects, CNS malformations
- **Phenylketonuria**: Maternal PKU causes fetal anomalies

**3. Multifactorial:**
- Most common genetic cause of congenital malformations
- Interaction of environmental influences with two or more genes of small effect
- Includes cleft lip and palate, neural tube defects
- Periconceptional folic acid intake dramatically reduces neural tube defect incidence

**Unknown:** Cause not identified for almost half of reported cases

### Pathogenesis of Congenital Anomalies

**Principle 1: Timing of teratogenic insult is critical**

**Embryonic period** (first 9 weeks):
- First 3 weeks: Agent damages enough cells to cause death/abortion, or only few cells → recovery without defects
- **Weeks 3-9**: Embryo extremely susceptible to teratogenesis; peak sensitivity weeks 4-5
- During this period, organs being crafted from germ cell layers

**Fetal period** (9 weeks to birth):
- Further growth and maturation of organs
- Greatly reduced susceptibility to teratogenic agents
- Susceptible to growth retardation or injury to already formed organs
- Same agent can produce different anomalies if exposure occurs at different gestational times

**Principle 2: Interplay between environmental teratogens and intrinsic genetic defects**

Environmental insults often recapitulate features caused by genetic defects in targeted pathways:

- **Cyclopamine** (plant teratogen): Inhibits Hedgehog signaling → sheep give birth to lambs with holoprosencephaly and cyclopia; Hedgehog gene mutations found in fetuses with holoprosencephaly
- **Valproic acid** (anticonvulsant): Disrupts homeobox (HOX) protein expression; HOX mutations cause congenital anomalies mimicking valproic acid embryopathy
- **All-trans-retinoic acid**: Essential for normal development; absence → malformations of eyes, genitourinary system, cardiovascular system, diaphragm, lungs; excess also teratogenic (retinoic acid embryopathy); disrupts TGF-β signaling pathway involved in palatogenesis; TGFB3 knockout mice uniformly develop cleft palate

### Summary: Congenital Anomalies

- Result from intrinsic abnormalities (malformations) and extrinsic disturbances (deformations, disruptions)
- Can result from genetic (chromosomal abnormalities, gene mutations), environmental (infections, drugs, alcohol), and multifactorial causes
- Timing of in utero insult has profound influence on extent; earlier events usually have greater impact
- Teratogens often target signaling pathways in which mutations have been reported as cause for same anomalies

## Perinatal Infections

Infections of fetus and neonate acquired **transcervically** (ascending infections) or **transplacentally** (hematologic infections).

### Transcervical (Ascending) Infections

- Spread of microbes from cervicovaginal canal
- Acquired in utero or during birth
- Most bacterial infections (e.g., α-hemolytic streptococcal infection) and some viral infections (e.g., herpes simplex)
- Fetus acquires infection by "inhaling" infected amniotic fluid into lungs or passing through infected birth canal
- Associated with inflammation of placental membranes (chorioamnionitis) and inflammation of umbilical cord (funisitis)
- Typical for pneumonia, sepsis, meningitis

### Transplacental Infections

- Gain access to fetal bloodstream by crossing placenta via chorionic villi
- Can occur at any time during gestation; occasionally at delivery (hepatitis B, HIV)
- Most parasitic (toxoplasma, malaria) and viral infections; few bacterial (Listeria, Treponema)

**TORCH Complex:**
- **T**oxoplasma
- **R**ubella virus
- **C**ytomegalovirus
- **H**erpesvirus
- **O**ther (e.g., Treponema pallidum)

**TORCH infections early in gestation:** Chronic sequelae — growth restriction, mental retardation, cataracts, congenital cardiac anomalies

**TORCH infections later in pregnancy:** Primarily tissue injury with inflammation — encephalitis, chorioretinitis, hepatosplenomegaly, pneumonia, myocarditis

**Zika virus:** Transmitted by pregnant females to offspring; devastating consequences — microcephaly, brain damage

## Prematurity and Fetal Growth Restriction

### Prematurity

**Definition:** Gestational age less than 37 weeks

**Significance:** Second most common cause of neonatal mortality (second only to congenital anomalies); infants born before completion of gestation weigh less than normal (<2500 gm)

**Major Risk Factors:**
- Preterm premature rupture of membranes
- Intrauterine infection → inflammation of placental membranes (chorioamnionitis)
- Structural abnormalities of uterus, cervix, and placenta
- Multiple gestation (e.g., twin pregnancy)

**Complications of Prematurity:**
- **Respiratory distress syndrome** (hyaline membrane disease)
- Necrotizing enterocolitis
- Sepsis
- Intraventricular and germinal matrix hemorrhage
- Long-term sequelae: developmental delay

### Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR)

**Small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants:** As many as one-third of infants <2500 gm are born at term — undergrown rather than immature

**Causes:**

**Fetal abnormalities:**
- Conditions intrinsically reducing growth potential despite adequate nutrients
- Chromosomal disorders, congenital anomalies, congenital infections
- Chromosomal abnormalities detected in 17% of fetuses evaluated for FGR; up to 66% of fetuses with ultrasonographic malformations
- TORCH infections common cause
- FGR is **symmetric** (affects all organ systems equally)

**Placental abnormalities:**
- Any factor compromising uteroplacental blood supply (placenta previa, placental abruption, placental infarction)
- FGR is **asymmetric** (brain spared relative to visceral organs such as liver)

**Maternal factors:**
- Most common causes of growth deficit in SGA infants
- Vascular diseases: Preeclampsia ("toxemia of pregnancy"), chronic hypertension
- Hypercoagulability (thrombophilias)
- Avoidable influences: Maternal narcotic abuse, alcohol intake, heavy cigarette smoking
- Drugs: Teratogens (phenytoin) and nonteratogenic agents
- Maternal malnutrition (prolonged hypoglycemia)

**Long-term consequences:** Cerebral dysfunction, learning disabilities, sensory (visual and hearing) impairment

## Respiratory Distress Syndrome of the Newborn

### Definition and Epidemiology

**Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)**: Also called **hyaline membrane disease**.

**Incidence:** Estimated 24,000 cases annually in the United States; deaths reduced from 5000 per year to fewer than 900 currently.

**Primary disorder of premature infants.**

Less common causes: Excessive sedation of mother, fetal head injury during delivery, aspiration of blood or amniotic fluid, intrauterine hypoxia from umbilical cord compression.

### Pathogenesis

**Incidence by gestational age:**
- 60% at <28 weeks
- 30% at 28-34 weeks
- <5% after 34 weeks

**Associations:** Male gender, maternal diabetes, delivery by cesarean section

**Fundamental defect:** Inability of immature lung to synthesize sufficient **surfactant**.

**Surfactant:** Complex of surface-active phospholipids (principally dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine/lecithin) and surfactant-associated proteins.

Surfactant deficiency → alveoli tend to collapse → greater inspiratory effort required → infant tires → generalized atelectasis → hypoxia → epithelial and endothelial damage → hyaline membrane formation.

**Regulation of surfactant synthesis:**
- Corticosteroids stimulate formation of surfactant lipids and associated proteins
- Intrauterine stress and fetal growth restriction increase corticosteroid release → lower risk of RDS
- Infants of diabetic mothers have high insulin levels → suppress surfactant synthesis → higher risk of RDS
- Labor increases surfactant synthesis; cesarean section before onset of labor associated with increased risk

### Morphology

- Lungs of normal size but heavy and relatively airless
- Mottled purple color
- Microscopic: Tissue appears solid with poorly developed, collapsed (atelectatic) alveoli
- Early: Necrotic cellular debris in terminal bronchioles and alveolar ducts
- Later: Characteristic **eosinophilic hyaline membranes** line respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and random alveoli — contain necrotic epithelial cells admixed with extravasated plasma proteins
- Remarkable paucity of neutrophilic inflammatory reaction
- Lesions never seen in stillborn infants or those dying within few hours of birth
- After several days: Reparative changes (proliferation of type II pneumocytes, interstitial fibrosis)

### Clinical Features

**Assessment of fetal lung maturity:** Analysis of amniotic fluid phospholipids (pulmonary secretions discharged into amniotic fluid).

**Prevention and treatment:**
- Delaying labor until fetal lung reaches maturity
- Inducing maturation of lung in at-risk fetus
- **Prophylactic administration of exogenous surfactant** to extremely premature infants (born <28 weeks) — very beneficial
- Antenatal glucocorticoid therapy

**Complications of oxygen therapy:**
- **Retinopathy of prematurity** (retrolental fibroplasia):
  - Phase I (hyperoxic): Decreased VEGF expression → endothelial cell apoptosis
  - Phase II (return to hypoxic room air): VEGF rebound → retinal vessel proliferation (neovascularization)
- **Bronchopulmonary dysplasia**:
  - Decreased alveolar septation (large, simplified alveolar structures)
  - Dysmorphic capillary configuration
  - Multiple factors: hyperoxemia, hyperventilation, prematurity, inflammatory cytokines, vascular maldevelopment

**Other complications:** Patent ductus arteriosus, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis

### Summary: Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome

- RDS is a disease of prematurity; most cases in neonates born <28 weeks' gestational age
- Fundamental abnormality: Insufficient pulmonary surfactant → failure of lungs to inflate after birth
- Characteristic morphologic pattern: Hyaline membranes (necrotic epithelial cells and plasma proteins) lining airways
- Ameliorated by prophylactic steroids, surfactant therapy, improved ventilation techniques
- Long-term sequelae: Retinopathy of prematurity and bronchopulmonary dysplasia; incidence decreased with improved management

## Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC)

**Epidemiology:** Most common in premature infants; incidence inversely proportional to gestational age; occurs in approximately 1 in 10 very-low-birth-weight infants (<1500 gm).

**Pathogenesis:**
- Associated with enteral feeding; suggests postnatal insult (e.g., introduction of bacteria) sets in motion cascade
- Infectious agents likely play a role; no single bacterial pathogen linked
- Many inflammatory mediators associated; **platelet-activating factor** implicated → increases mucosal permeability, promotes enterocyte apoptosis, compromises tight junctions

**Morphology:**
- Typically involves terminal ileum, cecum, and right colon (any part may be involved)
- Involved segment: Distended, friable, congested → frankly gangrenous; intestinal perforation with peritonitis possible
- Microscopic: Mucosal or transmural coagulative necrosis, ulceration, bacterial colonization, submucosal gas bubbles (pneumatosis intestinalis)
- Reparative changes: Granulation tissue, fibrosis

**Clinical Course:**
- Bloody stools, abdominal distention, circulatory collapse
- Abdominal X-rays: Gas within intestinal wall (pneumatosis intestinalis)
- Treatment: Often conservative, but 20-60% require operative intervention (resection of necrotic bowel segments)
- Associated with high perinatal mortality
- Survivors often develop post-NEC strictures from fibrosis

## Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)

### Definition

According to National Institute of Child Health and Human Development:

> "The sudden death of an infant under 1 year of age which remains unexplained after a thorough case investigation, including performance of a complete autopsy, examination of the death scene, and review of the clinical history."

**Key points:**
- Many cases of sudden death in infancy found to have anatomic or biochemical basis at autopsy → should be labeled **sudden unexpected infant death (SUID)**, not SIDS
- SIDS accounts for approximately half of SUID cases in the United States
- Infant usually dies while asleep (lay terms: "crib death," "cot death")
- Leading cause of death between 1 month and 1 year of age in U.S.; third leading cause overall in this age group
- 90% of cases: Infant younger than 6 months; most between 2 and 4 months
- SIDS in earlier sibling: Fivefold relative risk of recurrence

### Pathogenesis

**Multifactorial condition** with mixture of contributing causes.

**Three interacting variables:**
1. A vulnerable infant
2. A critical developmental period in homeostatic control
3. One or more exogenous stressors

**Vulnerable infant factors:**
- Brain stem abnormalities associated with delayed development of arousal and cardiorespiratory control
- Most compelling hypothesis: SIDS reflects delayed development of arousal reflexes and cardiorespiratory control
- Brain stem (particularly medulla oblongata) plays critical role in "arousal" response to noxious stimuli (episodic hypercarbia, hypoxia, thermal stress during sleep)
- **Serotonergic (5-HT) system** of medulla implicated in arousal responses and regulation of homeostatic functions (respiratory drive, blood pressure, upper airway reflexes)
- Abnormalities in serotonin-dependent signaling in brain stem may underlie SIDS

**Environmental risk factors:**
- **Prone sleeping position** — most important modifiable risk factor
- Sleeping on soft surfaces
- Thermal stress
- Prone position increases vulnerability to noxious stimuli during sleep and decreases arousal responsiveness
- "Back to Sleep" campaign (1994) → substantial decreases in SIDS-related deaths

**Important:** SIDS is diagnosis of exclusion → requires careful death scene examination and complete postmortem examination.

Postmortem may show alternate cause in up to 20% of presumed SIDS cases:
- **Infections**: Viral myocarditis, bronchopneumonia — most common causes of SUID
- **Congenital anomaly**: Congenital aortic stenosis, anomalous origin of left coronary artery from pulmonary artery
- **Traumatic child abuse**: Intentional suffocation (filicide)
- **Genetic/metabolic defects**:
  - Long QT syndrome (SCN5A and KCNQ1 mutations)
  - Fatty acid oxidation disorders (MCAD, LCHAD, SCHAD mutations) — up to 5% of sudden deaths in infancy
  - Histiocytoid cardiomyopathy (MTCYB mutations)
  - Abnormal inflammatory responsiveness (partial deletions in C4a and C4b)

### Morphology

- Multiple petechiae: Most common finding (~80% of typical SIDS autopsies) — on thymus, visceral and parietal pleura, epicardium
- Lungs: Congested; vascular engorgement with or without pulmonary edema
- Brain stem: Quantitative abnormalities (hypoplasia of arcuate nucleus, subtle decrease in neuronal populations) — not uniform; not feasible in routine autopsies

### Summary: Sudden Infant Death Syndrome

- Disorder of unknown cause; sudden death of infant <1 year that remains unexplained after thorough investigation including autopsy
- Most deaths occur between 2 and 4 months of age
- Most likely basis: Delayed development of arousal reflexes and cardiorespiratory control
- Prone sleeping position is best-recognized environmental risk factor → success of "Back to Sleep" program
- SIDS is diagnosis of exclusion; many cases have alternate cause found at autopsy

## Fetal Hydrops

**Definition:** Accumulation of edema fluid in the fetus during intrauterine growth.

**Causes:** Manifold; most important listed below.

### Immune Hydrops

**Cause:** Antibody-induced hemolytic disease from blood group incompatibility between mother and fetus.

**Mechanism:**
- Fetus inherits red cell antigenic determinants from father foreign to mother
- Most common antigens: **Rh** and **ABO** blood group antigens
- **Rh D antigen** is major cause of Rh incompatibility
- Fetal red cells reach maternal circulation during last trimester or during childbirth (fetomaternal bleed)
- Mother becomes sensitized → produces antibodies → in future pregnancies, antibodies cross placenta → cause red cell destruction → progressive anemia → tissue ischemia → intrauterine cardiac failure → peripheral pooling of fluid (edema)

**Factors influencing immune response:**
- **Concurrent ABO incompatibility protects** mother against Rh immunization (fetal red cells promptly coated by isohemagglutinins and removed)
- Antibody response depends on dose of immunizing antigen (hemolytic disease only when >1 mL of Rh-positive red cells enter)
- **Isotype important**: IgG (not IgM) antibodies cross placenta
- Initial exposure → IgM response → Rh disease very uncommon with first pregnancy
- Subsequent exposures → brisk IgG response

**Prevention:**
- Rh-negative mothers given **Rh immune globulin (RhIg)** at 28 weeks and within 72 hours after delivery of Rh-positive baby
- RhIg masks antigenic sites on fetal red cells that entered maternal circulation → prevents sensitization

**ABO Incompatibility:**
- Most common cause of immune hemolytic disease of newborn (since prevention of Rh)
- Occurs in 20-25% of pregnancies but hemolysis in only small fraction
- Disease much milder than Rh incompatibility
- Occurs almost exclusively in infants of blood group A or B born to group O mothers
- Group O mothers have IgG antibodies against A or B antigens (even without previous sensitization) → firstborn may be affected
- No effective prevention method

### Nonimmune Hydrops

**Major causes:**

**Cardiovascular:**
- Structural cardiovascular defects and functional abnormalities (arrhythmias) → intrauterine cardiac failure → hydrops

**Chromosomal:**
- 45,X karyotype (Turner syndrome), trisomies 21 and 18
- Usually from underlying structural cardiac anomalies
- Turner syndrome: Abnormal lymphatic drainage from neck → cystic hygromas

**Fetal anemia:**
- Other than Rh/ABO incompatibility
- **Homozygous α-thalassemia** — most common cause in parts of Southeast Asia
- **Parvovirus B19 infection** — increasingly recognized cause
  - Virus enters erythroid precursors (normoblasts) → replicates → cellular injury → death of normoblasts → aplastic anemia
  - Parvoviral intranuclear inclusions seen in circulating and marrow erythroid precursors

**Mechanism of hydrops (immune and nonimmune):**
- Tissue ischemia → secondary myocardial dysfunction → circulatory failure
- Secondary liver failure → loss of synthetic function → hypoalbuminemia → reduced plasma osmotic pressure → edema

### Morphology

- Varies with severity and etiology
- **Hydrops fetalis**: Most severe, generalized manifestation
- Lesser degrees: Isolated pleural, peritoneal, or postnuchal fluid collections (cystic hygromas)
- Infants may be stillborn, die within first few days, or recover completely
- Dysmorphic features suggest underlying chromosomal abnormalities
- **Fetal anemia**: Fetus and placenta characteristically pale; liver and spleen enlarged (cardiac failure and congestion)
- Bone marrow: Compensatory hyperplasia of erythroid precursors (exception: parvovirus-associated aplastic anemia)
- Extramedullary hematopoiesis in liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, lymph nodes, heart
- **Kernicterus**: Basal ganglia and brain stem prone to bilirubin pigment deposition when hyperbilirubinemia marked

### Clinical Course

- Early recognition imperative (severe cases sometimes salvageable with timely therapy)
- Immune hydrops from Rh incompatibility: Severity correlates with rapidly rising Rh antibody titers
- Antenatal identification: Amniocentesis, chorionic villus and fetal blood sampling
- Direct anti-globulin test (Coombs test) used for diagnosis

## Tumors and Tumorlike Lesions of Infancy and Childhood

### Overview

**Common sites of malignant neoplasms:**
- Hematopoietic system
- Neural tissue
- Soft tissues

This distribution contrasts sharply with adults (epithelial tumors of lung, colon, prostate most common).

**Key differences from adult tumors:**
1. Close relationship between abnormal development (teratogenesis) and tumor induction (oncogenesis) — suggests common stem cell defect
2. Prevalence of genetic abnormalities or familial syndromes predisposing to cancer
3. Tendency of fetal and neonatal malignancies to regress spontaneously or undergo "differentiation" into mature elements
4. Better survival/cure of many childhood tumors → attention to minimizing adverse delayed effects of therapy (including second malignancies)

**Histology:**
- Many childhood tumors are histologically unique
- Tend to exhibit primitive (embryonal) rather than pleomorphic-anaplastic appearance
- Often exhibit features of organogenesis specific to site of origin
- Collectively called **small, round, blue-cell tumors**: Neuroblastoma, lymphoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, some Wilms tumors

### Benign Neoplasms

#### Hemangiomas

- **Most common neoplasms of infancy**
- Capillary and cavernous types; capillary often more cellular than in adults
- Most located in skin (face, scalp); produce flat to elevated, red-blue masses
- Flat, larger lesions: **Port-wine stains**
- May enlarge as child ages; many spontaneously regress
- Most have only cosmetic significance
- May be manifestation of hereditary disorder with internal organ involvement (e.g., von Hippel-Lindau syndrome)
- Subset of CNS cavernous hemangiomas occur in familial setting (CCM gene mutations)

#### Lymphangiomas

- Lymphatic counterpart of hemangiomas
- Microscopic: Cystic and cavernous spaces lined by endothelial cells; surrounded by lymphoid aggregates; contain pale fluid
- Occur in skin and deeper regions (neck, axilla, mediastinum, retroperitoneum)
- Histologically benign but tend to increase in size after birth; may encroach on mediastinal structures or nerve trunks

#### Teratomas

- May be benign, well-differentiated cystic (mature), indeterminate potential (immature), or frankly malignant
- **Sacrococcygeal teratomas**: Most common of childhood (40%+ of cases)
- Approximately 10% associated with congenital anomalies (defects of hindgut, cloacal region, midline defects — e.g., meningocele, spina bifida)
- 75%: Mature teratomas (benign course)
- 12%: Unmistakably malignant (lethal)
- Remainder: Immature teratomas (malignant potential correlates with amount of immature tissue)
- Benign teratomas in younger infants (≤4 months); malignant lesions in older children

### Malignant Neoplasms

**Common malignant neoplasms by age group:**

**0-4 years:**
- Leukemia
- Retinoblastoma
- Neuroblastoma
- Wilms tumor
- Hepatoblastoma
- Soft tissue sarcoma (especially rhabdomyosarcoma)
- Teratomas
- CNS tumors

**5-9 years:**
- Leukemia
- Retinoblastoma
- Neuroblastoma
- Hepatocellular carcinoma
- Soft tissue sarcoma
- Ewing tumor
- CNS tumors
- Lymphoma

**10-14 years:**
- Hepatocellular carcinoma
- Soft tissue sarcoma
- Osteogenic sarcoma
- Thyroid carcinoma
- Hodgkin disease

#### Neuroblastoma

**Definition:** Tumor of sympathetic ganglia and adrenal medulla derived from primordial neural crest cells; most important member of neuroblastic family.

**Epidemiology:**
- Second most common solid malignancy of childhood after brain tumors
- Accounts for 7-10% of pediatric neoplasms
- Up to 50% of malignancies diagnosed in infancy

**Unique features:** Spontaneous regression; spontaneous or therapy-induced maturation.

**Genetics:**
- Most occur sporadically
- 1-2% are familial (autosomal dominant)
- May involve both adrenals or multiple primary autonomic sites
- **Germline mutations in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene** linked to familial predisposition
- Somatic **ALK mutations** (gain-of-function) observed in 8-10% of sporadic cases — markers of adverse prognosis
- ALK inhibitor trials underway

**Morphology:**

*Location:*
- 40% arise in adrenal medulla
- Remainder along sympathetic chain: Paravertebral abdomen (25%), posterior mediastinum (15%)

*Macroscopic:*
- Range from minute nodules (in situ lesions) to masses >1 kg
- In situ neuroblastomas 40 times more frequent than symptomatic tumors; great majority spontaneously regress
- Sharply demarcated with fibrous pseudocapsule OR infiltrative (invades kidneys, renal vein, vena cava, aorta)
- Soft, gray-tan, brainlike tissue; larger tumors have necrosis, cystic softening, hemorrhage

*Microscopic:*
- Small, primitive-appearing cells with dark nuclei, scant cytoplasm, poorly defined cell borders; grow in solid sheets
- May have prominent mitotic activity, nuclear breakdown ("karyorrhexis"), pleomorphism
- Faintly eosinophilic fibrillary material (**neuropil**) — corresponds to neuritic processes of primitive neuroblasts
- **Homer-Wright pseudorosettes**: Tumor cells concentrically arranged about central space filled with neuropil
- Immunochemistry: Neural markers (neuron-specific enolase)
- Electron microscopy: Small, membrane-bound, cytoplasmic catecholamine-containing secretory granules

*Differentiation:*
- Some tumors show signs of maturation (spontaneous or therapy-induced)
- Larger cells with abundant cytoplasm, vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleolus: Ganglion cells in various stages of maturation
- **Ganglioneuroblastoma**: Lesions with mixture of primitive neuroblasts and ganglion cells
- **Ganglioneuroma**: Well-differentiated with mature ganglion cells in absence of residual neuroblasts; maturation accompanied by appearance of Schwann cells

**Staging and Prognosis:**

*International Neuroblastoma Staging System:*
- Stage 1: Localized tumor completely excised
- Stage 2A: Localized tumor incompletely excised; ipsilateral lymph nodes negative
- Stage 2B: Localized tumor with or without complete gross excision; ipsilateral lymph nodes positive
- Stage 3: Unresectable unilateral tumor crossing midline; or localized with contralateral lymph node involvement
- Stage 4: Dissemination to distant lymph nodes, bone, bone marrow, liver, skin, other organs
- **Stage 4S (special)**: Localized primary tumor with dissemination limited to liver, skin, bone marrow (<10% nucleated cells); infants <1 year; excellent prognosis with minimal therapy; spontaneous regression common

*Prognostic factors:*
- **Stage and age** most important
- Children <18 months: Much more favorable prognosis
- Most neoplasms in first 18 months: Stage 1, 2, or 4S ("low risk")
- Older children: Usually "intermediate" or "high" risk
- **Histology**: Schwannian stroma and gangliocytic differentiation = favorable prognosis
- **MYCN amplification**: Profound impact; present in 25-30% of primary tumors; most in advanced-stage disease; more copies = worse prognosis
  - Amplification manifests as extrachromosomal double minute chromosomes or homogeneously staining regions
  - Most important genetic abnormality for risk stratification; automatically renders "high" risk regardless of stage/age
- **DNA ploidy**: Hyperdiploid (whole chromosome gains) = favorable prognosis; near-diploid = adverse
- Other prognostic factors: TrkA expression (favorable), RAS-MAP kinase pathway mutations in relapsed tumors (>75%)

**Clinical Features:**
- Children <2 years: Protuberant abdomen, fever, weight loss
- Older children: Metastases cause hepatomegaly, ascites, bone pain
- Metastases: Hematogenous and lymphatic spread to liver, lungs, bones, bone marrow
- Neonates: Disseminated neuroblastomas with multiple cutaneous metastases ("blueberry muffin baby")
- **90% produce catecholamines** → elevated blood catecholamine levels; elevated urine catecholamine metabolites (VMA, HVA)
- Hypertension much less frequent than with pheochromocytomas

### Summary: Neuroblastoma

- Arises from neural crest-derived cells in sympathetic ganglia and adrenal medulla
- Neuroblastomas undifferentiated; ganglioneuroblastomas and ganglioneuromas show differentiation (schwannian stroma, ganglion cells)
- Homer-Wright pseudorosettes characteristic of neuroblastomas
- Age, stage, MYCN amplification, and ploidy most important prognostic features
- Children <18 months usually have better prognosis; higher stage or MYCN amplification = worse prognosis
- Secretes catecholamines; VMA/HVA used for screening

#### Retinoblastoma

**Most common primary intraocular malignancy of children.**

**Genetics:**
- Approximately 40% associated with germline mutation in RB gene (heritable)
- Remaining 60%: Sporadic somatic RB gene mutations
- Familial cases: Multiple tumors; bilateral (may be unifocal and unilateral)
- Sporadic cases: Unilateral, unifocal
- Patients with familial retinoblastoma: Increased risk for osteosarcoma and other soft tissue tumors

**Morphology:**

*Gross:* Nodular masses, usually in posterior retina; often with satellite seedings

*Microscopic:*
- Undifferentiated areas: Small, round cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm (resembling retinoblasts)
- **Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes**: Cluster of cuboidal or short columnar cells arranged around central lumen; nuclei displaced away from lumen; resembles external limiting membrane of retina (contrast with pseudorosettes of neuroblastoma which lack central lumen)

*Spread:*
- Beyond eye through optic nerve or subarachnoid space
- Distant metastases: CNS, skull, distal bones, lymph nodes

**Clinical Features:**
- Poor vision, strabismus, whitish pupil ("cat's eye reflex"), pain and tenderness in eye
- Median age at presentation: 2 years (may be present at birth)
- Untreated: Usually fatal
- Treated with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, enucleation: Survival is rule
- Some tumors spontaneously regress
- Patients with familial retinoblastoma at increased risk for osteosarcoma and soft tissue tumors

#### Wilms Tumor (Nephroblastoma)

**Most common primary tumor of kidney in children; most cases occur between 2 and 5 years of age.**

**Illustrates important concepts:**
- Relationship between congenital malformation and increased tumor risk
- Histologic similarity between tumor and developing organ
- Remarkable success in treatment of childhood tumors

**Congenital Malformation Syndromes with Increased Wilms Tumor Risk:**

**WAGR syndrome:**
- **W**ilms tumor
- **A**niridia
- **G**enital abnormalities
- **M**ental **R**etardation
- Approximately one-third develop Wilms tumor
- Associated with deletions of WT1 gene at 11p13

**Denys-Drash syndrome (DDS):**
- Even higher risk (~90%)
- Gonadal dysgenesis and early onset nephropathy leading to renal failure
- Associated with dominant negative inactivating mutation of WT1

**Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS):**
- Increased risk for Wilms tumor
- Enlargement of individual body organs (tongue, kidneys, liver) or entire body segments (hemihypertrophy)
- Adrenal cytomegaly characteristic microscopic feature
- **Disorder of genomic imprinting** at chromosome 11p15.5 (WT2 locus)
- Genes at this site expressed from only one parental allele; other silenced by methylation
- **Imprinting abnormalities of IGF2** have strongest relationship to tumor predisposition
- IGF2 normally expressed solely from paternal allele; maternal allele imprinted
- Loss of imprinting → reexpression of IGF2 → overexpression → organ enlargement and tumorigenesis
- Also at increased risk for hepatoblastoma, adrenocortical tumors, rhabdomyosarcomas, pancreatic tumors

**WT1 and WT2:**
- **WT1**: Critical for normal renal and gonadal development; located at 11p13
- **WT2**: Imprinting abnormalities involved in BWS; precise gene not identified

**Sporadic Wilms Tumors (90% of cases):**
- Molecular abnormalities only recently elucidated
- **Gain-of-function mutations of β-catenin gene**: Approximately 10%
- **Mutations in microRNA processing genes** (DROSHA, DGCR8, DICER1): 15-20% of tumors with predominantly blastemal histology
- Aberrations in microRNA processing → reduced mature microRNA levels (especially miR-200 family involved in mesenchymal to epithelial transformation during renal morphogenesis)
- **TP53 mutations**: Associated with poor prognosis and distinctive anaplastic histology

**Morphology:**

*Gross:*
- Large, solitary, well-circumscribed mass (10% bilateral or multicentric)
- Cut surface: Soft, homogeneous, tan to gray; occasional hemorrhage, cystic degeneration, necrosis

*Microscopic:*
- Characterized by attempts to recapitulate different stages of nephrogenesis
- **Triphasic combination**: Blastemal, stromal, and epithelial cell types (percentage variable)
- **Blastemal component**: Sheets of small blue cells
- **Epithelial differentiation**: Abortive tubules or glomeruli
- **Stromal cells**: Fibrocytic or myxoid; skeletal muscle "differentiation" not uncommon
- **Anaplasia** (~5%): Large, hyperchromatic, pleomorphic nuclei; abnormal mitoses
  - Correlates with TP53 mutations and resistance to chemotherapy
  - Focal anaplasia: Prognosis no different than tumors without anaplasia
  - Diffuse anaplasia: Poor prognosis

*Nephrogenic rests:*
- Putative precursor lesions; sometimes present in renal parenchyma adjacent to tumor
- Spectrum: From expansile masses resembling Wilms tumors (hyperplastic rests) to sclerotic rests with fibrous tissue and occasional immature tubules/glomeruli
- Presence indicates increased risk for Wilms tumor in contralateral kidney

**Clinical Course:**
- Palpable abdominal mass (may extend across midline, down into pelvis)
- Less common: Fever, abdominal pain, hematuria, intestinal obstruction
- Prognosis generally very good; excellent results with nephrectomy and chemotherapy
- Anaplasia harbinger of adverse prognosis only if diffuse

### Summary: Wilms Tumor

- Most common renal neoplasm of childhood
- Three syndromes with increased risk: Denys-Drash, Beckwith-Wiedemann, WAGR
- WAGR and DDS associated with WT1 inactivation; BWS with imprinting abnormalities at WT2 locus (primarily IGF2)
- Morphologic components: Blastema (small, round blue cells), epithelial, stromal elements
- Nephrogenic rests are precursor lesions
- Excellent prognosis with surgery and chemotherapy; anaplasia (diffuse) indicates poor prognosis

## Molecular Diagnosis of Mendelian and Complex Disorders

### Overview

Advances enabling rapid expansion of molecular diagnostics:
1. Sequencing of human genome and public databases
2. Availability of numerous PCR kits for specific genetic disorders
3. High-resolution microarrays ("gene chips") for genomewide interrogation of DNA and RNA
4. Automated, high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies

**Indications for Genetic Analysis:**

**Prenatal genetic analysis** (offered to patients at risk for cytogenetically abnormal progeny):
- Advanced maternal age (>34 years) — greater risk of trisomies
- Confirmed carrier status for balanced translocation, robertsonian translocation, or inversion
- Fetal abnormalities on ultrasound or abnormal maternal blood screening
- Chromosomal abnormality or Mendelian disorder in previous child
- Determination of fetal sex when parent is confirmed carrier of X-linked disorder

**Postnatal genetic analysis** (usually on peripheral blood lymphocytes):
- Multiple congenital anomalies
- Suspicion of metabolic syndrome
- Unexplained mental retardation and/or developmental delay
- Suspected aneuploidy or syndromic chromosomal abnormality
- Suspected monogenic disease

**Acquired genetic alterations** (somatic mutations in cancer):
- Increasingly large focus area with advent of targeted therapies
- Single gene tests (EGFR, BRAF, HER2) for treatment decisions
- Next-generation sequencing: Interrogation of large numbers of coding genes (often 100s) and translocations in single assay
- "Genomic report" with molecularly targeted treatment recommendations
- Rapid identification of infectious diseases (tuberculosis, Ebola)

### Molecular Diagnosis of Copy Number Abnormalities

**Karyotype analysis by G banding:** Classic approach; relatively low resolution.

**Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization (FISH):**
- DNA probes recognize sequences >100 kilobases
- Probes labeled with fluorescent dyes; applied to metaphase spreads or interphase nuclei
- Can be performed on prenatal samples, peripheral blood lymphocytes, archival tissue sections
- Used for detection of:
  - Numeric abnormalities of chromosomes (aneuploidy)
  - Subtle microdeletions
  - Complex translocations not identifiable by routine karyotyping
  - Gene amplification (e.g., MYCN amplification in neuroblastomas)

**Array Comparative Genomic Hybridization (aCGH):**
- Test DNA and reference DNA labeled with different fluorescent dyes (red, green)
- Cohybridized to array with probes spanning genome at regularly spaced intervals
- Equal binding (diploid): Yellow (mixed green and red)
- Deletion or duplication: Skewing toward red or green
- Highly accurate determination of copy number variants across genome

**SNP Arrays:**
- Similar concept to CGH but probes designed to identify SNP sites genomewide
- Advantages over CGH: Provide zygosity data
- Can identify uniparental disomy (loss of heterozygosity in affected region with diploid DNA content)
- Current generation: >4 million SNP probes
- Mainstay of genomewide association studies (GWASs)

### Direct Detection of DNA Mutations by PCR Analysis

**PCR:** Exponential amplification of DNA

**RT-PCR:** RNA reverse-transcribed to cDNA, then amplified

**Sanger sequencing:**
- Amplified DNA mixed with DNA polymerase, primer, nucleotides, and fluorescently labeled dideoxy terminators (A, T, G, C)
- Produces series of DNA molecules of all possible lengths up to ~1 kilobase
- Size separation by capillary electrophoresis; sequence read and compared to normal
- "Gold standard" for many genotyping assays

**Allele-specific extension strategy:**
- For identifying mutations at specific nucleotide positions
- Fluorescently labeled nucleotides complementary to wild-type or mutant sequence
- More sensitive than Sanger sequencing; can detect mutant DNA even if small fraction (~1%) of total
- **Droplet digital PCR**: Template DNA separated into thousands to millions of microscopic oil droplets; each droplet analyzed individually; exquisite sensitivity (1 mutant strand in 10,000+ wild-type molecules)

### Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

Increasingly supplanting Sanger sequencing for most molecular diagnostics applications.

**Advantages:**
- Massive parallel sequencing (millions of fragments simultaneously)
- High throughput: >1 gigabase pairs per day at relatively low cost
- Whole human genome (~3 gigabases) sequenced several times in days
- Detects variant alleles occurring at low frequency (down to 1%) — unlike Sanger (lower limit ~20%)

**Clinical and research applications:**
- **Targeted sequencing**: Panel of genes (few to several hundred)
- **Whole exome sequencing (WES)**: All coding regions
- **Whole genome sequencing (WGS)**: Includes noncoding regions
- **RNA-Seq**: Transcriptome measurement
- **ChIP-Seq**: Genomewide binding sites for transcription factors or histones

### Linkage Analysis and Genomewide Association Studies

**Linkage analysis:**
- Used when direct diagnosis of mutations not possible (causal gene unknown or disease is multifactorial)
- Assesses shared marker loci (SNPs) in family members exhibiting disease/trait
- Assumes SNPs in linkage disequilibrium with disease allele are transmitted through pedigrees
- Most useful in Mendelian disorders related to one gene with profound effects and high penetrance

**Genomewide Association Studies (GWASs):**
- Large cohorts of patients with and without disease examined across entire genome
- Identifies variant SNPs overrepresented in persons with disease
- Identifies regions containing variant gene(s) conferring disease susceptibility
- Causal variant provisionally identified using candidate gene approach
- Also used for quantitative traits (height, body mass, hair/eye color, bone density)

## Summary: Major Concepts in Chapter 7

### Genetic Abnormalities
- **Mutations** in protein-coding genes include point mutations, frameshift mutations, and trinucleotide repeat mutations
- Structural variations (copy number changes, translocations) and non-coding RNA alterations also contribute to disease

### Mendelian Disorders
- Follow autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked inheritance patterns
- **Autosomal dominant**: Expressed in heterozygous state; often involves receptors and structural proteins; 50% reduction in gene product causes disease
- **Autosomal recessive**: Both alleles mutated; usually involves enzymes; 25% recurrence risk for siblings
- **X-linked**: Transmitted by heterozygous females to sons; females usually unaffected due to random X inactivation

### Diseases of Structural Proteins and Receptors
- **Marfan syndrome**: Fibrillin-1 mutation; affects skeleton, eyes, cardiovascular system; TGF-β activation contributes to pathogenesis
- **Ehlers-Danlos syndromes**: Collagen defects; fragile skin, hypermobile joints, organ rupture risk
- **Familial hypercholesterolemia**: LDL receptor mutations; hypercholesterolemia, premature atherosclerosis
- **Cystic fibrosis**: CFTR mutations; chloride transport defect; pulmonary and GI manifestations; pancreatic insufficiency

### Enzyme Deficiency Diseases
- **Phenylketonuria**: PAH deficiency; mental retardation preventable with dietary restriction
- **Galactosemia**: GALT deficiency; liver damage, cataracts, E. coli sepsis risk

### Lysosomal Storage Diseases
- Enzyme deficiency → accumulation of undegraded substrates
- **Tay-Sachs**: Hexosaminidase A deficiency; GM2 ganglioside accumulation; CNS degeneration
- **Niemann-Pick**: Sphingomyelinase deficiency (types A/B); cholesterol transport defect (type C)
- **Gaucher disease**: Glucocerebrosidase deficiency; "wrinkled tissue paper" macrophages; bone involvement; Parkinson disease risk
- **MPSs**: Mucopolysaccharide accumulation; coarse facies, organomegaly, corneal clouding

### Glycogen Storage Diseases
- Enzyme deficiencies affecting glycogen metabolism
- **Von Gierke disease**: Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency; hepatomegaly, hypoglycemia
- **McArdle disease**: Muscle phosphorylase deficiency; exercise intolerance, cramps
- **Pompe disease**: Lysosomal acid maltase deficiency; cardiomegaly

### Cytogenetic Disorders
- **Numeric abnormalities**: Trisomy (extra chromosome), monosomy (missing chromosome); result from nondisjunction
- **Structural abnormalities**: Translocations, deletions, inversions, isochromosomes, ring chromosomes
- **Down syndrome** (trisomy 21): Most common chromosomal disorder; intellectual disability, characteristic facies, cardiac defects, leukemia risk
- **22q11.2 deletion**: DiGeorge/velocardiofacial spectrum; cardiac, facial, thymic, parathyroid abnormalities
- **Klinefelter syndrome** (47,XXY): Male hypogonadism; eunuchoid habitus; gynecomastia; sterility
- **Turner syndrome** (45,X): Female short stature; webbed neck; cardiovascular anomalies; streak ovaries; amenorrhea

### Atypical Inheritance
- **Triplet repeat mutations**: Dynamic mutations that expand during gametogenesis; fragile X syndrome, Huntington disease
- **Mitochondrial inheritance**: Maternal transmission; Leber hereditary optic neuropathy
- **Genomic imprinting**: Parent-of-origin gene silencing; Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes (deletion of same chromosome region from different parents)

### Congenital Anomalies
- **Malformations**: Primary intrinsic errors of morphogenesis
- **Disruptions**: Secondary destruction of previously normal tissue
- **Deformations**: Extrinsic compression (e.g., uterine constraint)
- **Sequence**: Cascade from single initiating defect (e.g., Potter sequence)
- Causes: Genetic, environmental (TORCH, drugs, alcohol), multifactorial
- Timing of insult critical for type and severity of anomaly

### Perinatal Conditions
- **Prematurity**: <37 weeks gestation; RDS, NEC, sepsis, intraventricular hemorrhage
- **Respiratory distress syndrome**: Surfactant deficiency; hyaline membrane disease; preventable with steroids and surfactant therapy
- **Necrotizing enterocolitis**: Premature infants; enteral feeding-associated; bowel necrosis
- **SIDS**: Unexplained sudden infant death; likely delayed arousal/cardiorespiratory control; prone sleeping risk factor

### Pediatric Tumors
- Different distribution than adults (hematopoietic, neural, soft tissue > epithelial)
- **Neuroblastoma**: Neural crest origin; catecholamine secretion; spontaneous regression possible; MYCN amplification = poor prognosis
- **Retinoblastoma**: RB gene mutations; Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes
- **Wilms tumor**: WT1/WT2 involvement; associated with congenital syndromes; triphasic histology

### Molecular Diagnostics
- **FISH**: Fluorescence in situ hybridization for specific chromosomal regions
- **aCGH/SNP arrays**: Genomewide copy number and zygosity analysis
- **PCR and Sanger sequencing**: Mutation detection
- **Next-generation sequencing**: Targeted, exome, or whole genome sequencing
- **GWASs**: Genomewide association studies for complex disorders
