# Chapter 19: Female Genital System and Breast

## Vulva

### Nonneoplastic Epithelial Disorders

#### Lichen Sclerosus

**Lichen sclerosus** is characterized by:
- **Thinning of the epidermis**
- **Disappearance of rete pegs**
- **Zone of acellular, homogenized, dermal fibrosis**
- **Bandlike mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltrate** (Fig. 19.1)

**Clinical features:**
- Smooth, white plaques (termed **leukoplakia**) or papules
- When entire vulva is affected: **labia become atrophic and stiffened**
- **Vaginal orifice constricted**
- Occurs in all age groups but most common in **postmenopausal women** and **prepubertal girls**

**Pathogenesis:**
- Uncertain
- Presence of **activated T cells** in subepithelial inflammatory infiltrate
- Increased frequency of **autoimmune disorders** in affected women
- Suggests an **autoimmune etiology**

**Malignant potential:**
- Benign condition
- **1% to 5%** of women with symptomatic lichen sclerosus develop **HPV-negative squamous cell carcinoma of the vulva**

#### Lichen Simplex Chronicus

**Lichen simplex chronicus** is marked by:
- **Epithelial thickening** (particularly of the **stratum granulosum**)
- **Hyperkeratosis**
- Increased **mitotic activity** in basal and suprabasal layers
- **No epithelial atypia** (Fig. 19.1)
- Sometimes pronounced **leukocytic infiltration of the dermis**

**Etiology:**
- Consequence of **chronic irritation**
- Often caused by **pruritus** related to underlying **inflammatory dermatosis**
- Appears as an area of **leukoplakia**

**Clinical significance:**
- No increased predisposition to cancer when lesions are isolated
- Often present at margins of established **vulvar cancer**
- Suggests some association with **neoplastic disease**

**Note:** Lichen sclerosus and lichen simplex chronicus may coexist in different areas of the body in the same person. Both lesions may take the form of **leukoplakia**. Biopsy and microscopic examination are often needed to differentiate these clinically similar-appearing lesions from **squamous cell carcinoma in situ** and **invasive squamous cell carcinoma**.

#### Summary: Nonneoplastic Epithelial Disorders

- **Lichen sclerosus** is characterized by **atrophic epithelium**, **subepithelial dermal fibrosis**, and **bandlike chronic inflammation**
- Lichen sclerosus carries a slightly increased risk for development of **squamous cell carcinoma**
- **Lichen simplex chronicus** is characterized by **thickened epithelium (hyperplasia)**, usually with a **dermal inflammatory infiltrate**
- Lesions of lichen sclerosus and lichen simplex chronicus must be biopsied to distinguish definitively from other causes of **leukoplakia**, such as **squamous cell carcinoma of the vulva**

### Tumors of the Vulva

#### Condylomas

**Condyloma** = any warty lesion of the vulva

**Two distinctive forms:**

**1. Condylomata lata**
- Flat, minimally elevated lesions
- Occur in **secondary syphilis** (Chapter 18)
- Uncommon today

**2. Condylomata acuminata**
- May be **papillary and distinctly elevated** or **somewhat flat and rugose**
- May occur anywhere on the **anogenital surface**
- Often multiple lesions
- Size: a few millimeters to many centimeters in diameter
- Color: **red-pink to pink-brown** (Fig. 19.2)

**Histopathology:**
- Characteristic cellular feature: **koilocytosis**
  - Cytopathic change characterized by **perınuclear cytoplasmic vacuolization**
  - **Wrinkled nuclear contour**
  - Hallmark of **HPV infection** (Fig. 19.2)
- **More than 90%** positive for **HPV subtypes 6 and 11**
- HPV is **sexually transmitted**
- Identical lesions occur in men on the **penis** and around the **anus**

**Clinical significance:**
- **HPV 6 and 11 are low-risk viral types**
- Vulvar condylomas **do not commonly progress to cancer**
- Women with condyloma acuminata are at risk for other **HPV-related lesions** in the **vagina** and **cervix**

#### Carcinoma of the Vulva

**Epidemiology:**
- Represents about **3%** of all female genital tract cancers
- Most common in women **older than age 60**
- Approximately **90%** are **squamous cell carcinomas**
- Other tumors: **adenocarcinomas** or **basal cell carcinomas**

**Two distinct forms of vulvar squamous cell carcinomas:**

**Form 1: HPV-related squamous cell carcinoma**
- Usually **poorly differentiated**
- Sometimes **multifocal**
- Often evolves from **vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN)**
- Related to **high-risk HPV strains** (especially **HPV type 16**)
- Occurs in **middle-aged women**, particularly **cigarette smokers**

**Form 2: HPV-negative squamous cell carcinoma**
- Occurs in **older women**
- Usually **well-differentiated** and **unifocal**
- Often preceded by **differentiated vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (dVIN)**
- Associated with **lichen sclerosus**
- Characterized by **cytologic atypia** confined to the **basal layer**
- May give rise to **well-differentiated, keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma**

**Morphology of VIN and vulvar carcinoma:**
- VIN and early vulvar carcinomas commonly manifest as areas of **leukoplakia**
- About **one-fourth** of cases are **pigmented** (presence of **melanin**)
- With time: **exophytic or ulcerative/endophytic tumors**
- **HPV-positive tumors**: often **multifocal** and **warty**, tend to be **poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinomas**
- **HPV-negative tumors**: usually **unifocal**, typically **well-differentiated keratinizing squamous cell carcinomas**

**Spread:**
- Both forms tend to remain confined to site of origin for a few years
- Then **invade and spread**, usually first to **regional lymph nodes**
- Risk of metastasis correlates with **depth of invasion**
- Outcome dependent on **tumor stage**

**Prognosis:**
- **5-year survival**: 70%–93% for patients with **negative lymph nodes**
- **5-year survival**: 25%–41% for patients with **lymph node metastases**

#### Extramammary Paget Disease

**Definition:** **Intraepidermal proliferation of epithelial cells** that can occur in skin of the **vulva** or **nipple** of the breast

**Key difference from Paget disease of the breast:**
- In breast: virtually always associated with an **underlying carcinoma**
- In vulva: only a **minority** of cases have an **underlying tumor**
- Vulvar Paget cells most commonly arise from **epidermal progenitor cells**

**Clinical presentation:**
- **Red, scaly, crusted plaque**
- May mimic **inflammatory dermatitis**

**Histopathology:**
- **Large cells** with abundant **pale, finely granular cytoplasm**
- Occasional **cytoplasmic vacuoles**
- Infiltrate the epidermis, singly and in groups (Fig. 19.3)
- **Presence of mucin** (detected by **PAS staining**) distinguishes Paget disease from **vulvar melanoma** (which lacks mucin)

**Clinical course:**
- Intraepidermal Paget disease may persist for **years or decades** without evidence of invasion
- When there is an associated tumor involving skin appendages: Paget cells may **invade locally** and ultimately **metastasize**
- After metastasis: **prognosis is poor**

#### Summary: Tumors of the Vulva

- **HPV-related vulvar squamous cell carcinomas** usually are **poorly differentiated** lesions and sometimes are **multifocal**
- They often evolve from **vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia**
- **Non–HPV-related vulvar squamous cell carcinomas** occur in older women and usually are **well-differentiated** and **unifocal**
- They are often preceded by **"differentiated" vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia** associated with **lichen sclerosus**
- **Vulvar Paget disease** is characterized by a **red, scaky plaque** caused by **proliferation of epithelial cells within the epidermis**
- Usually there is **no underlying carcinoma**, unlike **Paget disease of the nipple**

## Vagina

### Congenital Anomalies
- **Total absence of the vagina**
- **Septate or double vagina** (usually associated with **septate cervix** and sometimes **septate uterus**)
- **Congenital, lateral Gartner duct cysts** arising from persistent **wolffian duct rests**

### Vaginitis

**Definition:** Common condition, usually transient and of no clinical consequence; associated with **vaginal discharge (leukorrhea)**

**Organisms implicated:**
- **Bacteria, fungi, and parasites**
- Many are **normal commensals** that become pathogenic in setting of:
  - **Diabetes**
  - **Systemic antibiotic therapy** (disruption of normal microbial flora)
  - **Immunodeficiency**
  - **Pregnancy**
  - **Recent abortion**

**Specific organisms:**

**Candida albicans**
- Causes **candidal (monilial) vaginitis**
- Characterized by **curdy white discharge**
- Part of normal vaginal flora in about **5%** of women
- Symptomatic infection involves predisposing influences or **superinfection by a new, more aggressive strain**

**Trichomonas vaginalis**
- **Sexually transmitted**
- Produces **watery, copious gray-green discharge**
- Parasites can be identified by **microscopy**
- Found in about **10%** of **asymptomatic women**
- Persistent infection usually stems from **sexual transmission of a new strain**

### Malignant Neoplasms of the Vagina

#### Squamous Cell Carcinoma
- **Extremely uncommon** cancer
- Usually occurs in women **older than 60 years**
- Risk factors similar to **carcinoma of the cervix**
- **Vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VAIN)** = precursor lesion
  - Nearly always associated with **HPV infection**
- Invasive squamous cell carcinoma: **HPV DNA** present in more than half of cases

#### Clear Cell Adenocarcinoma

**Historical significance:**
- Identified in **1970** in young women whose mothers took **diethylstilbestrol (DES)** during pregnancy

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence in DES-exposed individuals: **<1 per 1000**
- About **40 times greater** than unexposed population

**Pathology:**
- About **one-third** of exposed women develop small **glandular or microcystic inclusions** in vaginal mucosa
- Appear as **red, granular foci**
- Lined by **mucus-secreting or ciliated columnar cells**
- Condition called **vaginal adenosis** (benign)
- This is the precursor lesion for **clear cell adenocarcinoma**

#### Sarcoma Botryoides

- Rare form of **primary vaginal cancer**
- Manifests as **soft polypoid masses**
- Usually encountered in **infants and children younger than 5 years**
- Also found in **urinary bladder** and **bile ducts**
- = **Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma**
- Discussed in Chapter 21 (soft tissue tumors)

## Cervix

### Cervicitis

**Definition:** Inflammatory conditions of the cervix; may be associated with **purulent vaginal discharge**

**Classification:** Infectious or noninfectious (differentiation is difficult due to presence of normal vaginal flora)

**Common organisms in normal vaginal flora:**
- Incidental vaginal aerobes and anaerobes
- **Streptococci, staphylococci, enterococci, Escherichia coli, Candida spp.**

**Important pathogens:**
- **Chlamydia trachomatis** (most common - accounts for up to **40%** of cervicitis in sexually transmitted infection clinics)
- **Ureaplasma urealyticum**
- **Trichomonas vaginalis**
- **Neisseria gonorrhoeae**
- **HSV-2** (agent of herpes genitalis)
- **Certain types of HPV**

**Clinical significance:**
- **C. trachomatis** is the most common pathogen
- **Herpetic infections** are noteworthy because **maternal–infant transmission** during childbirth may result in **serious, sometimes fatal systemic herpetic infection** in the newborn

**Clinical presentation:**
- Often comes to attention on **routine examination** or because of **leukorrhea**
- Treated empirically with antibiotics active against **chlamydia and gonococcus**
- **Nucleic acid amplification tests** used on vaginal fluid to identify presence of these organisms and **Trichomonas vaginalis**

### Neoplasia of the Cervix

#### Normal Anatomy

**Cervical epithelium:**
- **Columnar mucus-secreting epithelium** of the **endocervix**
- Joined to **squamous epithelial covering** of the **exocervix** at the **cervical os**
- With onset of puberty: **squamocolumnar junction undergoes eversion**
- Columnar epithelium becomes visible on the **exocervix**
- Exposed columnar cells undergo **squamous metaplasia**
- Forms the **transformation zone** (where tumors most commonly arise - Fig. 19.4)

#### Pathogenesis

**HPV (Human Papillomavirus):**
- **Causative agent** of cervical neoplasia
- Has **tropism for immature squamous cells** of the transformation zone
- Most HPV infections are **transient** and eliminated within months by host immune response
- A subset **persists**; some cause **squamous intraepithelial lesions (SILs)**

**HPV and carcinogenesis:**
- HPV uses **host cell DNA polymerases** to replicate its genome
- HPV "solves" the problem of virion shedding through two viral oncoproteins: **E6 and E7**
- **E6 protein** inhibits **p53** (potent tumor suppressor)
- **E7 protein** inhibits **RB** (potent tumor suppressor)
- E6 and E7 **suppress division of squamous cells as they mature**
- Central role in **HPV life cycle** and **carcinogenic activity**

**HPV classification:**
- **High-risk HPV**: types 16, 18, 31, 33
- **Low-risk HPV**: types 6, 11

**HPV variants classification:**
- **High-risk types**: propensity to induce carcinogenesis; integrate into host genome
- **Low-risk types**: associated with **condylomas**; do not integrate into genome; remain as **free episomal viral DNA**

**HPV-related carcinogenesis pathway (Fig. 19.5):**
1. **HPV exposure**
2. **Low-risk HPV** (6, 11) → **Condyloma** (hundreds of thousands/year)
3. **High-risk HPV** (16, 18, others) → **SIL** (~1 million/year)
4. **Persistent infection** → **hSIL** (50,000/year)
5. **Viral integration** → **Invasive cancer** (13,000/year)
6. **Metastasis** (5,000/year)

**Risk factors for CIN and invasive carcinoma:**
- **Early age at first intercourse**
- **Multiple sexual partners**
- **Male partner with multiple previous sexual partners**
- **Persistent infection by high-risk HPV strains**

**Additional cofactors:**
- **Cigarette smoking**
- **Immunodeficiency** (e.g., HIV infection)
- **Immune and hormonal status**
- **Coinfection with other sexually transmitted agents**

**Mechanisms of viral integration:**
- Integration **always disrupts** an HPV gene that **negatively regulates E6 and E7**
- Leads to **increased expression** of E6 and E7
- Sometimes HPV integrates **near a host cell oncogene** (e.g., **MYC**), leading to its **overexpression**

#### Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (SIL)

**Definition:** Precancerous epithelial change that usually precedes overt cancer by **many years, sometimes decades**

**Epidemiology:**
- SIL peaks in incidence at about **30 years of age**
- Invasive carcinoma peaks at about **45 years of age**

**Classification system (two-tiered):**

| Feature | LSIL (Low-grade SIL) | HSIL (High-grade SIL) |
|---------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Previous name** | CIN I | CIN II, CIN III |
| **Associated with** | Productive HPV infection | Precancerous lesion |
| **Progression** | Most regress; small % progress to HSIL | High risk for progression to carcinoma |
| **Treatment** | Observation (not premalignant) | Surgical excision (cone biopsy) |

**LSIL (CIN I):**
- Dysplastic changes in the **lower third** of squamous epithelium
- **Koilocytotic change** in superficial layers
- Does **not progress directly** to invasive carcinoma
- **60%** regress; **30%** persist; **10%** progress to HSIL

**HSIL (CIN II):**
- Dysplasia extends to the **middle third** of epithelium
- Variation in **cell and nuclear size**
- **Heterogeneity of nuclear chromatin**
- **Mitoses** (some atypical) above basal layer
- **10%** progress to carcinoma within 10 years

**HSIL (CIN III):**
- Almost **complete loss of differentiation**
- Greater variation in **cell and nuclear size**
- **Chromatin heterogeneity**
- **Disorderly orientation of cells**
- **Abnormal mitoses**
- Affects **virtually all layers** of epithelium
- **Koilocytotic change usually absent**

**Table 19.1: Natural History of Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (SILs)**

| Lesion | Regress | Persist | Progress |
|--------|---------|---------|----------|
| **LSIL (CIN I)** | 60% | 30% | 10% (to HSIL) |
| **HSIL (CIN II, III)** | 30% | 60% | 10% (to carcinoma) |

**Screening - Papanicolaou (Pap) Test:**
- Cells scraped from transformation zone and examined microscopically
- **Most successful cancer-screening test ever developed**
- In the United States: dramatically lowered incidence of invasive cervical tumors to about **13,000 cases annually**
- Mortality: about **4,000 per year**
- Cervical cancer **no longer ranks among top 10 causes of cancer deaths** in U.S. women

**HPV DNA testing:**
- Approved by **FDA** for cervical scrapings
- Highly sensitive but **lower specificity** than Pap smear
- Only recommended for women **aged 30 or older**
- Positive test at this age more likely identifies **persistent infection** that may lead to cervical neoplasia

**HPV Vaccine:**
- **Quadrivalent vaccine** (types 6, 11, 16, 18)
- **9-valent vaccine** (additional types)
- Very effective in preventing HPV infections
- Does **not supplant** need for routine cervical cancer screening

#### Invasive Carcinoma of the Cervix

**Histologic types:**
- **Squamous cell carcinomas**: 75%
- **Adenocarcinomas** and **mixed adenosquamous carcinomas**: 20%
- **Small cell neuroendocrine carcinomas**: <5%
- All associated with **HPV**

**Epidemiology:**
- Peak incidence at about **45 years of age**
- 10–15 years after detection of precursor SIL

**Risk factors for progression:**
- **Cigarette smoking**
- **HIV infection** (suggesting immune surveillance plays a role)

**Morphology:**
- Develop in the **transformation zone**
- Range from **microscopic foci of stromal invasion** to **grossly conspicuous exophytic tumors** (Fig. 19.8)
- Consist of **tongues and nests of squamous cells** that produce a **desmoplastic stromal response**
- Grading based on degree of **squamous differentiation**
- Range from **minimal** to **well-differentiated tumors** that elaborate **keratin pearls**
- Rare tumors with **neuroendocrine differentiation** resemble **small cell carcinomas of the lung**
- Tumors encircling cervix and penetrating stroma produce **barrel cervix**
- Extension into **parametrial soft tissues** can affix uterus to surrounding pelvic structures

**Metastasis:**
- Likelihood correlates with **depth of tumor invasion** and **presence of tumor cells in vascular spaces**
- Risk: less than **1%** for tumors <3 mm depth; more than **10%** after invasion exceeds 3 mm

**Clinical features:**
- Most often seen in women who have **never had a Pap smear** or not screened for many years
- **Unexpected vaginal bleeding**
- **Leukorrhea**
- **Dyspareunia** (painful coitus)
- **Dysuria**

**Treatment:**
- **Hysterectomy** and **lymph node dissection**
- Small microinvasive carcinomas: **cone biopsy**
- **Radiation and chemotherapy**

**Prognosis:**
- Most strongly predicted by **tumor stage**
- **Neuroendocrine carcinomas**: prognosis also depends on **cell type** (aggressive course)

#### Endocervical Polyp

**Features:**
- **Benign polypoid masses** protruding from endocervical mucosa
- Can be several centimeters in size
- **Smooth, glistening surface**
- Underlying cystically dilated spaces filled with **mucinous secretions**
- Surface epithelium: **mucus-secreting columnar cells**
- Stroma: **edematous** with scattered **mononuclear cells**
- May undergo **squamous metaplasia** and **ulcerations**

**Clinical significance:**
- May **bleed**
- **No malignant potential**

#### Summary: Cervical Neoplasia

- Risk factors for cervical carcinoma are related to **HPV exposure** (early age at first intercourse, multiple sexual partners, cigarette smoking, immunodeficiency)
- Nearly all cervical carcinomas are caused by **HPV infections**, particularly **high-risk HPV types 16, 18, 31, and 33**
- **HPV vaccine** is effective in preventing infection from HPV types most commonly associated with carcinoma
- HPV expresses **E6 and E7 proteins** that **inactivate p53 and RB tumor suppressors**, resulting in:
  - **Increased cell proliferation**
  - **Suppression of DNA damage–induced apoptosis**
- In cervical cancer, **high-risk HPV is integrated in the host genome**, increasing E6 and E7 expression
- **Pap smear** is highly effective for detection of SIL and carcinoma
- **HPV testing** currently used in conjunction with Pap smear

## Uterus

### Endometritis

**Classification:**
- **Acute endometritis**: neutrophilic infiltrate predominates
- **Chronic endometritis**: lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate predominates
- Diagnosis of chronic endometritis requires **presence of plasma cells**

**Etiology:**
- Component of **pelvic inflammatory disease**
- Frequently a result of **N. gonorrhoeae** or **C. trachomatis** infection
- **Chlamydial infection**: prominent **lymphoid follicles** may be seen

**Tuberculous endometritis:**
- Causes **granulomatous endometritis**
- Frequently with associated **tuberculous salpingitis and peritonitis**
- Common in countries where **tuberculosis is endemic**
- Should be included in differential diagnosis for pelvic inflammatory disease in women who recently emigrated from endemic areas

**Other causes:**
- **Retained products of conception** (miscarriage or delivery)
- **Intrauterine device (IUD)** as foreign body
- Retained tissue or foreign bodies act as **nidus for ascending infection**

**Clinical features:**
- **Fever, abdominal pain, menstrual abnormalities**
- Increased risk of **infertility** and **ectopic pregnancy** due to damage and scarring of **fallopian tubes**

### Adenomyosis

**Definition:** Presence of **endometrial tissue in the myometrium**
- Nests of **endometrial stroma, glands, or both**
- Found deep in myometrium interposed between **muscle bundles**
- Induces **reactive hypertrophy of the myometrium**
- Results in **enlarged, globular uterus** with **thickened uterine wall**

**Clinical features:**
- **Menorrhagia, dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain** (particularly just prior to menstruation)
- Can coexist with **endometriosis**

### Endometriosis

**Definition:** Presence of **endometrial glands and stroma** in a location **outside the uterus**

**Epidemiology:**
- Occurs in as many as **10%** of women in reproductive years
- In nearly **half of women with infertility**

**Common sites:**
- **Pelvic structures**: ovaries, pouch of Douglas, uterine ligaments, tubes, rectovaginal septum
- Less frequently: **peritoneal cavity** or **periumbilical tissues**
- Rarely: **lymph nodes, lungs, heart, skeletal muscle, or bone**

**Theories of origin:**

1. **Regurgitation theory** (currently favored)
   - Menstrual backflow through fallopian tubes leads to implantation

2. **Benign metastases theory**
   - Endometrial tissue "spreads" to distant sites via **blood vessels and lymphatics**

3. **Metaplastic theory**
   - Endometrial differentiation of **coelomic epithelium** (mesothelium of pelvis and abdomen)

4. **Extrauterine stem/progenitor cell theory**
   - Circulating stem/progenitor cells from **bone marrow** differentiate into endometrial tissue

**Pathogenesis (Fig. 19.9):**
- Endometriotic tissue is **not just misplaced but also abnormal**
- Compared to normal endometrium, exhibits **increased levels of inflammatory mediators** (particularly **prostaglandin E2**)
- Inflammation results from **recruitment and activation of macrophages** by factors made by endometrial stromal cells
- Stromal cells also make **aromatase**, leading to **local production of estrogen**
- These factors enhance **survival and persistence** of endometriotic tissue in foreign location
- Explains beneficial effects of **COX-2 inhibitors** and **aromatase inhibitors** in treatment

**Morphology (Fig. 19.10):**
- Functioning endometrium undergoes **cyclic bleeding**
- Appear grossly as **red-brown nodules or implants**
- Size: microscopic to **1–2 cm** in diameter
- Lie on or just under affected **serosal surface**
- Lesions may coalesce to form larger masses
- When ovaries are involved: form large, **blood-filled cysts** ("**chocolate cysts**" - Fig. 19.10)
- With seepage and organization of blood: **widespread fibrosis**
- Leading to:
  - **Adhesions** among pelvic structures
  - **Sealing of tubal fimbriated ends**
  - **Distortion of fallopian tubes and ovaries**
- Diagnosis: **endometrial glands and stroma** at sites external to endometrium

**Clinical features:**
- **Dysmenorrhea** and **pelvic pain** (from intrapelvic bleeding and intraabdominal adhesions)
- **Infertility** (from scarring of tubes and ovaries)
- **Dyspareunia** (rectal wall involvement)
- **Dysuria** (uterine or bladder serosa involvement)

### Summary: Nonneoplastic Disorders of Endometrium

- **Adenomyosis** refers to growth of endometrium into the myometrium often with uterine enlargement
- **Endometriosis** refers to endometrial glands and stroma located outside the uterus; most often involves **pelvic or abdominal peritoneum**
- The ectopic endometrium in endometriosis undergoes **cyclic bleeding**, causing **dysmenorrhea and pelvic pain**

### Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

**Definitions:**
- **Menorrhagia**: profuse or prolonged bleeding at the time of the period
- **Metrorrhagia**: irregular bleeding between the periods
- **Postmenopausal bleeding**

**Common causes:**
- **Dysfunctional uterine bleeding**
- **Endometrial polyps**
- **Leiomyomas**
- **Endometrial hyperplasia**
- **Endometrial carcinoma**

**Table 19.2: Causes of Abnormal Uterine Bleeding by Age**

| Age Group | Cause(s) |
|-----------|----------|
| **Prepuberty** | Precocious puberty (hypothalamic, pituitary, or ovarian origin) |
| **Adolescence** | **Anovulatory cycle** |
| **Reproductive age** | Complications of pregnancy (abortion, trophoblastic disease, ectopic pregnancy); Proliferations (leiomyoma, adenomyosis, polyps, endometrial hyperplasia, carcinoma); **Anovulatory cycle**; Ovulatory dysfunctional bleeding (e.g., inadequate luteal phase) |
| **Perimenopause** | **Anovulatory cycle**; Irregular shedding; Proliferations (carcinoma, hyperplasia, polyps) |
| **Postmenopause** | Proliferations (carcinoma, hyperplasia, polyps); **Endometrial atrophy** |

**Dysfunctional Uterine Bleeding:**
- Bleeding in the **absence of an organic uterine lesion**
- Most common cause: **anovulation** (failure to ovulate)
- Results from **hormonal imbalances**
- Most common at **menarche** and in **perimenopausal period**
- Due to fluctuations in **hypothalamus/pituitary/ovarian axis**

**Less common causes of anovulation:**
- **Endocrine disorders**: thyroid disease, adrenal disease, pituitary tumors
- **Ovarian lesions**: functioning ovarian tumor (granulosa cell tumors), **polycystic ovarian syndrome**
- **Generalized metabolic disturbances**: obesity, malnutrition, chronic systemic disorders

**Inadequate Luteal Phase:**
- Thought to stem from **insufficient production of progesterone** by the **corpus luteum**

### Proliferative Lesions of the Endometrium and Myometrium

#### Endometrial Hyperplasia

**Definition:** Exaggerated **endometrial proliferation (hyperplasia)** due to **excess estrogen relative to progestin**

**Causes of estrogen excess:**
- **Obesity** (adipose tissue converts steroid precursors into estrogens)
- **Failure of ovulation** (perimenopause)
- **Prolonged administration of estrogenic steroids** without counterbalancing progestin
- **Estrogen-producing ovarian lesions** (polycystic ovary syndrome, **granulosa-theca cell tumors**)

**Classification (based on presence of cytologic atypia):**
- **Hyperplasia without atypia**
- **Hyperplasia with atypia** (also called **endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia - EIN**)

**Clinical significance:**
- **Without atypia**: low risk (1%–3%) for progression to endometrial carcinoma
- **With atypia (EIN)**: much higher risk (**20%–50%**)
- When hyperplasia with atypia is discovered: must be evaluated for presence of cancer
- Usually warrants **hysterectomy** in patients no longer desiring fertility
- Younger patients: treatment with **high-dose progestins** may preserve uterus

**Molecular findings:**
- **PTEN tumor suppressor gene** inactivation:
  - Found in approximately **50%** of hyperplasia with atypia
  - Found in >**70%** of endometrioid carcinoma
- Indicates hyperplasia with atypia is a **precursor lesion** for **endometrioid endometrial carcinoma**

#### Endometrial Carcinoma

**Epidemiology:**
- **Most frequent cancer** occurring in the female genital tract (in U.S. and many Western countries)
- Generally appears between **55 and 65 years** of age
- Uncommon **before age 40**

**Two major types:**

**1. Endometrioid Carcinoma (80% of cases)**
- Histologically similar to normal endometrial glands
- Arises in association with **estrogen excess** in setting of **endometrial hyperplasia**
- Risk factors:
  - **Obesity**
  - **Diabetes**
  - **Hypertension**
  - **Infertility**
  - **Exposure to unopposed estrogen**
- Molecular changes:
  - Early: **Mismatch repair gene mutations** and **PTEN gene mutations**
  - Late: **TP53 mutations** (uncommon)
- Women with germline mutations in **PTEN** (Cowden Syndrome) and **DNA mismatch repair genes** (Lynch Syndrome) at high risk

**2. Serous Carcinoma (~15% of cases)**
- **Less common** but **far more aggressive**
- Arises in setting of **endometrial atrophy** in **older postmenopausal women**
- Not associated with **unopposed estrogen** or **endometrial hyperplasia**
- Molecular changes:
  - **TP53 mutations** in nearly all cases
  - Rare: DNA mismatch repair gene mutations and PTEN mutations
- Preceded by **serous endometrial intraepithelial carcinoma (SEIC)** where TP53 mutations often detected

**Morphology:**

**Endometrioid carcinomas (Fig. 19.12A–B):**
- Resemble normal endometrium
- May be **exophytic or infiltrative**
- Include histologic types: **mucinous, tubal (ciliated), and squamous differentiation**
- Often infiltrate myometrium
- Can enter **vascular spaces (lymphovascular invasion)**
- May metastasize to **regional lymph nodes**
- **Graded 1 to 3** based on degree of differentiation

**Serous carcinomas (Fig. 19.12C–D):**
- Grow in **small tufts and papillae**
- **Marked cytologic atypia**
- Behave **aggressively**; by definition **high grade**
- Immunohistochemistry: **diffuse, strong p53 staining** (correlates with TP53 mutations)

**Clinical features:**
- Manifest with **irregular or postmenopausal bleeding**
- With progression: **uterus enlarges**
- May become affixed to surrounding structures as cancer infiltrates
- **Endometrioid carcinoma**: usually slow to metastasize
- **5-year survival** for early-stage endometrioid carcinoma: **90%**
- Survival drops precipitously in **higher-stage tumors**
- **Serous carcinomas**: prognosis strongly dependent on operative staging; often presents as **high-stage disease** with **poor prognosis**

#### Endometrial Polyps

**Features:**
- Usually **sessile**
- Size: **0.5 to 3 cm** in diameter
- Larger polyps may project into **uterine cavity**
- Composed of endometrium resembling **basalis**
- Frequently with **small muscular arteries**
- Some glands normal architecturally; most are **cystically dilated**
- **Stromal cells are monoclonal** with rearrangement of chromosomal region **6p21**
- Constitute the **neoplastic component** of the polyp

**Clinical features:**
- Most common around **time of menopause**
- Main significance: may produce **abnormal uterine bleeding**

#### Leiomyoma

**Definition:** Benign tumors from **smooth muscle cells** in the myometrium
- Often called **"fibroids"** clinically (due to firmness)

**Epidemiology:**
- **Most common benign tumor** in females
- Affects **30% to 50%** of women of reproductive age
- **More frequent in black women**

**Molecular genetics:**
- Associated with recurrent chromosomal abnormalities
- Rearrangements of **chromosomes 6 and 12**
- **MED12 gene mutations** in up to **70%** of cases (encodes component of RNA polymerase transcription complex)

**Hormonal regulation:**
- **Estrogens** and possibly **oral contraceptives** stimulate growth
- Tumors **shrink postmenopausally**

**Morphology (Fig. 19.13):**
- **Sharply circumscribed**, **firm gray-white masses**
- **Characteristic whorled cut surface**
- May occur singly or as **multiple tumors**
- Size: from small nodules to large tumors that dwarf the uterus
- Location:
  - **Intramural**: embedded within myometrium
  - **Submucosal**: beneath endometrium
  - **Subserosal**: beneath serosa (may extend on attenuated stalks = **parasitic leiomyomas**)
- Histology: **bundles of smooth muscle cells** mimicking normal myometrium
- May have foci of **fibrosis, calcification, degenerative softening**

**Clinical features:**
- Often **asymptomatic** (discovered incidentally)
- Most frequent presenting sign: **menorrhagia** (with or without metrorrhagia)
- **Rarely transforms into sarcomas**
- Multiple lesions do **not increase risk of malignancy**

#### Leiomyosarcoma

**Origin:** Virtually always arises **de novo** from mesenchymal cells of myometrium
- Almost always **solitary**
- Most often occurs in **postmenopausal women**
- In contrast to leiomyomas (which are often multiple and arise premenopausally)

**Morphology:**
- **Soft, hemorrhagic, necrotic masses**
- Wide histologic variation: from resembling **leiomyoma** to **wildly anaplastic neoplasms**
- Designated **smooth muscle tumors of uncertain malignant potential** when well-differentiated

**Diagnostic features:**
- **Tumor necrosis**
- **Cytologic atypia**
- **Mitotic activity**
- Assessment of **all three features** necessary for malignancy diagnosis

**Clinical features:**
- **Recurrence after surgery** is common
- Many metastasize, typically to **lungs**
- **5-year survival rate**: about **40%**
- **Anaplastic tumors**: less favorable outlook

#### Summary: Proliferative Lesions of Endometrium and Myometrium

- **Endometrial hyperplasia** results from **unopposed endogenous or exogenous estrogen**
- Risk factors: anovulatory cycles, polycystic ovary syndrome, estrogen-producing ovarian tumor, obesity, estrogen therapy without progestin
- Hyperplasia classified based on **cytologic atypia** (determines risk of endometrioid carcinoma)
- **Endometrioid carcinoma** is associated with **estrogen excess and endometrial hyperplasia**; early molecular changes include **inactivation of DNA mismatch repair genes and PTEN gene**
- **Serous carcinoma** arises in older women; associated with **endometrial atrophy** and **serous intraepithelial carcinoma**; **TP53 gene mutations** are early events
- **Stage** is the major determinant of survival in both types
- Serous tumors tend to manifest more frequently with **extrauterine extension** and have **worse prognosis**

## Fallopian Tubes

### Salpingitis

**Definition:** Inflammation of the fallopian tubes; almost always a component of **pelvic inflammatory disease**

**Etiology:**
- **Gonorrhea**
- **Nongonococcal organisms**: Chlamydia, Mycoplasma hominis, coliforms, streptococci, staphylococci (in postpartum setting)
- **Tuberculous salpingitis**: almost always combined with **tuberculous endometritis**

**Pathology:**
- Morphologic changes from gonococci similar to those in male genital tract (Chapter 18)
- Coliforms, streptococci, staphylococci can penetrate tubal wall → **blood-borne infections** seeding to distant sites

**Clinical features:**
- **Fever, lower abdominal or pelvic pain, pelvic masses**
- Result from distention of tubes with **exudate or inflammatory debris** (Fig. 19.14)
- May produce **tuboovarian abscess** (adherence of inflamed tube to ovary)
- Resulting adhesions between ovary and tubes
- **Adhesions of tubal plicae**: increased risk of **tubal ectopic pregnancy**
- Damage to or obstruction of tubal lumina: **permanent sterility**

### Tumors of the Fallopian Tube

**Primary adenocarcinomas of the fallopian tube:**
- Once thought uncommon
- May be the site of origin for many **high-grade serous carcinomas** thought to arise in the ovary
- **Serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC)** identified in fimbriated ends of fallopian tubes
- STICs have **TP53 mutations** in >**90%** of cases
- Found frequently in tubes removed prophylactically from women with **BRCA1 and BRCA2** mutations
- Suggests that many **"ovarian" serous carcinomas** originate in the **fallopian tube**
- Because fimbriated end is intimately associated with ovary and has access to peritoneal cavity: fallopian tube carcinomas frequently involve **ovary, omentum, and peritoneal cavity** at presentation

#### Summary: Ectopic Pregnancy

- **Ectopic pregnancy** = implantation of fertilized ovum **outside the uterine corpus**
- Approximately **1%** of pregnancies implant ectopically
- Most common site: **fallopian tube** (>90%)
- Major risk factor: **chronic salpingitis with scarring**
- **Rupture of ectopic pregnancy** = medical emergency (may result in exsanguination and death)

## Ovaries

### Follicle and Luteal Cysts

**Definition:** Common variants of normal physiology
- Originate from **unruptured graafian follicles** or from follicles that rupture and then immediately seal
- Often multiple; develop subjacent to **serosa of the ovary**
- Size: typically **1–1.5 cm** in diameter
- Filled with **clear serous fluid**
- Occasionally become large (**4–5 cm**) → **palpable masses and pelvic pain**
- When small: lined by **granulosa lining cells or luteal cells**
- May rupture → **intraperitoneal bleeding** and **peritoneal symptoms (acute abdomen)**

### Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome

**Definition (formerly Stein-Leventhal syndrome):** Complex endocrine disorder characterized by:
- **Hyperandrogenism**
- **Menstrual abnormalities**
- **Polycystic ovaries**
- **Chronic anovulation**
- **Decreased fertility**

**Clinical presentation:**
- Usually comes to attention after menarche
- **Oligomenorrhea, hirsutism, infertility, obesity**

**Pathology:**
- Ovaries: usually **twice normal size**, gray-white with **smooth outer cortex**
- Studded with **subcortical cysts 0.5–1.5 cm** in diameter
- **Thickened, fibrotic ovarian capsule**
- **Innumerable cystic follicles** lined by **granulosa cells** with **hyperplastic luteinized theca interna**
- **Conspicuous absence of corpora lutea**

### Tumors of the Ovary

**Epidemiology:**
- Over **20,000 ovarian cancer cases** diagnosed annually in the U.S.
- **>14,000 deaths** annually
- **Fifth leading contributor** to cancer mortality in women

**Three cell types of origin:**
1. **Multipotent surface (coelomic) epithelium**
2. **Totipotent germ cells**
3. **Sex cord–stromal cells**

**Relative frequencies:**
- Epithelial tumors: **great majority** of ovarian tumors; **~90%** of malignant ovarian cancers
- Germ cell and sex cord–stromal cell tumors: **20%–30%** of ovarian tumors; **<10%** of malignant tumors

**Ovarian tumor pathogenesis (Fig. 19.15):**

**Type I tumors:**
- Progress from **benign tumors** through **borderline tumors** to **low-grade carcinoma**
- Include: **low-grade serous, endometrioid, mucinous**
- Associated with mutations in signaling genes (e.g., **KRAS**)

**Type II tumors:**
- Arise from **inclusion cysts/fallopian tube epithelium** via intraepithelial precursors
- Demonstrate **high-grade features**
- Most commonly **high-grade serous** histology
- **TP53 mutations** in >**95%** of cases

**Risk factors for ovarian cancer:**
- **Nulliparity**
- **Family history**
- **Germline mutations** in tumor suppressor genes (**BRCA1, BRCA2**)
- Higher incidence in **unmarried women** and married women with **low parity**
- **Prolonged use of oral contraceptives** reduces the risk
- **5%–10%** of ovarian cancers are **familial** (most associated with **BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations**)
- Lifetime risk in **BRCA1 carriers**: approximately **30%**
- **BRCA2 carriers**: somewhat lower risk
- **BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations** found in only **8%–10%** of sporadic ovarian cancers

#### Surface Epithelial Tumors

**Serous Tumors**
- **Most common** of ovarian epithelial tumors
- **Greatest fraction** of malignant ovarian tumors
- 60% benign, 15% borderline, 25% malignant
- Benign: usually in patients **30–40 years** of age
- Malignant: more common **45–65 years** of age

**Two types of serous carcinoma:**

**Low-grade serous carcinoma:**
- Arise from **benign or borderline lesions**
- Progress **slowly** in stepwise manner
- Associated with **KRAS** mutations

**High-grade serous carcinoma:**
- Develop **rapidly**
- Many arise in **fimbriated end of fallopian tube** via **STIC**
- **TP53 mutations** in >**95%** of cases
- Other mutations: **NF1, RB, BRCA1, BRCA2**

**Morphology of serous tumors:**
- Large, spherical to ovoid, **cystic structures** up to **30–40 cm** diameter
- About **25%** of benign tumors are **bilateral**
- Benign: **smooth and glistening** serosal covering
- Adenocarcinoma: **nodular irregularities** on surface
- Cut section: **single cavity** (small tumors) or **multiloculated masses** (large tumors)
- Cystic spaces filled with **clear serous fluid**
- **Papillary projections** protrude into cystic cavities (more prominent in malignant)

**Histology:**
- Benign: **single layer of columnar epithelial cells** (often ciliated); **psammoma bodies** (concentrically laminated calcified concretions) common in papillary tips
- High-grade carcinoma: **markedly atypical cells**, **complex multilayered papillary formations**, **stromal invasion**
- Borderline tumors: less cytologic atypia, typically **no stromal invasion**

**Spread:**
- Malignant serous tumors: spread throughout **peritoneal cavity** and to **regional lymph nodes** (including periaortic)
- Distant lymphatic and hematogenous metastases: **infrequent**

**Prognosis:**
- High-grade serous carcinoma: **poor prognosis** even after surgery and chemotherapy
- Depends heavily on **stage at diagnosis**
- **5-year survival** confined to one ovary: about **90%**
- Falls to **<40%** with higher-stage tumors
- Borderline tumors: **nearly 100%** survival
- Women with **BRCA1/2 mutations**: better prognosis

**Mucinous Tumors:**
- Neoplastic epithelium: **mucin-secreting cells**
- **Considerably less likely to be malignant** than serous tumors
- 80% benign, 10% borderline, 10% malignant
- Much less likely to be **bilateral** than serous tumors
- May be indistinguishable from serous tumors except by **mucinous nature of cyst contents**
- More likely to be **larger and multicystic** (Fig. 19.17A)
- **KRAS mutations** in approximately **50%** of ovarian mucinous carcinomas

**Endometrioid Tumors:**
- May be **solid or cystic**
- Sometimes develop in association with **endometriosis**
- Distinguished by formation of **tubular glands** similar to those of endometrium
- Usually **malignant**
- **Bilateral** in about **30%** of cases
- About **15%–30%** of women have **concomitant endometrial carcinoma**
- Frequently have **PTEN tumor suppressor gene mutations**
- Mutations also in genes that **upregulate PI3K-AKT signaling**

**Brenner Tumor:**
- Uncommon, **solid, usually unilateral**
- **Abundant stroma** containing nests of **transitional-type epithelium** (resembling urinary tract)
- Nests may be cystic, lined by **columnar mucus-secreting cells**
- Generally **smoothly encapsulated** and **gray-white**
- Size: a few centimeters to **20 cm** in diameter
- Most are **benign**; malignant and borderline forms described

#### Teratomas

**Definition:** Tumors with tissues derived from **all three germ cell layers**: ectoderm, endoderm, and mesoderm

**Epidemiology:**
- **15%–20%** of ovarian tumors
- Tend to arise in **first 2 decades of life**
- Younger the person, greater the likelihood of **malignancy**
- **>90%** are **benign mature cystic teratomas**; **immature malignant variant is rare**

**Benign (Mature) Cystic Teratomas (Dermoid Cysts):**
- Contain **mature tissues** derived from all three germ cell layers
- Cysts lined by **epidermis** with **adnexal appendages** → "dermoid cysts"
- Usually discovered in **young women** as ovarian masses or found incidentally on radiographs (contain **calcification** from toothlike structures)
- About **90%** are **unilateral** (right side more commonly affected)
- Rarely exceed **10 cm** in diameter
- Cut section: filled with **sebaceous secretion and matted hair**; hair-bearing epidermal lining (Fig. 19.18)
- May contain **nodular projection with teeth**
- May contain foci of **bone, cartilage, bronchial or GI epithelium**
- **Clinical complications**:
  - May produce **infertility**
  - Prone to **torsion** (10%–15%)
  - Rare paraneoplastic complication: **limbic encephalitis** (from mature neural tissue; remits with tumor resection)
  - **Malignant transformation** (usually to **squamous cell carcinoma**) in about **1%** of cases

**Immature Malignant Teratomas:**
- Found early in life (mean age: **18 years**)
- Typically **bulky** and appear **solid** on cut section
- Often with areas of **necrosis**
- Histology: **immature elements** or **minimally differentiated cartilage, bone, muscle, nerve** or other tissues
- Prognosis depends on **grade and stage**

**Specialized Teratomas:**

**Struma ovarii:**
- Composed entirely of **mature thyroid tissue**
- May produce **hyperthyroidism**
- Appear as **small, solid, unilateral brown ovarian masses**

**Ovarian carcinoid:**
- Rare
- May produce **carcinoid syndrome**

#### Clinical Features of Ovarian Tumors

**Challenge:** Management poses formidable clinical challenge because symptoms usually appear only when tumors are well advanced

**Symptoms:**
- **Epithelial tumors**: usually **asymptomatic** until large enough to cause **local pressure symptoms** (pain, gastrointestinal complaints, urinary frequency)
- About **30%** of all ovarian neoplasms discovered incidentally on routine gynecologic examination
- Larger masses: **increase in abdominal girth**
- Smaller masses (dermoid cysts): sometimes **twist on their pedicles (torsion)** → **severe abdominal pain** mimicking acute abdomen
- **Metastatic seeding** of malignant serous tumors: often causes **ascites**
- **Functioning ovarian tumors**: come to attention due to **endocrinopathies**

**Screening and tumor markers:**
- **CA-125**: elevated in sera of **75%–90%** of women with epithelial ovarian cancer
- **Undetectable** in up to **50%** of women with cancer limited to the ovary
- Often elevated in **benign conditions** and **nonovarian cancers**
- Usefulness as **screening test in asymptomatic postmenopausal women** is limited
- Greatest value: **monitoring response to therapy**

#### Summary: Ovarian Tumors

- Tumors may arise from **epithelium, sex cord–stromal cells, or germ cells**
- **Epithelial tumors** are the most common malignant ovarian tumor; more common in women **>40 years**
- Major types: **serous, mucinous, and endometrioid**; each has **benign, malignant, and borderline** counterparts
- **Serous carcinoma** is the most common; many arise in the **distal fallopian tube**
- **Sex cord–stromal tumors** may display differentiation toward **granulosa, Sertoli, Leydig, or ovarian stromal cell types**; may produce **estrogens or androgens**
- **Germ cell tumors** (mostly cystic teratomas) are the most common ovarian tumor in **young women**; vast majority are **benign**
- Germ cell tumors may differentiate toward: **oogonia (dysgerminoma)**, primitive embryonal tissue (embryonal), **yolk sac (endodermal sinus tumor)**, **placental tissue (choriocarcinoma)**, or **multiple tissue types (teratoma)**

## Diseases of Pregnancy

### Placental Inflammation and Infections

**Routes of infection:**
1. **Ascension through the birth canal** (more common)
2. **Hematogenous (transplacental) spread**

**Ascending infections:**
- By far the more common route
- Usually **bacterial**
- Associated with **premature rupture of fetal membranes**
- Morphology: chorioamnion shows **neutrophilic infiltration** with **edema and congestion** (**acute chorioamnionitis**)
- Extension beyond membranes → involvement of **umbilical cord and placental villi** → **acute vasculitis of the cord (funisitis)**
- Caused by **Mycoplasma, Candida**, and bacteria of vaginal flora

**Hematogenous infections:**
- Less common
- **Placental villi** most frequently affected (**villitis**)
- Caused by: **syphilis, tuberculosis, listeriosis, toxoplasmosis, and various viruses** (rubella, cytomegalovirus, herpes simplex virus)
- **TORCH complex**: Toxoplasmosis, Other infections, Rubella, Cytomegalovirus infection, Herpes

### Ectopic Pregnancy

**Definition:** Implantation of a **fertilized ovum** in any site **other than the uterus**

**Epidemiology:** Occurs in as many as **1%** of pregnancies

**Sites:**
- **Fallopian tube** (>90% - tubal pregnancy)
- **Ovaries**
- **Abdominal cavity**

**Risk factors:**
- Any factor that **retards passage of ovum** through fallopian tube
- In about **50%** of cases: **chronic inflammation and scarring** in oviduct
- **Intrauterine tumors** and **endometriosis** may also hamper passage
- In other 50%: no anatomic cause evident
- **Ovarian pregnancies**: probably from fertilization just as follicle ruptures
- **Abdominal pregnancy**: fertilized egg drops out of fimbriated end of fallopian tube and implants on peritoneum

**Morphology:**
- Early development proceeds normally: formation of **placental tissue, amniotic sac, decidual changes**
- With tubal pregnancies: **placenta eventually burrows through wall of fallopian tube**
- Results in: **intratubal hematoma (hematosalpinx)**, **intraperitoneal hemorrhage**, or both
- Tube distended by **freshly clotted blood** containing bits of gray placental tissue and fetal parts
- Diagnosis: visualization of **placental villi** or rarely **embryo**

**Clinical features:**
- Until rupture: may be indistinguishable from normal pregnancy
- **Cessation of menstruation** and **elevation of serum and urinary placental hormones**
- Endometrium may show **hypersecretory and decidual changes of pregnancy** (in approximately 50% of cases)
- **Absence of elevated gonadotropin levels** does not exclude diagnosis
- **Rupture**: sudden onset of **intense abdominal pain**, signs of **acute abdomen**, often followed by **shock**
- **Prompt surgical intervention** necessary

### Gestational Trophoblastic Disease

**Definition:** Abnormal proliferation of **fetal trophoblast cells**

**Classification (WHO):**

**Molar lesions:**
- **Partial hydatidiform mole**
- **Complete hydatidiform mole**
- **Invasive mole**

**Nonmolar lesions:**
- **Choriocarcinoma**
- Other uncommon types of trophoblast-derived malignancies

**Key feature:** All elaborate **human chorionic gonadotropins (hCG)** - detected in blood and urine at levels considerably higher than normal pregnancy

**Clinical use of hCG:** Monitor **treatment efficacy**

#### Hydatidiform Mole: Complete and Partial

**Definition:** Voluminous mass of **swollen, sometimes cystically dilated, chorionic villi** appearing as **grapelike structures**

**Complete Mole:**
- **Not compatible with embryogenesis**; rarely contains fetal parts
- **All villi are abnormal**
- Diploid (46,XX or 46,XY)
- All chorionic epithelial cells are **diploid** with **only paternal chromosomes**
- Results from: two spermatozoa (or a diploid sperm) fertilizing an **empty egg**

**Partial Mole:**
- **Compatible with early embryo formation**; may contain fetal parts
- Some **normal chorionic villi**
- Almost always **triploid** (69,XXY)
- Results from: normal egg fertilized by **two spermatozoa** (or a diploid sperm)
- Has **two sets of paternal chromosomes** and one set of maternal chromosomes

**Table 19.5: Features of Complete and Partial Hydatidiform Mole**

| Feature | Complete Mole | Partial Mole |
|---------|--------------|-------------|
| **Karyotype** | 46,XX (46,XY) | Triploid (69,XXY) |
| **Villous edema** | All villi | Some villi |
| **Trophoblast proliferation** | Diffuse; circumferential | Focal; slight |
| **Serum hCG** | Elevated ++++ | Less elevated + |
| **Tissue hCG** | Elevated | Less elevated |
| **Risk of subsequent choriocarcinoma** | 2% | Rare |

**Epidemiology:**
- Complete mole: about **1–1.5 per 2000 pregnancies** in Western countries
- Much higher incidence in **Asian countries**
- Most common **before age 20** and **after age 40**

**Morphology (Complete Mole - Fig. 19.19, 19.20):**
- Uterine cavity expanded by **delicate, friable mass of thin-walled, translucent cystic structures**
- **Fetal parts rarely seen**
- Microscopy: **hydropic swelling** of **poorly vascularized chorionic villi** with **loose, myxomatous, edematous stroma**
- Chorionic epithelium: **proliferation of both cytotrophoblasts and syncytiotrophoblasts**

**Partial Mole:**
- Villous edema involves only a **subset of villi**
- Trophoblastic proliferation: **focal and slight**
- Some fetal cells present in most cases

**Clinical features:**
- Formerly discovered at **12–14 weeks** during investigation for "too large for dates" gestation
- Early monitoring by **ultrasound** has lowered gestational age at detection
- Both complete and partial moles: **elevation of hCG** and **absence of fetal heart sounds**

**Prognosis:**
- **80%–90%** of moles do **not recur** after thorough curettage
- **10%** of complete moles are **invasive**
- Only **2%–3%** give rise to **choriocarcinoma**

#### Invasive Mole

**Definition:** Complete moles that are **locally invasive** but **lack metastatic potential** of choriocarcinoma

**Features:**
- Retain **hydropic villi** that penetrate uterine wall deeply
- May cause **rupture** and **life-threatening hemorrhage**
- Microscopy: epithelium of villi shows **atypical changes** with proliferation of both trophoblastic and syncytiotrophoblast components
- **Hydropic villi may embolize** to distant organs (lungs, brain) but **do not behave like true metastases** and may **regress spontaneously**
- Difficult to remove completely by curettage
- If **serum β-hCG remains elevated**: further treatment required
- Most cases: **cure possible with chemotherapy**

#### Gestational Choriocarcinoma

**Definition:** Very **aggressive malignant tumor** arising from **gestational chorionic epithelium**

**Epidemiology:**
- Rare in Western Hemisphere: about **1 per 30,000 pregnancies** in U.S.
- Much more common in **Asian and African countries**: 1 per 2000 pregnancies

**Origin:**
- Approximately **50%**: from **complete hydatidiform moles**
- Approximately **25%**: after an **abortion**
- Remainder: following **apparently normal pregnancy**

**Clinical features:**
- **Bloody, brownish discharge**
- **Rising titer of β-hCG** in blood and urine
- **Absence of marked uterine enlargement** (unlike mole)

**Morphology:**
- Usually appear as **hemorrhagic, necrotic uterine masses**
- Sometimes necrosis so extensive that little viable tumor remains
- Primary lesion may "self-destruct" with only **metastases** revealing the diagnosis
- Early: insinuates into **myometrium and vessels**
- **Chorionic villi are NOT formed**
- Composed of **anaplastic cuboidal cytotrophoblasts** and **syncytiotrophoblasts** (Fig. 19.21)

**Spread:**
- **Widespread vascular spread** by time of discovery
- Lung: 50%
- Vagina: 30%–40%
- Brain, liver, or kidneys
- **Lymphatic invasion** is uncommon

**Prognosis:**
- **Remarkably sensitive to chemotherapy**
- Nearly **100%** of affected patients are **cured**, even those with distant metastases
- **Gonadal choriocarcinomas** (ovary or testis): relatively **poor response** to chemotherapy
- Difference may be related to **paternal antigens** on placental choriocarcinomas

#### Placental Site Trophoblastic Tumor

**Definition:** Derived from **placental site or intermediate trophoblast**

**Features:**
- Uncommon, **diploid tumors** (often XX)
- Typically arise a few months after pregnancy
- Produce only **slightly elevated hCG** (intermediate trophoblasts do not produce hCG in large amounts)
- More typically produce **human placental lactogen**
- **Indolent clinical course**
- Generally **favorable outcome** if confined to **endomyometrium**
- **Not as sensitive to chemotherapy** as other trophoblastic tumors
- **Poor prognosis** when spread occurs beyond uterus

#### Summary: Gestational Trophoblastic Disease

- **Molar disease** results from **abnormal contribution of paternal chromosomes** to the conceptus
- **Partial moles** are **triploid** with two sets of paternal chromosomes; typically accompanied by fetal tissue; **low rate of persistent disease**
- **Complete moles** are **diploid** with all chromosomes paternal; rarely associated with embryonic or fetal tissues
- Among complete moles: **10%–15%** associated with **persistent disease** (usually invasive mole); only **2%** progress to **choriocarcinoma**
- **Gestational choriocarcinoma** is **highly invasive and frequently metastatic** but **responsive to chemotherapy** and curable in most cases
- **Placental site trophoblastic tumor** is an **indolent tumor** of intermediate trophoblast; can be cured surgically but does **not respond well to chemotherapy** once it spreads

### Preeclampsia/Eclampsia (Toxemia of Pregnancy)

**Definition:** Development of **hypertension, proteinuria, and edema** in the **third trimester**

**Epidemiology:**
- Occurs in **5%–10%** of pregnancies
- Particularly with **first pregnancies** in women **older than 35 years**
- **Eclampsia**: when **seizures** occur

**Note:** "Toxemia of pregnancy" is a **misnomer** - no blood-borne toxin has been identified

**Pathogenesis:**
- **Common feature**: insufficient maternal blood flow to placenta secondary to **inadequate remodeling of spiral arteries** of uteroplacental vascular bed
- In normal pregnancy: trophoblasts invade spiral arteries, permitting them to dilate into **wide vascular sinusoids**
- In preeclampsia/eclampsia: **vascular remodeling is impaired**; musculoelastic walls are retained; channels remain **narrow**
- Results in: **decreased uteroplacental blood flow** → **placental hypoxia** → **placental dysfunction**
- Altered release of circulating factors regulating angiogenesis:
  - **Increases** in anti-angiogenic factors: **soluble Flt1 (sFlt1)** and **soluble endoglin (sEng)**
  - **Reductions** in proangiogenic factors: **VEGF**
- Results in: **endothelial cell dysfunction**, **vascular hyperreactivity**, **end-organ microangiopathy**

**Serious consequences:**
- **Placental infarction** (chronic hyperperfusion)
- **Hypertension**: reduced endothelial production of vasodilators (**prostacyclin, prostaglandin E2**) and increased production of vasoconstrictor (**thromboxane A2**)
- **Hypercoagulability**: endothelial dysfunction and release of **tissue factor** from placenta
- **End-organ failure**: kidney and liver (in full-blown eclampsia)
- Approximately **10%** of patients with severe preeclampsia develop **HELLP syndrome**:
  - **E**levated **L**iver **E**nzymes
  - **M**icroangiopathic **H**emolytic **A**nemia
  - **T**hrombocytopenia (platelet consumption)
  - Sometimes **disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)**

**Morphology:**

**Placental abnormalities:**
- **Infarcts** (more numerous with severe preeclampsia or eclampsia)
- **Retroplacental hemorrhages**
- **Premature maturation of placental villi** (villous edema, hypovascularity, increased syncytial epithelial knots)
- **Fibrinoid necrosis** and focal accumulation of **lipid-containing macrophages (acute atherosis)** of decidual vessels

**Clinical features:**
- Insidious onset at weeks **24–25** of gestation
- **Edema, proteinuria, rising blood pressure**
- Evolution into eclampsia: **renal function impairment**, **further rise in blood pressure**, **convulsions**
- **Prompt therapy** early in course aborts associated organ changes
- **All abnormalities resolve promptly after delivery** or cesarean section

## Breast

### Clinical Presentations of Breast Disease

**Predominant symptoms and signs:**
- **Pain**
- **Inflammatory changes**
- **Nipple discharge**
- **"Lumpiness"**
- **Palpable mass** (Fig. 19.23A)

**Important principle:** Most symptomatic breast lesions (>90%) are **benign**

**Breast cancer presentations (Fig. 19.23B):**
- About **45%** of women with cancer have symptoms
- Remainder: come to attention through **screening tests**

**Symptom analysis:**

**Pain (mastalgia/mastodynia):**
- Common symptom often related to **menses** (cyclic edema and swelling)
- **Localized pain**: usually caused by **ruptured cyst** or **fat necrosis**
- Almost all painful masses are **benign**
- About **10%** of cancers cause pain

**Inflammation:**
- Rare; most often caused by **infections** (only during **lactation and breastfeeding**)
- Important mimic: **"inflammatory" breast carcinoma** (discussed later)

**Nipple discharge:**
- Normal when **small in quantity** and **bilateral**
- Most common benign lesion: **papilloma** in large ducts below nipple
- Discharge of concern: **spontaneous, unilateral, and bloody**

**Lumpiness:**
- Usually results from **normal glandular tissue**
- **Pronounced lumpiness**: imaging studies help determine if **discrete mass** present

**Palpable masses:**
- Arise from proliferations of **stromal cells or epithelial cells**
- Generally detected when **2–3 cm** in size
- Most (~95%): **benign** (round to oval, **circumscribed borders**)
- Malignant tumors: usually **irregular borders** (invade across tissue planes)
- All palpable masses require evaluation

**Gynecomastia:**
- Only common breast symptom in **males**
- Increase in both **stroma and epithelial cells**
- Results from **imbalance between estrogens and androgens**

**Mammographic screening:**
- Introduced in 1980s to detect **early, nonpalpable asymptomatic breast carcinomas**
- Average size of invasive carcinomas detected by mammography: about **1 cm** (significantly smaller than palpable cancers)
- Only **15%** have metastasized to regional lymph nodes at diagnosis
- Most cancers in women **>50 years** are now detected by mammography
- Likelihood that abnormal mammographic finding is malignancy: increases with age (**10%** at age 40 to **>25%** at age >50)

#### Summary: Clinical Presentations of Breast Disease

- Symptoms affecting breasts are evaluated primarily to determine if **malignancy is present**
- Regardless of symptom, underlying cause is **benign in the majority of cases**
- **Breast cancer** most commonly detected by **palpation** in younger women and unscreened populations; by **mammographic screening** in older women

### Inflammatory Processes

**Definition:** Rare; caused by **infections, autoimmune disease, or foreign body–type reactions**

**Symptoms:** **Erythema and edema**, often with **pain and focal tenderness**

**Important consideration:** Because inflammatory diseases are rare, possibility that symptoms are caused by **inflammatory carcinoma** should always be considered

**Infectious mastitis:**
- Only **Staphylococcus aureus** causes breast disease with any frequency
- Entry via **fissures in nipple skin** during **first weeks of breastfeeding**
- May lead to **"lactational abscesses"** (collections of neutrophils and bacteria in fibroadipose tissue)
- If untreated: **tissue necrosis** → **fistula tracks** opening onto skin
- Treatment: **antibiotics** and continued milk expression
- Rarely: **surgical incision and drainage** required

### Stromal Neoplasms

**Two types of breast stroma:**

**1. Intralobular stroma** → gives rise to:
- **Fibroadenomas** (benign)
- **Phyllodes tumors** (sometimes recur, rarely malignant)

**2. Interlobular stroma** → gives rise to:
- **Hemangiomas** (Chapter 10)
- **Lipomas** (Chapter 21)
- **Angiosarcoma** (Chapter 21) - may arise after local radiotherapy

**Note:** These tumors share **driver mutations in the same genes** and appear to be part of a spectrum of related neoplasms

**Morphology of intralobular stromal tumors:**
- **Biphasic** (both stromal cells and epithelial cells)
- Neoplastic proliferation of specialized lobular fibroblasts stimulates **reactive proliferation of lobular epithelial cells**
- Fibroblasts push and distort epithelial cells → **elongated slitlike structures** (not round acini)

**Fibroadenoma (Fig. 19.24A):**
- **Circumscribed borders**
- **Low cellularity**
- **Rare mitoses**

**Phyllodes tumors (Fig. 19.24B):**
- Stromal cells outgrow epithelial cells → **bulbous nodules** covered by epithelium
- Characteristic **"phyllodes" (leaflike) growth pattern**
- **High-grade phyllodes tumors**: epithelium may be scant/absent → **sarcomatous appearance**
- About **2%** of phyllodes tumors **metastasize to distant sites**

---

### Benign Epithelial Lesions

**Clinical significance:** Major importance is their **relationship to subsequent risk of developing breast cancer**

**Classification:**

**1. Nonproliferative disease:**
- **Not associated with increased risk** of breast cancer

**2. Proliferative disease without atypia:**
- Associated with **slightly increased risk** of breast cancer

**3. Proliferative disease with atypia:**
- Associated with **modest increase** in risk of breast cancer
- **13%–17%** of women with these lesions develop breast cancer

**Morphology:**

**Nonproliferative disease (Fig. 19.25A):**
- Three major morphologic changes: **cysts, fibrosis, adenosis**
- Termed "nonproliferative" because lesions contain **single layers of epithelial cells**
- Most common: **simple cysts** lined by a layer of luminal cells often undergoing **apocrine metaplasia**
- Apocrine secretions may **calcify** (detected by mammography)
- When cysts rupture: **chronic inflammation and fibrosis** → palpable nodularity ("**fibrocystic changes**")

**Proliferative disease without atypia:**
- Includes: **epithelial hyperplasia, sclerosing adenosis, complex sclerosing lesion, papilloma**
- Varying degrees of **epithelial cell proliferation**
- In epithelial hyperplasia: increased numbers of both **spindled myoepithelial cells** and **epithelioid luminal cells** expand ductal and lobular spaces (Fig. 19.25B)

**Proliferative disease with atypia (Fig. 19.25C):**
- Includes: **Atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH)** and **Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH)**
- ALH closely resembles **lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)**
- ADH closely resembles **ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)**
- Cells in ADH: **uniform in appearance**; form **sharply marginated spaces or rigid bridges**

### Breast Carcinoma

**Epidemiology:**
- **Most common malignancy** of women globally (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer)
- Causes **majority of cancer deaths** in women
- **Lifetime risk**: 1 in 8 for women living to age 90 in the U.S.
- Predicted: about **250,000 breast cancers** diagnosed in 2016; about **40,000 deaths**
- Second only to **lung cancer** in cancer deaths
- Since mid-1980s: mortality rate dropped from **30% to <20%** (improved screening and more effective systemic treatment)

**Almost all breast malignancies** are **adenocarcinomas** (>95%)

**Classification based on hormone receptor and HER2 status:**

**1. ER positive (HER2 negative):** 50%–65% of cancers
**2. HER2 positive (ER positive or negative):** 10%–20% of cancers
**3. Triple negative (ER, PR, and HER2 negative):** 10%–20% of cancers

**Gene expression profiling classification:**
- **Luminal A**: majority are lower-grade ER-positive cancers that are HER2 negative
- **Luminal B**: majority are higher-grade ER-positive cancers that may be HER2 positive
- **HER2-enriched**: majority overexpress HER2 and do not express ER
- **Basal-like**: majority resemble basally located myoepithelial cells; ER-negative, HER2-negative

|---------|--------------------------|---------------|-----------------|

#### Epidemiology and Risk Factors

**Age and Gender:**
- Rare in women **younger than 25**
- Incidence increases rapidly after age 30
- **75%** of women with breast cancer are **older than 50**
- Only **5%** are **younger than 40**
- Incidence in men: only **1%** of that in women

**Family History:**
- Greatest risk: individuals with **multiple affected first-degree relatives** with **early-onset breast cancer**
- Approximately **5%–10%** of breast cancers occur in persons with **highly penetrant germline mutations**
- Lifetime risk may be **>90%** for these individuals

**Geographic Factors:**
- Significantly higher risk in **Americas and Europe** than in **Asia and Africa**
- Five times higher in U.S. than in Japan
- Migrants from low-incidence to high-incidence areas acquire rates of new home countries
- **Diet, reproductive patterns, breastfeeding practices** thought to be involved

**Race/Ethnicity:**
- Highest rate: women of **European descent** (largely due to higher incidence of **ER-positive cancers**)
- **Hispanic and African American women**: tend to develop cancer at **younger age** and more likely to develop **aggressive tumors**

**Reproductive History:**
- **Early age at menarche**
- **Nulliparity**
- **Absence of breastfeeding**
- **Older age at first pregnancy**
- All associated with increased risk (increase exposure of "at-risk" breast epithelial cells to **estrogenic stimulation**)

**Ionizing Radiation:**
- Chest radiation increases risk if exposure occurs while breast is **still developing**
- **25%–30%** of women irradiated for **Hodgkin lymphoma** in teens and 20s develop breast cancer
- Risk not elevated for women treated later in life

**Other Risk Factors:**
- **Postmenopausal obesity**
- **Postmenopausal hormone replacement**
- **Mammographic density**
- **Alcohol consumption**

#### Pathogenesis

**Three major subtypes arise through more-or-less distinct pathways** involving stepwise acquisition of driver mutations

**Genetic factors:**

**Germline mutations:**
- **BRCA1 and BRCA2**: classic tumor suppressor genes
  - Encode proteins required for **repair of certain kinds of DNA damage**
  - Lifetime risk for breast cancer: approximately **30%** in BRCA1 carriers; somewhat lower in **BRCA2 carriers**
  - For average-risk women: about **12%**
  - **BRCA2 mutations** primarily associated with **ER-positive tumors**
  - **BRCA1 mutations** show strong association with **triple-negative cancers** (Fig. 19.27)
- **TP53** mutations: part of Cowden syndrome (with **PTEN**)
- **PTEN**: important negative regulator of pro-growth **PI3K-AKT pathway**

**Somatic mutations:**
- **BRCA1** inactivated by methylation in up to **50%** of triple-negative cancers
- **TP53** mutations common in breast cancer (particularly **triple-negative and HER2-positive tumors**)
- **PIK3CA** mutations (activating): frequently found in **sporadic ER-positive and HER2-positive** breast cancers

**HER2 amplification:**
- **Common clinically important driver mutation**
- **HER2**: receptor tyrosine kinase that promotes cell proliferation and opposes apoptosis
- Cancers that overexpress HER2 are **pathogenically distinct** and **highly proliferative**
- **Therapeutic agents that target HER2** have markedly improved prognosis

**Hormonal influences:**
- **Estrogens** stimulate production of growth factors (**TGF-α, PDGF, FGF**) → promote tumor development through **paracrine and autocrine mechanisms**
- **ER** regulates dozens of genes in estrogen-dependent fashion
- Hormonal influences likely drive proliferation during development from **precursor lesions** (which typically strongly express ER) to fully **malignant and metastatic carcinomas**
- **Estrogen antagonists** reduce development of ER-positive cancers in high-risk women and are **mainstays** in treatment of established ER-positive tumors

**Environmental factors:**
- Variable incidence in genetically homogeneous groups
- Geographic differences suggest environmental influence

#### Morphology

**Location:**
- Most common: **upper outer quadrant** (50%)
- Central portion: 20%
- **4%** of women with breast cancer have **bilateral primary tumors** or sequential lesions in same breast

**Classification:**

**A. Noninvasive (in situ) carcinoma**

**1. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS):**
- Wide variety of histologic appearances: **solid, comedo, cribriform, papillary, micropapillary, "clinging"**
- Nuclear appearances: from **bland and monotonous (low nuclear grade)** to **pleomorphic (high nuclear grade)**
- **Comedo subtype**: characterized by **extensive central necrosis** (toothpastelike necrotic tissue)
- **Calcifications frequently associated** with DCIS (from calcification of necrotic debris or secretory material)
- Constitutes only **5%** of breast cancers in unscreened populations but up to **30%** in screened populations
- Treatment: **surgery and irradiation** ± **anti-estrogenic agents** (tamoxifen) for ER-positive DCIS
- Prognosis: **excellent** (>97% long-term survival)
- If untreated: progresses to invasive cancer in roughly **one-third** of cases

**2. Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS):**
- **Uniform appearance**: monomorphic cells with **bland, round nuclei**
- Found in **loosely cohesive clusters within lobules** (Fig. 19.28A)
- Virtually always an **incidental finding**
- Approximately **one-third** of women with LCIS eventually develop **invasive carcinoma**
- Unlike DCIS, invasive carcinomas following LCIS may arise in **either breast** (two-thirds same breast, one-third contralateral)
- LCIS is both a **marker of increased risk** of carcinoma in both breasts **and a direct precursor** of some cancers

**Paget disease of the nipple:**
- Caused by **extension of DCIS** up lactiferous ducts into skin of nipple
- Produces **unilateral crusting exudate** over nipple and areolar skin
- Unlike vulvar Paget disease: stems from **in situ extension of an underlying carcinoma**
- Prognosis determined by **other factors** of the carcinoma of origin

**B. Invasive (infiltrating) carcinoma**

**1. Invasive ductal carcinoma (70%–80%):**
- All carcinomas that cannot be subclassified into specialized types
- Usually associated with **DCIS**
- Microscopic appearance varies: from **well-developed tubules and low-grade nuclei** to **sheets of anaplastic cells**
- Most produce a **desmoplastic response**
- **50%–65%**: ER positive
- **20%**: HER2 positive
- **15%**: negative for ER and HER2 (triple-negative)

**2. Invasive lobular carcinoma (10%–15%):**
- Cells morphologically similar to LCIS tumor cells
- Two-thirds associated with **LCIS**
- Invade stroma **individually** and often aligned in **"single-file"** (Fig. 19.29C)
- May invade **without producing desmoplastic response** (difficult to detect by imaging)
- Unique metastatic pattern: frequently spread to **CSF, serosal surfaces, GI tract, ovary, uterus, and bone marrow**
- Almost all express **hormone receptors**; HER2 overexpression is **rare**

**3. Carcinomas with medullary features (~5%):**
- Special type of **triple-negative cancer**
- Grow as **rounded masses** (may mimic benign tumors on imaging)
- Consist of **sheets of large anaplastic cells** with **pronounced lymphocytic infiltrates** (predominantly T cells)
- **Lymphocytes** associated with **favorable prognosis** (better response to chemotherapy)
- Frequently seen in women with **germline BRCA1 mutations**

**4. Mucinous (colloid) carcinoma:**
- **ER-positive/HER2-negative** tumor
- Produces abundant **extracellular mucin**
- Usually **soft and gelatinous**; creates **expansile circumscribed mass**

**5. Tubular carcinoma:**
- **ER-positive/HER2-negative** cancer
- Almost always detected on mammography as **small irregular mass**
- Tumor cells arranged in **well-formed tubules** with **low-grade nuclei**
- **Rare** lymph node metastases; **excellent prognosis**

**6. Inflammatory carcinoma:**
- Defined by **clinical presentation**, not specific morphology
- **Swollen erythematous breast** without palpable mass
- Underlying invasive carcinoma: **poorly differentiated**, infiltrates and **obstructs dermal lymphatic spaces**
- Causes "inflamed" appearance; **true inflammation absent**
- Many metastasize to distant sites
- **5-year survival <50%**
- About half express **ER**; **40%–60%** overexpress **HER2**

**Grading (all invasive carcinomas):**
- Grade 1 (low-grade) to Grade 3 (high-grade)
- Based on: **nuclear pleomorphism**, **tubule formation**, **proliferation**
- **Low-grade nuclei**: similar to normal cell nuclei
- **High-grade nuclei**: enlarged with **irregular nuclear contours**
- **Proliferation**: evaluated by **counting mitotic figures**
- Majority of **HER2-positive and triple-negative** carcinomas are **highly proliferative**
- **ER-positive** cancers show **wide range of proliferation**

#### Clinical Features

**Detection:**
- In **unscreened populations**: most breast cancers detected as **palpable mass** by patient
- Almost all invasive; typically **2–3 cm** in size
- At least half have spread to **regional lymph nodes**
- In **older screened populations**: approximately **60%** discovered **before symptoms** (about 20% are in situ carcinomas)
- Invasive carcinomas detected by screening in older women: **1–2 cm** in size; only **15%** have metastasized to lymph nodes

**Factors influencing outcome:**

**1. Biologic type:**
- Evaluated by: **histologic appearance, grade (including proliferative rate), expression of hormone receptors, HER2 expression**
- **Proliferation**: evaluated by **mitotic count**; closely tied to **responsiveness to cytotoxic chemotherapy**
- **ER/PR expression**: predicts response to **anti-estrogen therapy**
- **Triple-negative cancers**: no targeted therapy available; treated with **chemotherapy**
- **HER2 overexpression** (~20%): best-characterized example of effective **targeted therapy**; **complete response rates exceed 60%** with targeted therapy + chemotherapy

**2. RNA expression profiling:**
- Newer method of subclassifying cancers
- Greatest clinical value: identifying patients with **slow-growing, anti-estrogen-responsive cancers** who can be **spared toxicity of chemotherapy**

**3. Tumor stage:**
- Based on **AJCC/UICC staging system**:
  - **T** (primary tumor): T1, T2, T3 based on size; T4 (ulceration, chest wall involvement, inflammatory carcinoma)
  - **N** (regional lymph nodes): based on nodal involvement
  - **M** (distant metastases)
- **Stage 0**: CIS (>95% survival)
- **Stage I**: smaller cancers, nodes free or micrometastases only (~86% 10-year survival)
- **Stage II**: larger size or up to three positive nodes (~71% 10-year survival)
- **Stage III**: locally advanced (large size, skin/chest wall involvement, ≥4 positive nodes) (~54% 10-year survival)
- **Stage IV**: distant metastases (~11% survival)

**Metastatic patterns vary by biologic type:**
- **Triple-negative and HER2 cancers**: more likely to metastasize to **brain and viscera**
- **ER-positive cancers**: most often metastasize to **bones** (Table 19.7)

**Table 19.8: Targeted Treatment of Breast Cancer**

| Target | Treatment | Assay | Comments |
|--------|-----------|-------|----------|
| **ER** | Estrogen deprivation (oophorectomy, aromatase inhibitors); Blockage of ER (tamoxifen) | IHC for nuclear ER | Effective cytostatic (but not cytotoxic) |
| **HER2** | Antibodies to HER2; Tyrosine kinase inhibitors; Chemotherapy linked to HER2 antibody | IHC for membrane HER2; ISH for HER2 gene amplification | Effective for HER2-positive cancers |

#### Summary: Breast Carcinoma

- **Lifetime risk** of developing breast cancer for an American woman: **1 in 8**
- **75%** of breast cancers diagnosed after age 50
- Major risk factors: **hormonal factors and inherited susceptibility**
- About **12%** of breast cancers caused by identified **germline mutations**; **BRCA1 and BRCA2** account for one-half of cases with single-gene mutations
- **DCIS** is a **precursor to invasive ductal carcinoma**; most often found on mammographic screening as **calcifications**
- **LCIS** is both a **marker of increased risk** and a **precursor lesion**
- **Invasive carcinomas** classified according to histologic type and biologic type: **ER-positive/HER2-negative, HER2-positive, and triple-negative**; these types have important differences in **patient characteristics, grade, mutation profile, metastatic pattern, response to therapy, time to recurrence, and prognosis**
- Prognosis dependent on **biologic type of tumor, stage, and availability of treatment modalities**
