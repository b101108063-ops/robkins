# Chapter 02: Cell Injury, Cell Death, and Adaptations

---

## Introduction

Cell injury and cell death are central to the understanding of all disease processes. These phenomena result from diverse causes including **ischemia** (lack of blood flow), **infections**, **toxins**, and **immune reactions**. Cell death is also a normal and essential process in **embryogenesis**, organ development, and tissue **homeostasis**.

Because damage to cells is the basis of all disease, this chapter covers:

- **Causes, mechanisms, and consequences** of acute cell injury (reversible injury and cell death)
- **Cellular adaptations** to stress
- **Deposition of abnormal substances** in cells and tissues
- **Cell aging**

### Sequence of Reversible Cell Injury and Cell Death

```
NORMAL CELL (homeostasis)
    ↓ Injurious stimulus
REVERSIBLE INJURY
    ↓ Severe, progressive
IRREVERSIBLE INJURY
    ↓
NECROSIS  or  APOPTOSIS  or  CELL DEATH
```

*Note: Figure 2.1 shows steps in the evolution of disease; Figure 2.2 illustrates the sequence from reversible injury to cell death.*

## Causes of Cell Injury

**ETIOLOGY (Causes of Disease):**

- **Hypoxia and ischemia** — oxygen deficiency and reduced blood supply; most common cause of cell injury
- **Toxins** — environmental pollutants, insecticides, CO, asbestos, cigarette smoke, ethanol, drugs
- **Infectious agents** — viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoans
- **Immunologic reactions** — autoimmune reactions, allergic reactions, excessive immune responses
- **Genetic abnormalities** — congenital malformations (Down syndrome), single amino acid substitutions (sickle cell anemia)
- **Nutritional imbalances** — protein-calorie insufficiency, vitamin deficiencies, or excessive dietary intake (obesity)
- **Physical agents** — trauma, temperature extremes, radiation, electric shock, atmospheric pressure changes
- **Aging** — diminished ability to respond to stress, eventual cell and organism death

## Sequence of Events in Cell Injury and Cell Death

### Reversible Cell Injury

Reversible injury is the stage at which deranged function and morphology can return to normal if the damaging stimulus is removed.

**Key features:**

- **Cellular swelling** — cells take in water due to failure of energy-dependent ion pumps in the plasma membrane
- **Fatty change** — appearance of triglyceride-containing lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm (mainly in liver)
- **Cytoplasmic changes** — cells may become redder (eosinophilic)
- **Organelle changes:**
  - Mitochondrial swelling with phospholipid-rich amorphous densities
  - Dilation of ER with detachment of ribosomes
  - Dissociation of polysomes
- **Nuclear alterations** — clumping of chromatin
- **"Myelin figures"** — collections of phospholipids derived from damaged membranes

**Smooth ER Adaptation:**
- Cells exposed to chemicals (e.g., barbiturates) show **hypertrophy of the smooth ER** as an adaptive response
- Cytochrome P-450 mixed-function oxidase system metabolizes these chemicals
- Protracted use leads to tolerance and increased drug-metabolizing capacity

### Transition to Irreversible Injury

**"Point of no return"** — characterized by:

1. **Inability to restore mitochondrial function** (ATP generation) even after resolution of injury
2. **Loss of plasma membrane and intracellular membrane structure and function**
3. **Loss of DNA and chromatin structural integrity**

### Cell Death

Two major pathways:

| Feature | **Necrosis** | **Apoptosis** |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| **Cell size** | Enlarged (swelling) | Reduced (shrinkage) |
| **Nucleus** | Pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis | Fragmentation into nucleosome-sized fragments |
| **Plasma membrane** | Disrupted | Intact; altered structure |
| **Cellular contents** | Enzymatic digestion; may leak | Intact; released in apoptotic bodies |
| **Adjacent inflammation** | Frequent | No |
| **Role** | Invariably pathologic (irreversible cell injury) | Often physiologic (eliminating unwanted cells); may be pathologic after DNA/protein damage |

**Additional distinctions:**
- **Necrosis** = "accidental" cell death; severe disturbances (loss of oxygen, toxins); not regulated
- **Apoptosis** = "regulated" cell death; can be manipulated by therapeutic agents or genetic mutations
- **Necroptosis** = regulated cell death with features of both necrosis and apoptosis

**Important:** Cellular function may be lost long before cell death occurs. Morphologic changes lag far behind loss of function and viability.

*Example:* Myocardial cells become noncontractile after 1–2 minutes of ischemia but may not die until 20–30 minutes of ischemia.

## Necrosis

Necrosis is a form of cell death in which cellular membranes break down, and cellular enzymes leak out and digest the cell. Necrosis elicits **inflammation** induced by substances released from dead cells.

### Biochemical Mechanisms of Necrosis

- **Failure of ATP generation** (reduced oxygen supply or mitochondrial damage)
- **Damage to cellular membranes** (plasma and lysosomal membranes) — leakage of cellular contents
- **Irreversible damage** to cellular lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids (often via **reactive oxygen species [ROS]**)

### Morphology

**Cytoplasmic changes:**
- **Increased eosinophilia** (cells stained red by eosin dye)
- **Glassy, homogeneous appearance** (loss of glycogen particles)
- **Prominent myelin figures**
- **Vacuoalted, "moth-eaten" cytoplasm** after enzyme digestion of organelles

**Nuclear changes (three patterns):**
- **Pyknosis** — nuclear shrinkage, increased basophilia, DNA condenses into dark shrunken mass
- **Karyorrhexis** — fragmentation of pyknotic nucleus
- **Karyolysis** — basophilia fades due to digestion of DNA by DNase; nucleus may disappear in 1–2 days

**Fates of necrotic cells:**
- May persist or be digested by enzymes
- Phagocytosed by other cells or degraded into fatty acids
- Fatty acids may bind calcium salts → **calcification**

### Morphologic Patterns of Tissue Necrosis

#### Coagulative Necrosis

- **Underlying tissue architecture is preserved** for at least several days
- Affected tissues become **firm**
- **Denaturation of structural proteins AND enzymes** blocks proteolysis
- Eosinophilic, anucleate cells may persist for days or weeks
- **Characteristic of infarcts** in all solid organs **except the brain**
- Dead cells digested by lysosomal enzymes of infiltrating neutrophils and macrophages

#### Liquefactive Necrosis

- Seen in **focal bacterial and fungal infections**
- Microbes stimulate rapid accumulation of inflammatory cells
- Enzymes of leukocytes digest ("liquefy") tissue
- Also occurs in **hypoxic death of CNS cells** (for obscure reasons)
- Dead cells completely digested → viscous liquid removed by phagocytes
- In acute bacterial infections: material is creamy yellow = **pus**

#### Gangrenous Necrosis

- Refers to a **limb (usually lower leg)** that has lost blood supply and undergone **coagulative necrosis** involving multiple tissue layers
- When bacterial infection is superimposed → **"wet gangrene"** (liquefactive necrosis features)

#### Caseous Necrosis

- Most often in **tuberculous infection**
- "Caseous" = "cheeselike" — friable yellow-white appearance on gross examination
- **Tissue architecture is completely obliterated**; cellular outlines cannot be discerned
- Necrotic focus has amorphous granular pink appearance in H&E-stained sections
- Often surrounded by **macrophages and inflammatory cells** forming a **granuloma**

#### Fat Necrosis

- Focal areas of fat destruction
- Typically from release of activated **pancreatic lipases** into pancreas and peritoneal cavity
- Occurs in **acute pancreatitis**
- Pancreatic enzymes liquefy membranes of fat cells; lipases split triglyceride esters
- Released fatty acids combine with calcium → **chalky white areas (fat saponification)**
- Histology: shadowy outlines of necrotic fat cells surrounded by basophilic calcium deposits

#### Fibrinoid Necrosis

- Special form occurring in **immune reactions**
- **Immune complexes deposited in blood vessel walls** OR in **severe hypertension**
- Deposited complexes and plasma proteins produce **bright pink, amorphous appearance** (called "fibrinoid")
- Seen in diseases such as **polyarteritis nodosa**

### Clinical Detection of Necrosis

Leakage of intracellular proteins through damaged cell membrane provides markers of tissue-specific necrosis:

| Tissue | Markers |
|--------|---------|
| **Cardiac muscle** | Creatine kinase (CK) isoform, troponin |
| **Hepatic bile duct epithelium** | Alkaline phosphatase |
| **Hepatocytes** | Transaminases |

## Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a pathway of cell death in which cells activate enzymes that degrade their own **nuclear DNA** and **nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins**. The plasma membrane remains intact but is altered so that fragments (**apoptotic bodies**) are rapidly consumed by phagocytes.

**Key difference from necrosis:** Minimal leakage of cellular contents → **no inflammatory reaction**

### Causes of Apoptosis

#### Physiologic Apoptosis

| Condition | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| **During embryogenesis** | Loss of growth factor signaling (presumed) |
| **Turnover of proliferative tissues** (intestinal epithelium, lymphocytes) | Loss of growth factor signaling (presumed) |
| **Involution of hormone-dependent tissues** (endometrium) | Decreased hormone levels → reduced survival signals |
| **Decline of leukocytes** at end of immune/inflammatory responses | Loss of survival signals as stimulus eliminated |
| **Elimination of self-reactive lymphocytes** | Strong recognition of self antigens induces apoptosis via mitochondrial and death receptor pathways |

#### Pathologic Apoptosis

| Condition | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| **DNA damage** (radiation, toxins, free radicals) | Activation of proapoptotic proteins by BH3-only sensors |
| **Accumulation of misfolded proteins** | Activation of proapoptotic proteins by BH3-only sensors; possibly direct activation of caspases |
| **Infections** (especially viral) | Activation of mitochondrial pathway by viral proteins |
| **Killing by cytotoxic T lymphocytes** | CTLs activate caspases in infected cells |

### Mechanisms of Apoptosis

Apoptosis is regulated by biochemical pathways controlling the balance of death- and survival-inducing signals, ultimately activating **caspases** (cysteine proteases that cleave proteins after aspartic acid residues).

Two distinct pathways converge on caspase activation:

#### Mitochondrial (Intrinsic) Pathway

Responsible for apoptosis in most physiologic and pathologic situations.

**Process:**
1. In healthy cells: **Bcl-2** and **Bcl-xL** maintain mitochondrial membrane integrity by holding proapoptotic **Bax** and **Bak** in check
2. When cells are deprived of survival signals, DNA is damaged, or misfolded proteins accumulate → **BH3-only sensor proteins** are activated
3. BH3-only proteins shift balance in favor of proapoptotic **Bak and Bax**
4. **Bak and Bax dimerize**, insert into mitochondrial membrane, form channels
5. **Cytochrome c** and other mitochondrial proteins leak into cytosol
6. Cytochrome c activates **caspase-9**
7. Caspase cascade → nuclear fragmentation → apoptotic bodies

#### Death Receptor (Extrinsic) Pathway

Many cells express **death receptors** (members of **TNF receptor family**) with conserved "death domain."

**Process:**
1. **Fas ligand (FasL)** on activated T lymphocytes binds **Fas** on target cells
2. Fas molecules crosslinked by FasL bind adaptor proteins via death domain
3. **Caspase-8** is recruited and activated
4. Caspase-8 activates downstream caspases → same end result as mitochondrial pathway

**Functions:**
- Elimination of self-reactive lymphocytes
- Killing of target cells by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) expressing FasL

#### Clearance of Apoptotic Cells

- Apoptotic cells produce **"eat-me" signals**
- **Phosphatidylserine** "flips" from inner to outer leaflet of plasma membrane — recognized by tissue macrophages
- Cells also secrete soluble factors recruiting phagocytes
- Prompt clearance before membrane damage and content release
- **Inflammation is virtually absent**

### Morphology of Apoptosis

- **Chromatin condensation and aggregation** → ultimately **karyorrhexis**
- **DNA fragmentation** into nucleosome-sized pieces
- Cells **rapidly shrink**, form cytoplasmic buds, fragment into apoptotic bodies
- Apoptotic bodies composed of membrane-bound pieces of cytosol and organelles
- May be histologically undetectable even with substantial apoptosis (rapid extrusion and phagocytosis)

## Other Pathways of Cell Death

### Necroptosis

- Initiated by engagement of **TNF receptors** and other poorly defined triggers
- Unlike extrinsic apoptosis pathway, **RIP kinases** are activated
- Results in dissolution of the cell (like necrosis)
- Has features of both necrosis and apoptosis
- Hypothesized to play a role in ischemic injury and inflammatory conditions with TNF production

### Pyroptosis

- Associated with activation of **inflammasome** (cytosolic danger-sensing protein complex)
- Results in activation of caspases that:
  - Induce production of **proinflammatory cytokines** (causing fever)
  - Trigger **apoptosis**
- Name stems from association with apoptosis + fever (Greek: pyro = fire)
- Thought to be a mechanism by which some infectious microbes cause death of infected cells

## Autophagy

**Autophagy** ("self-eating") refers to **lysosomal digestion of the cell's own components**.

**Purpose:** Survival mechanism during nutrient deprivation — starved cell eats its own contents and recycles them for nutrients and energy.

**Process:**
1. Intracellular organelles and cytosol are sequestered within an **ER-derived autophagic vacuole**
2. Vacuole fuses with lysosomes → **autophagolysosome**
3. Lysosomal enzymes digest cellular components
4. Products used as sources of nutrients

**Additional roles:**
- May be associated with **tissue atrophy**
- May represent adaptation helping cells survive "lean times"
- If stress is too severe → autophagy may lead to **apoptotic cell death**

**Clinical associations:**
- Extensive autophagy in **ischemic injury** and some **myopathies**
- Polymorphisms in autophagy gene associated with **inflammatory bowel disease**
- Role in cancer (discussed in Chapter 6)

## Mechanisms of Cell Injury and Cell Death

### General Principles

1. **Response depends on type, duration, and severity of injury**
   - Low doses/brief ischemia → reversible injury
   - Large doses/prolonged ischemia → irreversible injury and cell death

2. **Consequences depend on cell type, status, adaptability, and genetic makeup**
   - Striated skeletal muscle tolerates complete ischemia for 2–3 hours
   - Cardiac muscle dies after only 20–30 minutes of ischemia
   - Glycogen-replete hepatocytes survive ischemia better

3. **Cell injury results from functional and biochemical abnormalities in essential cellular components**
   - Different insults affect different organelles and pathways
   - Loss of oxygen/nutrients → impairs energy-dependent functions → necrosis
   - Damage to proteins/DNA → triggers apoptosis

### Hypoxia and Ischemia

**Deficiency of oxygen** leads to failure of energy-dependent metabolic pathways → death by **necrosis**.

**ATP production:** Most cellular ATP is produced from ADP by **oxidative phosphorylation** in mitochondria during reduction of oxygen.

**ATP required for:**
- Membrane transport
- Protein synthesis
- Lipogenesis
- Deacylation-reacylation reactions (phospholipid turnover)

#### HIF-1 Response

Cells subjected to hypoxia activate **hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1)**:

- Stimulates synthesis of proteins helping cell survive low oxygen
- **Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** — stimulates growth of new vessels
- **Adaptive metabolic changes** — stimulates glucose uptake and glycolysis; dampens mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation

#### Anaerobic Glycolysis

- Generates ATP in absence of oxygen using glucose from circulation or glycogen hydrolysis
- Tissues with greater glycolytic capacity and glycogen stores (liver, striated muscle) survive hypoxia better than tissues with limited glucose stores (brain)

**Note:** Rapidly proliferating cells and cancer cells rely on **aerobic glycolysis** (Warburg effect) — glycolysis + TCA cycle metabolites serve as precursors for cellular constituents needed for growth and division.

#### Consequences of Persistent Hypoxia/Ischemia

- **Reduced plasma membrane ATPase activity** → intracellular Na⁺ accumulation, K⁺ efflux, cell swelling, ER dilation
- **Compensatory anaerobic glycolysis** → lactic acid accumulation, decreased intracellular pH, decreased enzyme activity
- **Structural disruption of protein synthetic apparatus** → detachment of ribosomes from RER, dissociation of polysomes
- **Hypoxia per se increases ROS accumulation** → predisposes to ROS-mediated damage upon reperfusion
- **Irreversible damage to mitochondrial and lysosomal membranes** → necrosis
- Apoptosis via mitochondrial pathway also contributes

#### Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

Restoration of blood flow to ischemic but viable tissues can paradoxically cause **increased cell injury**.

**Mechanisms of exacerbation:**

1. **Increased ROS generation** during reoxygenation
   - Damaged mitochondria cannot carry out complete reduction of oxygen
   - Cellular antioxidant defenses compromised by ischemia
   - ROS from infiltrating leukocytes also contribute

2. **Increased inflammation** upon reperfusion
   - Enhanced influx of leukocytes and plasma proteins
   - Products of activated leukocytes cause additional tissue injury
   - **Complement system activation** — complement proteins bind to injured tissues; activation generates byproducts that exacerbate injury

### Oxidative Stress

**Oxidative stress** = cellular abnormalities induced by **reactive oxygen species (ROS)**.

**Free radicals** = chemical species with single unpaired electron in outer orbit; extremely unstable, readily react with molecules; attack nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids.

#### Generation and Removal of ROS

**Production pathways:**

1. **Normal cellular respiration** (mitochondria)
   - Molecular O₂ reduced to water (4 electrons)
   - Small amounts of reactive intermediates generated when O₂ is only partially reduced
   - Superoxide (O₂•⁻) → converted to H₂O₂ spontaneously or by **superoxide dismutase (SOD)**
   - H₂O₂ converted to highly reactive **hydroxyl radical (•OH)** by Fenton reaction (with Fe²⁺)

2. **Phagocytic leukocytes** (respiratory/oxidative burst)
   - Phagosome membrane enzyme catalyzes generation of superoxide → H₂O₂
   - H₂O₂ converted to **hypochlorite** by **myeloperoxidase** (present in leukocytes)

3. **Nitric oxide (NO)** from macrophages and leukocytes
   - Reacts with O₂•⁻ → **peroxynitrite** → participates in cell injury

**Increased ROS generation under:**
- Absorption of radiant energy (UV light, x-rays, ionizing radiation)
- Enzymatic metabolism of exogenous chemicals (e.g., carbon tetrachloride)
- Inflammation (leukocytes produce free radicals)
- Reperfusion of ischemic tissues

**Removal mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| **Superoxide dismutase (SOD)** | Increases rate of superoxide decay |
| **Glutathione (GSH) peroxidases** | Protect from oxidative damage; catalyze breakdown of H₂O₂ |
| **Catalase** (peroxisomes) | Decomposes hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H₂O) |
| **Endogenous antioxidants** (vitamins E, A, C; β-carotene) | Block formation or scavenge free radicals |

#### Pathologic Effects of ROS

- **Lipid peroxidation of membranes** — double bonds in membrane polyunsaturated lipids attacked → peroxides → autocatalytic chain reaction
- **Protein crosslinking and changes** — sulfhydryl-mediated crosslinking, enhanced degradation, loss of enzymatic activity, polypeptide fragmentation
- **DNA damage** — reactions with thymine residues → single-strand breaks → implicated in apoptosis, aging, malignant transformation
- **Low concentrations of ROS** — involved in numerous signaling pathways and physiologic reactions; intracellular concentrations tightly regulated

#### Major Free Radicals in Cell Injury

| Free Radical | Production | Removal | Pathologic Effects |
|--------------|------------|---------|-------------------|
| **Superoxide (O₂•⁻)** | Incomplete O₂ reduction in mitochondria; phagocyte oxidase | Conversion to H₂O₂ and O₂ by SOD | — |
| **Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)** | From superoxide by SOD action | Conversion to H₂O and O₂ by catalase, glutathione peroxidase | Direct damage to lipids (peroxidation), proteins, DNA; can be converted to •OH and OCl⁻ |
| **Hydroxyl radical (•OH)** | From H₂O, H₂O₂, and O₂•⁻ by chemical reactions | Conversion to H₂O by glutathione peroxidase | Direct damaging effects on lipids, proteins, DNA |
| **Peroxynitrite (ONOO•)** | Interaction of O₂•⁻ and NO by NO synthase | Conversion to nitrite by enzymes | Direct damaging effects on lipids, proteins, DNA |

### Cell Injury Caused by Toxins

#### Direct-Acting Toxins

- Act directly by combining with critical molecular component or cellular organelle
- **Example:** Mercuric chloride poisoning — mercury binds to sulfhydryl groups of membrane proteins → inhibits ATP-dependent transport → increased membrane permeability

#### Latent (Indirect-Acting) Toxins

- Not intrinsically active; must be converted to **reactive metabolites**
- Conversion typically by **cytochrome P-450** in smooth ER of liver
- Most important mechanism: **formation of free radicals**
- **Examples:**
  - **Carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄)** — converted to toxic free radical → causes membrane phospholipid peroxidation
  - **Acetaminophen** — analgesic

**CCl₄ poisoning sequence:**
1. <30 min: ER membrane damage → decline in synthesis of enzymes and plasma proteins
2. 2 hours: swelling of smooth ER; dissociation of ribosomes from RER
3. Decreased synthesis of apoproteins → triglycerides accumulate → "fatty liver"
4. Mitochondrial injury → diminished ATP → defective ion transport → cell swelling
5. Plasma membrane damaged by fatty aldehydes from lipid peroxidation
6. **Cell death**

### Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress

**Accumulation of misfolded proteins** can stress compensatory pathways in the ER → lead to **apoptosis**.

**Unfolded Protein Response (UPR):**

1. **Mild ER stress:**
   - Sensors (e.g., IRE1) detect misfolded proteins
   - Signaling → increased synthesis of chaperones; reduced protein synthesis; increased protein degradation
   - **Adaptive response** — reduces misfolded protein load

2. **Severe ER stress:**
   - Excessive misfolded protein accumulation cannot be corrected
   - ER sensors activate **BH3-only proapoptotic proteins**
   - Direct activation of caspases
   - **Terminal unfolded protein response: apoptosis** via mitochondrial pathway

**Causes of misfolded protein accumulation:**
- Gene mutations → proteins that cannot fold properly
- **Aging** — decreased capacity to correct misfolding
- **Viral infections** — large amounts of microbial proteins synthesized
- Increased demand for secretory proteins (e.g., insulin in insulin-resistant states)
- Changes in intracellular pH and redox state
- **Ischemia/hypoxia** — deprivation of glucose and oxygen

**Effects of protein misfolding:**

| Effect | Mechanism | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Deficiency of essential protein** | Misfolded proteins lose activity and are degraded | Cystic fibrosis (CFTR mutations) |
| **Cell death by apoptosis** | ER stress triggers apoptotic pathway | Neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer, Huntington, Parkinson); type 2 diabetes |

**Table 2.4 — Diseases Caused by Misfolded Proteins:**

*Diseases with mutant proteins degraded (leading to deficiency):*
- **Cystic fibrosis** — CFTR; loss of chloride transport
- **Familial hypercholesterolemia** — LDL receptor; hypercholesterolemia
- **Tay-Sachs disease** — Hexosaminidase β subunit; GM2 ganglioside storage in neurons

*Diseases with misfolded proteins causing ER stress-induced cell loss:*
- **Retinitis pigmentosa** — abnormal rhodopsin folding; photoreceptor loss
- **Creutzfeldt-Jacob disease** — abnormal prion (PrP^sc) folding; neuronal cell death
- **Alzheimer disease** — abnormal Aβ peptide; aggregation within neurons; apoptosis

*Diseases from both ER stress-induced cell loss AND functional deficiency:*
- **Alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency** — storage of nonfunctional protein in hepatocytes (apoptosis); absence of enzyme activity in lungs (emphysema)

### DNA Damage

- Exposure to **radiation** or **chemotherapeutic agents**
- **Intracellular generation of ROS**
- **Acquisition of mutations**

**Response to DNA damage:**

1. **Sentinel proteins** detect damage → signals accumulate **p53 protein**
2. **p53 arrests cell cycle** (G1 phase) → allows DNA repair before replication
3. If damage is too severe to repair → **p53 triggers apoptosis**
   - Stimulates **BH3-only sensor proteins** → activate **Bax and Bak**
   - Mitochondrial pathway of apoptosis

**Clinical significance:** When **p53 is mutated or absent** (certain cancers), cells with damaged DNA that would undergo apoptosis survive → DNA damage may result in mutations/rearrangements → neoplastic transformation

### Inflammation

- Common cause of cell and tissue injury
- Inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, other leukocytes) secrete products that may damage host tissues
- Classified as **hypersensitivity reactions** (Chapter 5)

## Common Events in Cell Injury

### Mitochondrial Dysfunction

Mitochondria are critical players in cell injury and death; sensitive to hypoxia, toxins, radiation.

**Biochemical abnormalities:**

1. **Failure of oxidative phosphorylation** → ATP depletion → **necrosis**
2. **Abnormal oxidative phosphorylation** → **ROS production**
3. **Formation of mitochondrial permeability transition pore** → loss of membrane potential, pH changes → further compromises oxidative phosphorylation
4. **Cytochrome c release** into cytoplasm → activates **apoptosis** via mitochondrial pathway

### Defects in Membrane Permeability

Most forms of cell injury culminating in necrosis involve increased membrane permeability → overt membrane damage.

**Key sites of membrane damage:**

| Membrane | Consequence |
|----------|-------------|
| **Mitochondrial** | Decreased ATP → many deleterious effects → necrosis |
| **Plasma** | Loss of osmotic balance; influx of fluids/ions; loss of cellular contents; leakage of vital metabolites → further ATP depletion |
| **Lysosomal** | Leakage of enzymes into cytoplasm; activation of acid hydrolases → enzymatic digestion of cell components → **necrosis** |

*Note: Increased plasma membrane and lysosomal membrane permeability is NOT a feature of apoptosis.*

## Cellular Adaptations to Stress

**Adaptations** = reversible changes in number, size, phenotype, metabolic activity, or function of cells in response to environmental changes.

- **Physiologic adaptations** — responses to normal stimulation by hormones or mechanical stress
- **Pathologic adaptations** — responses to stress that allow cells to escape injury (at the expense of normal function)

### Hypertrophy

**Hypertrophy** = increase in cell size → increase in organ size (contrast with **hyperplasia** = increase in cell number).

- Pure hypertrophy = **no new cells**, just bigger cells with increased structural proteins and organelles
- Occurs when cells have **limited capacity to divide**
- Can occur concurrently with hyperplasia → enlarged organ

**Causes:**
- **Increased functional demand** OR
- **Growth factor/hormonal stimulation**

**Examples:**

| Type | Example |
|------|--------|
| **Physiologic** | Uterus during pregnancy (estrogen-stimulated smooth muscle hypertrophy + hyperplasia) |
| **Physiologic** | Skeletal muscle enlargement in weightlifters (hypertrophy only — adult muscle cells have limited division capacity) |
| **Pathologic** | Cardiac enlargement in hypertension or aortic valve disease |

**Mechanisms of cardiac hypertrophy:**
1. **Mechanical triggers** (stretch) + **soluble mediators** (growth factors, adrenergic hormones)
2. Signal transduction → gene induction → synthesis of growth factors and structural proteins
3. More proteins and myofilaments per cell → increased contractile force
4. Switch from adult to fetal/neonatal contractile protein forms (e.g., α-myosin heavy chain → β form)

**Progression to injury:** Adaptation can progress to cell injury if stress is not relieved. Eventually degenerative changes occur (fragmentation/loss of myofibrillar elements) → **ventricular dilation → cardiac failure**

### Hyperplasia

**Hyperplasia** = increase in number of cells in an organ from increased proliferation (of differentiated cells or less differentiated progenitor cells).

- Occurs in tissues with cell populations capable of replication
- May occur concurrently with hypertrophy

**Types:**

| Type | Example |
|------|--------|
| **Hormonal hyperplasia** (physiologic) | Proliferation of female breast glandular epithelium at puberty and during pregnancy |
| **Compensatory hyperplasia** (physiologic) | Liver regeneration after partial resection (mitotic activity begins ~12 hours later; growth factors from hepatocytes and nonparenchymal cells; restored by growth inhibitors when mass is recovered) |
| **Pathologic hyperplasia** | Endometrial hyperplasia (excess estrogenic stimulation); benign prostatic hyperplasia (androgen stimulation); papillomavirus-induced skin warts/mucosal lesions (viral growth factor genes) |

**Key point:** Hyperplastic process remains controlled — if signals ablate, hyperplasia disappears. This responsiveness distinguishes hyperplasia from **cancer** (permanent dysregulation of growth control). However, hyperplasia may predispose to cancer (e.g., endometrial hyperplasia → endometrial cancer).

### Atrophy

**Atrophy** = shrinkage in size of cells by loss of cell substance → tissue or organ reduced in size.

- Atrophic cells are diminished in function but **not dead**
- Represents retreat to smaller size where survival is possible

**Causes:**
- Decreased workload (e.g., limb immobilization)
- Loss of innervation
- Diminished blood supply
- Inadequate nutrition
- Loss of endocrine stimulation
- **Aging (senile atrophy)**

**Cellular mechanisms:**
1. **Decreased protein synthesis** — reduced metabolic activity
2. **Increased protein degradation** — mainly by **ubiquitin-proteasome pathway**
   - Nutrient deficiency and disuse activate ubiquitin ligases
   - Multiple ubiquitin copies attached to proteins → targeted for proteasome degradation
   - Responsible for accelerated proteolysis in catabolic conditions (e.g., cancer cachexia)
3. **Autophagy** — increased autophagic vacuoles; cell eats own organelles to survive

### Metaplasia

**Metaplasia** = change in which one adult cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal) is replaced by another adult cell type.

- Cell type sensitive to particular stress is replaced by cell type better able to withstand the adverse environment
- Thought to arise from **reprogramming of stem cells** to differentiate along a new pathway

**Examples:**

| Location | Change |
|----------|--------|
| Respiratory epithelium in smokers | Ciliated columnar → stratified squamous |
| Lower esophagus (chronic gastric reflux) | Stratified squamous → gastric/intestinal columnar |
| Vitamin A deficiency | Respiratory epithelium squamous metaplasia |
| Soft tissue injury | Mesenchymal cells → bone |

**Respiratory (squamous) metaplasia in smokers:**
- Stratified squamous epithelium survives noxious chemicals better than fragile specialized epithelium
- **Trade-off:** Lost protective mechanisms (mucus secretion, ciliary clearance)
- **Double-edged sword**

**Malignant potential:** Persistent metaplastic changes may predispose to malignant transformation. Squamous metaplasia of respiratory epithelium often coexists with lung squamous cell carcinomas.

## Intracellular Accumulations

Under some circumstances, cells accumulate abnormal amounts of various substances.

**Main pathways:**
1. **Inadequate removal/degradation** of endogenous substances
2. **Excessive production** of endogenous substances
3. **Deposition of abnormal exogenous material**

### Fatty Change (Steatosis)

- **Accumulation of triglycerides** within parenchymal cells
- Most often in **liver** (major fat metabolism organ); also heart, skeletal muscle, kidney
- **Causes:** Toxins, protein malnutrition, diabetes mellitus, obesity, anoxia
- **Most common causes** in industrialized nations: **alcohol abuse** and **diabetes associated with obesity**

### Cholesterol and Cholesteryl Esters

- Cellular cholesterol metabolism is tightly regulated
- Phagocytic cells may become overloaded with lipid in pathologic processes
- **Atherosclerosis** = most important condition with lipid deposition (cholesterol, triglycerides, cholesteryl esters) in macrophages and smooth muscle cells of vessel walls

### Proteins

**Examples:**
- **Hyaline cytoplasmic droplets** in kidney proximal tubules — from excessive reabsorption of albumin (in nephrotic syndrome); reversible
- **Russell bodies** — rounded eosinophilic structures in plasma cells from accumulated immunoglobulins in RER
- **Other:** "Alcoholic hyaline" in liver; neurofibrillary tangles in neurons

### Glycogen

- Associated with abnormalities in metabolism of glucose or glycogen
- **Poorly controlled diabetes mellitus** — glycogen accumulates in renal tubular epithelium, cardiac myocytes, β cells of islets of Langerhans
- **Glycogen storage diseases (glycogenoses)** — genetic disorders with defective lysosomal enzymes

## Pathologic Calcification

**Pathologic calcification** = abnormal deposition of calcium salts (with smaller amounts of iron, magnesium, other minerals) in two forms:

### Dystrophic Calcification

- **Calcium metabolism is normal**
- Calcium deposits in **injured or dead tissue**
- Occurs in areas of necrosis of any type
- Common in arterial lesions of advanced atherosclerosis
- May be incidental or cause organ dysfunction (e.g., calcification of aging/damaged heart valves → aortic stenosis)

**Mechanism:**
- Extracellular deposition of crystalline calcium phosphate in membrane-bound vesicles (derived from injured cells)
- Or intracellular deposition in mitochondria of dying cells
- Calcium concentrated in vesicles by affinity for membrane phospholipids
- Phosphates accumulate from action of membrane-bound phosphatases
- Crystals propagate → larger deposits

### Metastatic Calcification

- Associated with **hypercalcemia**
- Occurs in **normal tissues**
- Principal causes of hypercalcemia:
  1. **Increased PTH secretion** — primary parathyroid tumors or PTH-related protein from malignancies
  2. **Destruction of bone** — Paget disease, immobilization, tumors (multiple myeloma, leukemia, diffuse skeletal metastases)
  3. **Vitamin D–related disorders** — vitamin D intoxication, sarcoidosis (macrophages activate vitamin D precursor)
  4. **Renal failure** — phosphate retention → secondary hyperparathyroidism

**Distribution:** Principally affects interstitial tissues of vasculature, kidneys, lungs, gastric mucosa.

**Morphology:** Fine white granules or clumps; gritty deposits on gross examination. In tuberculosis: calcification in areas of caseous necrosis. May form heterotopic bone over time.

## Cellular Aging

**Individuals age because their cells age.** Age is one of the strongest independent risk factors for chronic diseases (cancer, Alzheimer disease, ischemic heart disease).

**Key discovery:** Cellular aging is regulated by a limited number of genes and signaling pathways (evolutionarily conserved from yeast to mammals).

**Cellular aging** = progressive decline in life span and functional activity of cells.

### Mechanisms of Cellular Aging

#### Accumulation of Mutations in DNA

- Metabolic insults over time → damage to nuclear and mitochondrial DNA
- **ROS** induced by toxins and radiation contribute to DNA damage
- Most DNA damage is repaired, but some persists and accumulates
- Compromises functional activities and survival of cells

#### Decreased Cellular Replication (Replicative Senescence)

- Normal cells (other than stem cells) have **limited replication capacity**
- After fixed number of divisions → **terminally nondividing state (replicative senescence)**
- Cells from older individuals have reduced replication capacity
- **Werner syndrome** (premature aging) — markedly reduced in vitro life span

**Telomere mechanism:**
- **Telomeres** = short repeated DNA sequences at chromosome ends
- Protect chromosome ends from fusion and degradation; ensure complete replication
- Somatic cells replicate → small section of telomere not duplicated → progressive shortening
- Shortened telomeres → chromosome ends sensed as broken DNA → **cell cycle arrest**
- **Telomerase** = specialized RNA-protein complex that adds nucleotides to chromosome ends
  - Expressed in germ cells and stem cells (low levels)
  - **Absent in most somatic cells**
- As somatic cells age → telomeres shorten → exit cell cycle → inability to generate new cells
- **Cancer cells** reactivate telomerase → stabilize telomere length → proliferate indefinitely

**Telomere maintenance abnormalities implicated in:**
- Aplastic anemia (failure of hematopoietic stem cells)
- Premature greying of hair
- Skin pigment and nail abnormalities
- Pulmonary and liver fibrosis
- ("Telomeropathies")

#### Defective Protein Homeostasis

- Cells unable to maintain normal protein homeostasis over time
- **Increased turnover** + **decreased synthesis** (reduced translation, defective chaperone and proteasome activity)
- Resultant decrease in intracellular proteins → deleterious effects on survival, replication, function
- Accumulation of misfolded proteins → loss of functional proteins + triggers apoptosis

#### Counteracting Aging

**Calorie restriction** — slows aging and prolongs life in all species tested.

**Mechanisms:**
- Alters signaling pathways influencing aging
- **Reduced insulin/IGF-1 receptor signaling** → lower rates of cell growth/metabolism; possibly reduced DNA replication errors; better DNA repair; improved protein homeostasis
- Improves immunity
- All of these inhibit aging

**Additional factors:**
- **Physical activity** — slows aging
- **Stress/glucocorticoids** — may accelerate aging

#### Persistent Inflammation

- Accumulation of damaged cells, lipids, endogenous substances → activates **inflammasome pathway**
- Results in persistent low-level inflammation
- Induces chronic diseases (atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes)
- Cytokines from inflammatory reactions may exacerbate aging
- Chronic metabolic disorders may accelerate aging process

## Summary: Cell Injury and Cell Death

### Patterns of Cell Injury and Cell Death

- **Reversible cell injury:** cell swelling, fatty change, plasma membrane blebbing, loss of microvilli, mitochondrial swelling, ER dilation, eosinophilia
- **Necrosis:** accidental cell death — increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia; nuclear shrinkage, fragmentation, dissolution; breakdown of plasma and organellar membranes; abundant myelin figures; leakage and enzymatic digestion of contents
- **Morphologic types of tissue necrosis:** coagulative, liquefactive, gangrenous, caseous, fat, fibrinoid
- **Apoptosis:** regulated mechanism eliminating unwanted and irreparably damaged cells with minimal host reaction — enzymatic degradation of proteins and DNA initiated by caspases; rapid recognition and removal by phagocytes

**Two major pathways of apoptosis:**
1. **Mitochondrial (intrinsic) pathway** — triggered by loss of survival signals, DNA damage, misfolded protein accumulation (ER stress); associated with leakage of proapoptotic proteins from mitochondria → caspase activation; inhibited by anti-apoptotic Bcl family members induced by survival signals
2. **Death receptor (extrinsic) pathway** — eliminates self-reactive lymphocytes and damage by CTLs; initiated by engagement of death receptors (TNF receptor family) by ligands on adjacent cells

**Other pathways:**
- **Necroptosis** — features of both necrosis and apoptosis; regulated by specific signaling pathways
- **Pyroptosis** — releases proinflammatory cytokines; may initiate apoptosis

**Autophagy:** adaptation to nutrient deprivation — cells digest own organelles and recycle for energy/substrates; if stress is too severe → apoptotic cell death

### Mechanisms of Cell Injury

- **Hypoxia and ischemia** → ATP depletion → failure of energy-dependent functions → reversible injury → necrosis
- **Ischemia-reperfusion injury** — restoration of blood flow exacerbates damage via increased ROS and inflammation
- **Oxidative stress** — accumulation of ROS → damage to cellular lipids, proteins, DNA
- **Protein misfolding** — depletes essential proteins; accumulation → apoptosis
- **DNA damage** — if unrepaired → apoptosis
- **Inflammation** — damaging products of inflammatory leukocytes

### Common Events

- **Mitochondrial dysfunction** — failure of oxidative phosphorylation, ROS production, cytochrome c release (apoptosis)
- **Defects in membrane permeability** — mitochondrial, plasma, and lysosomal membrane damage

## Summary: Cellular Adaptations to Stress

- **Hypertrophy:** increased cell and organ size in response to increased workload; induced by growth factors from mechanical stress or other stimuli; occurs in tissues incapable of cell division
- **Hyperplasia:** increased cell numbers in response to hormones and growth factors; occurs in tissues capable of division or with abundant stem cells
- **Atrophy:** decreased cell and organ size from decreased nutrient supply or disuse; associated with decreased synthesis of cellular components and increased breakdown via ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and autophagy
- **Metaplasia:** change in phenotype of differentiated cells in response to chronic irritation; makes cells better able to withstand stress; usually from altered differentiation of tissue stem cells; may reduce functions or increase propensity for malignant transformation

---

## Summary: Intracellular Accumulations and Calcifications

- **Abnormal deposits** result from excessive intake or defective transport/catabolism
- **Fatty change:** accumulation of free triglycerides from excessive intake or defective transport; manifestation of reversible cell injury
- **Cholesterol deposition:** defective catabolism and excessive intake; seen in macrophages and smooth muscle in atherosclerosis
- **Protein deposition:** reabsorbed proteins in kidney tubules; immunoglobulins in plasma cells
- **Glycogen deposition:** in macrophages of patients with defects in lysosomal enzymes (glycogen storage diseases)
- **Pigment deposition:** typically indigestible pigments — carbon, lipofuscin (lipid peroxidation product), iron (hemosiderosis)
- **Pathologic calcification:**
  - **Dystrophic:** deposition at sites of cell injury and necrosis (normal calcium metabolism)
  - **Metastatic:** deposition in normal tissues from hypercalcemia (usually from PTH excess)

---

## Summary: Cellular Aging

- Results from combination of progressive cellular alterations:
  - Accumulation of **DNA damage and mutations**
  - **Replicative senescence** — reduced capacity to divide secondary to telomere shortening
  - **Defective protein homeostasis** — loss of normal proteins and accumulation of misfolded proteins
- Exacerbated by chronic diseases (especially prolonged inflammation) and stress
- Slowed by **calorie restriction** and **exercise**

---

## Key Points

1. Cell injury and cell death are the foundation of all disease processes
2. Reversible injury can progress to irreversible injury and cell death if the damaging stimulus persists
3. **Necrosis** and **apoptosis** are the two major pathways of cell death with distinct morphologies and clinical implications
4. Multiple mechanisms contribute to cell injury: hypoxia, toxins, ROS, misfolded proteins, DNA damage, inflammation
5. Cellular adaptations (**hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia**) allow cells to respond to stress but may have pathologic consequences
6. Intracellular accumulations of lipids, proteins, glycogen, and pigments reflect underlying metabolic disturbances
7. Pathologic calcification (dystrophic and metastatic) is a common endpoint of cell injury and metabolic derangements
8. Cellular aging involves multiple progressive mechanisms including DNA damage, telomere shortening, and defective protein homeostasis
