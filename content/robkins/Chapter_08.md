# Chapter 8: Environmental and Nutritional Diseases

## Climate Change and Health

Climate change represents one of the greatest threats to global health in the 21st century. The rising atmospheric and ocean temperatures have led to numerous effects:

- **Changes in storm frequency**, drought, and flood
- **Large-scale ice losses** in Greenland, Antarctica, and other glaciated regions
- **Dramatic thinning or disappearance** of Arctic ocean sea ice
- **Sea level rise** of approximately 80 mm since 1993, currently rising at 3.5 ± 0.4 mm/year

### Greenhouse Gases and the Greenhouse Effect

The atmospheric level of **carbon dioxide (CO2)** in 2015 (approximately 401 ppm) was higher than at any point in approximately 650,000 years. Without changes in human behavior, levels are expected to increase to 500 to 1200 ppm by the end of this century.

**Key greenhouse gases include:**
- Carbon dioxide (CO2) from fossil fuel burning
- Ozone (an important air pollutant)
- Methane
- Water vapor

### Health Consequences of Climate Change

Climate change impacts human health through multiple pathways:

**Cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and respiratory diseases**
- Exacerbated by heat waves and air pollution

**Gastroenteritis, cholera, and other food- and waterborne infectious diseases**
- Caused by contamination from floods
- Disruption of clean water supplies and sewage treatment after heavy rains

**Vector-borne infectious diseases**
- Malaria and dengue fever
- Resulting from changes in vector number and geographic distribution
- Related to increased temperatures, crop failures, and extreme weather variation

**Malnutrition**
- Caused by changes in local climate that disrupt crop production
- Most severe in tropical locations where average temperatures may already be near or above crop tolerance levels
- Estimated that by 2080, agricultural productivity may decline by 10% to 25% in some developing countries

### Sea Level Rise and Population Displacement

Projected rise of 2 to 6 feet by 2100 from glacial melting and thermal expansion:
- Approximately 10% of the world's population (roughly 600 million people) live in low-lying areas at risk for flooding
- A rise of 1.5 feet will submerge 70% of the land mass of Maldive islands by 2100
- A 3-foot rise will inundate 100% of the islands by 2085

## Toxicity of Chemical and Physical Agents

**Toxicology** is defined as the science of poisons. It studies the distribution, effects, and mechanisms of action of toxic agents, as well as effects of physical agents such as radiation and heat.

### Basic Principles of Xenobiotic Toxicity

**Important concepts:**
- The definition of a poison is not straightforward; it is a **quantitative concept strictly dependent on dosage**. Paracelsus stated: "All substances are poisons; the right dosage differentiates a poison from a remedy."

- **Xenobiotics** are exogenous chemicals in the environment that may be absorbed by the body through:
  - Inhalation
  - Ingestion
  - Skin contact

- Chemicals may be:
  - Excreted in urine or feces
  - Eliminated in expired air
  - Accumulated in bone, fat, brain, or other tissues

- Chemicals may act at the site of entry or be transported to other sites

### Metabolism of Xenobiotics

**Phase I Reactions:**
- Hydrolysis, oxidation, or reduction
- Most important enzyme system: **cytochrome P-450 system**
- Located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of the liver
- May produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a byproduct

**Phase II Reactions:**
- Glucuronidation, sulfation, methylation, and conjugation with glutathione (GSH)
- Convert products to water-soluble compounds for excretion

**Key points about P-450:**
- Present in organs throughout the body, most active in liver
- Catalyzes reactions that may either detoxify or activate xenobiotics
- Varies widely in activity among different people due to:
  - Polymorphisms in encoding genes
  - Interactions with drugs
  - Decreased by fasting or starvation
  - Increased by alcohol consumption and smoking

**Examples of metabolic activation:**
- Conversion of carbon tetrachloride to toxic trichloromethyl free radical
- Generation of DNA-binding metabolite from benzo[a]pyrene (carcinogen in cigarette smoke)

## Environmental Pollution

### Air Pollution

Air pollution is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly among at-risk individuals with preexisting pulmonary or cardiac disease.

#### Outdoor Air Pollution

**Six pollutants monitored by the EPA:**
- Sulfur dioxide
- Carbon monoxide (CO)
- Ozone
- Nitrogen dioxide
- Lead
- Particulate matter

The lungs bear the brunt of adverse consequences, but air pollutants affect many organ systems.

**Table 8.1 Health Effects of Outdoor Air Pollutants**

| Pollutant | Populations at Risk | Effect(s) |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Ozone** | Healthy adults and children; Athletes, outdoor workers; Asthmatics | Decreased lung function; Increased airway reactivity; Lung inflammation; Decreased exercise capacity; Increased hospitalizations |
| **Nitrogen dioxide** | Healthy adults; Asthmatics; Children | Decreased lung function; Increased respiratory infections |
| **Sulfur dioxide** | Healthy adults; Patients with chronic lung disease; Asthmatics; Children | Increased respiratory symptoms; Increased mortality; Increased hospitalization; Decreased lung function; Altered mucociliary clearance |

##### Ozone

- Gas formed by sunlight-driven reactions involving nitrogen oxides from automobile exhaust
- Combines with oxides and fine particulate matter to form smog
- Toxicity stems from participation in chemical reactions that generate free radicals
- Free radicals injure lining cells of respiratory tract and alveoli
- Low levels may be tolerated by healthy persons but are detrimental to those with asthma or emphysema

##### Sulfur Dioxide, Particles, and Acid Aerosols

- Emitted by coal- and oil-fired power plants and industrial processes
- Particles less than 10 µm in diameter are particularly harmful
- When inhaled, they are carried to the alveoli
- Phagocytosed by macrophages and neutrophils
- Cause release of mediators and incite inflammatory reaction

##### Carbon Monoxide (CO)

**Characteristics:**
- Nonirritating, colorless, tasteless, odorless gas
- Produced by incomplete oxidation of carbonaceous materials
- Sources include automotive engines, industries using fossil fuels, home oil burners, and cigarette smoke

**Mechanism of toxicity:**
- Binds to hemoglobin with 200-fold greater affinity for CO than for O2
- Carboxyhemoglobin formed is incapable of carrying oxygen
- Systemic asphyxiant that kills by preventing oxygen transport

**Clinical effects:**
- Systemic hypoxia appears when hemoglobin is 20% to 30% saturated with CO
- Unconsciousness and death probable with 60% to 70% saturation
- CNS depression develops insidiously; victims often unaware of their plight

**MORPHOLOGY - Carbon Monoxide Poisoning:**

*Chronic poisoning:*
- Carboxyhemoglobin is remarkably stable
- Slowly developing hypoxia evokes widespread ischemic changes in brain, particularly basal ganglia and lenticular nuclei
- May be permanent neurologic damage after cessation of exposure

*Acute poisoning:*
- Characteristic generalized cherry-red color of skin and mucous membranes in light-skinned people
- Brain may be slightly edematous with punctate hemorrhages and hypoxia-induced neuronal changes
- Complete recovery possible, but impairments of memory, vision, hearing, and speech may remain

#### Indoor Air Pollution

**Common pollutants:**
- Tobacco smoke (discussed under Effects of Tobacco)
- Carbon monoxide
- Nitrogen dioxide
- Asbestos (discussed in Chapter 13)

**Additional indoor pollutants:**

**Smoke from burning organic materials:**
- Contains various oxides of nitrogen and carbon particulates
- Irritant that predisposes to lung infections
- May contain carcinogenic polycyclic hydrocarbons
- One-third of the world burns carbon-containing material (wood, dung, charcoal) for cooking, heating, and light

**Radon:**
- Radioactive gas derived from uranium
- Widely present in soil and homes
- Can cause lung cancer in uranium miners (particularly smokers)
- Does not appear that low-level chronic home exposures increase lung cancer risk for nonsmokers

**Bioaerosols:**
- Pathogenic microbiologic agents (Legionnaires' disease, viral pneumonia, common cold)
- Allergens from pet dander, dust mites, fungi and molds
- Can cause rhinitis, eye irritation, and asthma

### Summary: Environmental Diseases and Pollution

- **Environmental diseases** are conditions caused by exposure to chemical or physical agents in ambient, workplace, and personal environments
- **Xenobiotics** enter through inhalation, ingestion, and skin contact; can be eliminated or accumulate in fat, bone, brain, and other tissues
- Xenobiotics converted to nontoxic or toxic compounds through two-phase reactions involving **cytochrome P-450**
- Most common air pollutants: **ozone**, **sulfur dioxide**, **acid aerosols**, and **particles less than 10 µm**
- **CO** is both an air pollutant and important cause of death; binds hemoglobin with high affinity, causing systemic asphyxiation

## Metals as Environmental Pollutants

### Lead

**Characteristics:**
- Readily absorbed metal that binds to sulfhydryl groups in proteins
- Interferes with calcium metabolism
- Causes hematologic, skeletal, neurologic, GI, and renal toxicities

**Sources of exposure:**
- Contaminated air, food, and water
- Historically: house paints and gasoline
- Current hazards: mines, foundries, batteries, spray paints (occupational)
- Flaking lead paint in older houses and soil contamination (major hazards for children)

**Special vulnerability of children:**
- Absorb more than 50% of lead from food (adults absorb approximately 15%)
- More permeable blood-brain barrier creates high susceptibility to brain damage
- CDC recommends intervention when blood levels exceed 5 µg/dL

** Flint Water Crisis (2014-2016):**
- Water source changed from Lake Huron to Flint River
- Higher chloride concentration leached lead from century-old pipes
- Lead levels in tap water exceeded 15 ppb in about 25% of homes
- Some cases as high as 13,200 ppb
- 6000 to 12,000 residents developed very high blood lead levels

**Distribution of absorbed lead:**
- 80% to 85% taken up into developing teeth and bone (competes with calcium, binds phosphates)
- Half-life of 20 to 30 years in bone
- 5% to 10% remains in blood
- Remainder distributed throughout soft tissues

**Clinical features:**

*BRAIN:*
- Adult: Headache, memory loss
- Child: Encephalopathy, mental deterioration

*GINGIVA:* Lead line

*BLOOD:* Anemia, red cell basophilic stippling

*PERIPHERAL NERVES:* Adult: Demyelination

*KIDNEY:* Chronic tubulointerstitial disease

*GASTROINTESTINAL TRACT:* Abdominal pain (lead "colic")

*BONES:* Child: Radiodense deposits in epiphyses

**Sources:**
- Occupational: Spray painting, foundry work, mining and extracting lead, battery burning
- Nonoccupational: Water supply, paint dust and flakes, automotive exhaust, urban soil

**MORPHOLOGY - Lead Toxicity:**

*Blood changes:*
- Microcytic, hypochromic anemia
- Distinctive punctate basophilic stippling of red cells
- Inhibition of heme synthesis in marrow erythroid progenitors
- Zinc-protoporphyrin formed instead of heme

*Brain damage (more common in children):*
- Brain edema, demyelination of cerebral and cerebellar white matter, necrosis of cortical neurons
- Diffuse astrocytic proliferation
- In adults: peripheral demyelinating neuropathy (wristdrop, footdrop)

*GI tract:* Lead "colic" with severe, poorly localized abdominal pain

*Kidneys:* Proximal tubular damage with intranuclear lead inclusions; interstitial fibrosis; "saturnine gout"

**Diagnosis:**
- Elevated blood lead and red cell free protoporphyrin levels (>50 µg/dL)
- Zinc-protoporphyrin levels
- In milder cases, anemia may be the only abnormality

### Mercury

**Characteristics:**
- Binds to sulfhydryl groups in certain proteins with high affinity
- Causes damage in CNS, GI tract, and kidneys

**Historical uses:**
- Pigment in cave paintings
- Cosmetic
- Remedy for syphilis
- Component of diuretics
- Hat-making (associated with "Mad Hatter" in Alice in Wonderland)

**Modern sources of exposure:**
- Contaminated fish
- Dental amalgams (release mercury vapors)
- Gold mining contamination in some areas

**Methyl mercury:**
- Inorganic mercury converted to organic compounds (methyl mercury) by bacteria
- Enters food chain; levels in carnivorous fish (swordfish, shark, bluefish) may be 1 million times higher than surrounding water
- Almost 90% absorbed in GI tract

**Minamata Disease:**
- Caused by consumption of contaminated fish (Minamata Bay, Japan) and bread containing methyl mercury-treated grain (Iraq)
- Cerebral palsy, deafness, blindness, major CNS defects in children exposed in utero
- Developing brain extremely sensitive to methyl mercury
- CDC recommends pregnant women avoid fish known to contain mercury

**Effects of ingested mercury:**
- Injury to gut with ulcerations and bloody diarrhea
- Acute tubular necrosis and renal failure in kidneys

### Arsenic

**Characteristics:**
- Arsenic salts interfere with several aspects of cellular metabolism
- Toxicities most prominent in GI tract, nervous system, skin, and heart

**Historical:** Poison of choice of skilled practitioners in Borgia and Medici families

**Modern sources:**
- Found naturally in soil and water
- Used in wood preservatives, herbicides, agricultural products
- Released by mining and smelting industries
- Present in Chinese and Indian herbal medicine
- Arsenic trioxide is a frontline treatment for acute promyelocytic leukemia

**Ground water contamination:**
- Large concentrations in Bangladesh, Chile, and China
- 35 to 77 million people in Bangladesh drink arsenic-contaminated water
- One of the greatest environmental cancer risks uncovered

**Effects of arsenic poisoning:**

*Acute toxicity:*
- Severe abdominal pain, diarrhea
- Cardiac arrhythmias, shock
- Respiratory distress syndrome
- Acute encephalopathy
- Interference with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation

*Chronic exposure:*
- Hyperpigmentation and hyperkeratosis of skin
- Basal and squamous cell carcinomas (not melanomas)
- Appear on palms and soles; multiple lesions
- Symmetrical sensorimotor polyneuropathy
- Increased risk of lung carcinoma

### Cadmium

**Characteristics:**
- Preferentially toxic to kidneys and lungs
- Mechanisms may involve increased production of ROS
- Relatively modern toxic agent

**Sources:**
- Mainly used in nickel-cadmium batteries (disposed as household waste)
- Contaminates soil and plants through fertilizers and irrigation water
- Food is most important source of exposure for general population

**Effects:**
- Obstructive lung disease
- Renal toxicity (initially tubular damage, may progress to end-stage renal disease)
- Skeletal abnormalities with calcium loss

**Itai-itai disease (Japan):**
- Cadmium-contaminated water used to irrigate rice fields
- Postmenopausal women affected
- Combination of osteoporosis and osteomalacia with renal disease
- 5% of persons aged 20+ in U.S. have urinary cadmium levels that may produce subtle kidney injury

### Summary: Toxic Effects of Heavy Metals

- **Lead, mercury, arsenic, and cadmium** are the heavy metals most commonly associated with toxic effects
- Children absorb more ingested lead than adults; main source is lead-containing paint
- Excess lead causes CNS defects in children and peripheral neuropathy in adults; competes with calcium in bones
- Major source of mercury is contaminated fish; developing brain highly sensitive to methyl mercury
- Fetal exposure to high mercury levels may cause **Minamata disease** (cerebral palsy, deafness, blindness)
- Arsenic found naturally in soil and water; causes polyneuropathy, skin lesions, and carcinomas
- Cadmium causes obstructive lung disease and kidney damage

### Important Agents

**Organic solvents:**
- Widely used in huge quantities worldwide
- Found in degreasing agents, dry cleaning agents, paint removers
- Acute exposure: dizziness, confusion, CNS depression, coma
- Lower levels: liver and kidney toxicity
- Benzene (rubber workers): increased risk of leukemia
- Oxidized to epoxide through CYP2E1; disrupts progenitor cell differentiation in bone marrow

**Polycyclic hydrocarbons:**
- Released during combustion of coal and gas (especially steel foundries)
- Present in tar and soot
- Among the most potent carcinogens
- Implicated in lung and bladder cancer

**Organochlorines:**
- Synthetic products that resist degradation
- Lipophilic
- Include DDT, lindane, aldrin, dieldrin, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), dioxin (TCDD)
- DDT banned in U.S. in 1973, but more than half the population has detectable serum levels of p,p'-DDE
- Acute DDT poisoning causes neurologic toxicity
- Most are endocrine disruptors with anti-estrogenic or anti-androgenic activity

**Dioxins and PCBs:**
- Cause chloracne (acne, cyst formation, hyperpigmentation, hyperkeratosis)
- Can cause abnormalities in liver and CNS
- Induce P-450 enzyme system
- Yusho (Japan) and yu-cheng (China) outbreaks from contaminated rice oil

**Bisphenol A (BPA):**
- Used in polycarbonate food containers and epoxy resins lining food bottles and cans
- Exposure virtually ubiquitous in humans
- Potential endocrine disruptor
- Linked to heart disease in adult populations
- Infants particularly susceptible to endocrine effects
- Canada first country to list BPA as toxic substance (2010)

**Vinyl chloride:**
- Used in synthesis of polyvinyl resins
- Can cause angiosarcoma of the liver

**Mineral dusts (Pneumoconioses):**
- Chronic, nonneoplastic lung diseases
- Include coal dust (mining), silica (sandblasting, stone cutting), asbestos (mining, fabrication, insulation), beryllium
- Discussed in detail in Chapter 13

## Effects of Tobacco

Tobacco is the most common exogenous cause of human cancers, responsible for 90% of lung cancers.

### Epidemiology

- **Cigarette smoking** is the main culprit, but smokeless tobacco (snuff, chewing tobacco) also harmful
- Important cause of oral cancer
- Passive tobacco inhalation ("second-hand smoke") causes lung cancer in nonsmokers
- Approximately 20% of U.S. adults remain smokers
- China: world's largest producer and consumer of cigarettes
- Approximately 350 million smokers in China; consume about 33% of all cigarettes worldwide

**Global mortality:**
- More than 4 million deaths annually worldwide
- Expected to reach 8 million by 2020
- 500 million people alive today will die from tobacco-related illnesses
- United States: more than 400,000 deaths per year; one-third attributable to lung cancer

**Survival impact:**
- 80% of nonsmokers alive at age 70
- Only about 50% of smokers survive to age 70
- Cessation greatly reduces risk of death from lung cancer

### Mechanisms of Tobacco-Induced Diseases

**Direct irritant effect:**
- Irritates tracheobronchial mucosa
- Produces inflammation and increased mucus production (bronchitis)
- Recruits leukocytes to lung
- Increases local elastase production
- Causes injury leading to emphysema

**Carcinogenesis:**
- Polycyclic hydrocarbons and nitrosamines are potent carcinogens
- Risk related to intensity of exposure (pack years or cigarettes per day)
- Contributes to cancers of oral cavity, esophagus, pancreas, bladder
- Multiplies risk from other carcinogens (asbestos workers, uranium miners who smoke have 10-fold increased incidence)
- Combined tobacco and alcohol has multiplicative effects on oral, laryngeal, and esophageal cancers

**Atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction:**
- Increased platelet aggregation
- Decreased myocardial oxygen supply (lung disease + CO)
- Increased oxygen demand
- Decreased threshold for ventricular fibrillation
- Almost one-third of all heart attacks associated with cigarette smoking
- Multiplicative effect with hypertension and hypercholesterolemia

**Maternal smoking:**
- Increases risk of spontaneous abortions and preterm births
- Results in intrauterine growth retardation
- Birth weights normal if mother stopped smoking before pregnancy

### Passive Smoke Inhalation

- Relative risk of lung cancer in nonsmokers exposed to environmental smoke: approximately 1.3 times
- Approximately 3000 lung cancer deaths annually in nonsmokers older than 35 attributed to environmental tobacco smoke
- 30,000 to 60,000 cardiac deaths annually associated with passive exposure
- Children in households with smokers have increased frequency of respiratory illnesses and asthma
- Blood cotinine levels (metabolite of nicotine) used to estimate passive exposure

### Summary: Health Effects of Tobacco

- **Smoking is the most preventable cause of human death**
- Tobacco smoke contains more than 2000 compounds, including **nicotine** (addiction) and strong carcinogens (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, nitrosamines, aromatic amines)
- Approximately **90% of lung cancers** occur in smokers
- Also associated with increased risk of cancers of oral cavity, larynx, esophagus, stomach, bladder, kidney, and some forms of leukemia
- **Smokeless tobacco** is an important cause of oral cancers
- Tobacco interacts with alcohol to multiply risk of oral, laryngeal, and esophageal cancer
- Increases risk of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease
- Predisposes to emphysema, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive disease
- Maternal smoking increases risk of abortion, premature birth, and intrauterine growth retardation

## Effects of Alcohol

Despite attention given to illegal drugs, alcohol abuse is more widespread and claims many more lives.

**Statistics:**
- 50% of adults in Western world drink alcohol
- Approximately 5% to 10% have chronic alcoholism
- More than 10 million chronic alcoholics in the United States
- More than 100,000 deaths annually from alcohol consumption
- Almost 50% of deaths from accidents (drunken driving, homicides, suicides)
- About 15% from cirrhosis of the liver

### Metabolism of Ethanol

**Absorption and distribution:**
- Absorbed unaltered in stomach and small intestine
- Distributes to all tissues and fluids in direct proportion to blood level
- Less than 10% excreted unchanged in urine, sweat, and breath
- Amount exhaled is proportional to blood level (basis for breath test)

**Blood alcohol levels:**
- Legal definition of drunk driving: 80 mg/dL
- Reached after approximately 3 standard drinks
- Drowsiness at 200 mg/dL
- Stupor at 300 mg/dL
- Coma with possible respiratory arrest at higher levels

**Three enzyme systems metabolize alcohol to acetaldehyde in the liver:**

1. **Alcohol dehydrogenase** (main enzyme, in cytosol of hepatocytes)
2. **Cytochrome P-450 isoenzymes** (particularly CYP2E1, in smooth ER)
3. **Catalase** (in peroxisomes; responsible for only about 5%)

**Acetaldehyde** is converted by aldehyde dehydrogenase to acetate, used in mitochondrial respiratory chain

![Fig. 8.10 Metabolism of ethanol](Oxidation of ethanol to acetaldehyde by three different routes: alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) in cytosol, cytochrome P-450 system (CYP2E1) in microsomes, and catalase in peroxisomes. Oxidation of acetaldehyde by aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) occurs in mitochondria.)

### Toxic Effects of Ethanol Metabolism

**NAD+ depletion:**
- Alcohol oxidation by ADH causes decrease in NAD+ and increase in NADH
- NAD+ required for fatty acid oxidation in liver
- Deficiency is main cause of fat accumulation in liver of alcoholics
- Increased NADH/NAD+ ratio causes lactic acidosis

**Acetaldehyde toxicity:**
- Responsible for some acute effects of alcohol
- Genetic variation in metabolism
- Approximately 50% of Asians express defective form of acetaldehyde dehydrogenase
- Experience flushing, tachycardia, hyperventilation from acetaldehyde accumulation

**ROS generation:**
- Metabolism by CYP2E1 produces ROS
- Causes lipid peroxidation of cell membranes

**Endotoxin release:**
- Alcohol causes release of endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) from intestinal flora
- Stimulates release of TNF and other cytokines from macrophages and Kupffer cells
- Causes cell injury

### Acute Alcoholism

**Effects mainly on CNS; may induce reversible hepatic and gastric injuries:**
- Multiple fat droplets accumulate in hepatocytes (fatty change or hepatic steatosis)
- Acute gastritis and ulceration
- CNS: depressant first affecting subcortical structures
- Progressively higher blood levels depress cortical neurons, then medullary centers
- Respiratory arrest may follow

### Chronic Alcoholism

**Liver (main site of chronic injury):**
- Fatty change
- Alcoholic hepatitis
- Cirrhosis (associated with portal hypertension and increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma)

**GI tract:**
- Massive bleeding from gastritis, gastric ulcer, or esophageal varices (associated with cirrhosis)

**Neurologic effects:**
- Thiamine deficiency common; peripheral neuropathies and Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome
- Cerebral atrophy, cerebellar degeneration, optic neuropathy

**Cardiovascular effects:**
- Dilated congestive cardiomyopathy (alcoholic cardiomyopathy)
- Moderate amounts (one drink/day): increase HDL, inhibit platelet aggregation (protective)
- Heavy consumption: decreased HDL, increased heart disease
- Increased incidence of hypertension

**Pancreatitis:**
- Excess alcohol intake increases risk of acute and chronic pancreatitis

**Fetal effects:**
- Fetal alcohol syndrome: microcephaly, growth retardation, facial abnormalities
- Reduction of mental functions
- Most harmful during first trimester

**Carcinogenesis:**
- Increased incidence of cancers of oral cavity, esophagus, liver, and possibly breast
- Mechanisms uncertain

**Malnutrition:**
- Ethanol is substantial source of energy but "empty calories"
- Consumed at expense of food
- Associated with deficiencies of B vitamins

### Summary: Alcohol — Metabolism and Health Effects

- Acute abuse causes drowsiness at approximately 200 mg/dL; stupor and coma at higher levels
- Oxidized to acetaldehyde in liver primarily by **alcohol dehydrogenase**, and by **cytochrome P-450** and **catalase**
- Oxidation depletes NAD, leading to fat accumulation in liver and metabolic acidosis
- Main effects: fatty liver, alcoholic hepatitis, cirrhosis (leads to portal hypertension and hepatocellular carcinoma)
- Can cause bleeding from gastritis and gastric ulcers, peripheral neuropathy from thiamine deficiency, alcoholic cardiomyopathy
- Major risk factor for cancers of oral cavity, larynx, and esophagus (risk greatly increased by smoking)

| **CNS** | |
| Tinnitus and dizziness | Salicylates |
| Acute dystonic reactions and parkinsonian syndrome | Phenothiazine antipsychotics |
| Respiratory depression | Sedatives |

### Exogenous Estrogens and Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT)

**MHT (previously HRT):**
- Administration of estrogens with progestogen
- Estrogen alone only for hysterectomized women (risk of uterine cancer)

**Historical benefits:**
- Counteract "hot flashes" and other menopausal symptoms
- Early studies suggested prevention/slowing of osteoporosis and reduction of myocardial infarction

**Subsequent findings:**
- Combination MHT increased risk of breast cancer after 5-6 years
- Increased risk of stroke and venous thromboembolism
- No effect on coronary heart disease incidence

**Current understanding:**
- Effects depend on type of regimen, age, risk factor status, duration, dose, formulation, route
- Combination estrogen-progestin: increases breast cancer risk after 5-6 years
- Estrogen alone (hysterectomized women): borderline reduction in breast cancer risk
- May have protective effect on atherosclerosis and coronary disease in women younger than 60
- Should not be used for prevention of cardiovascular disease or other chronic diseases

### Oral Contraceptives

**Composition:**
- Almost always contain synthetic estradiol and progestin ("combination OCs")
- Some preparations contain only progestins
- Current OCs contain smaller amount of estrogens (<50 µg/day)

**Breast carcinoma:** Does not appear to cause increase in breast cancer risk

**Endometrial and ovarian cancers:** OCs have protective effect

**Cervical cancer:** May increase risk in women with HPV infection

**Thromboembolism:**
- Threefold to sixfold increased risk of venous thrombosis and pulmonary thromboembolism
- Higher risk with third-generation OCs containing synthetic progestins
- Risk higher in women with factor V Leiden mutation
- Risk two to six times lower than risk associated with pregnancy

**Cardiovascular disease:**
- No increase in coronary artery disease risk in women younger than 30 or older nonsmokers
- Risk approximately doubles in women older than 35 who smoke

**Hepatic adenoma:** Well-defined association, especially in older women after prolonged use

### Acetaminophen

**Mechanism:**
- At therapeutic doses: mostly conjugated with glucuronide or sulfate
- About 5% metabolized to NAPQI through P-450 system
- Very large doses: NAPQI accumulates, leading to centrilobular hepatic necrosis

**Mechanisms of injury:**
1. Covalent binding to hepatic proteins
2. Depletion of reduced GSH (makes hepatocytes susceptible to ROS)

**Therapeutic window:** Large (usual dose 0.5 g; toxic dose 15-25 g)

**Toxicity:**
- Leading cause of acute liver failure in the United States
- Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, sometimes shock
- Jaundice appears in few days
- Treatment: N-acetylcysteine (restores GSH)
- Serious overdoses may require liver transplantation
- May also cause concurrent renal damage

### Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid)

**Overdose effects:**
- Metabolic (few morphologic changes)
- Initially respiratory alkalosis, then metabolic acidosis
- Fatal doses: 2-4 g in children, 10-30 g in adults

**Chronic aspirin toxicity (salicylism):**
- Develops in persons taking 3 gm or more daily
- Headache, dizziness, tinnitus, hearing difficulty, mental confusion, drowsiness, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
- May progress to convulsions and coma

**Morphologic consequences:**
- Acute erosive gastritis
- Overt or covert GI bleeding
- Gastric ulceration
- Bleeding tendency (aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase, blocks thromboxane A2)
- Petechial hemorrhages in skin and internal viscera

**Analgesic nephropathy:**
- Proprietary mixtures of aspirin and phenacetin or acetaminophen
- Taken throughout several years
- Cause tubulointerstitial nephritis with renal papillary necrosis

#### Cocaine

**In 2014:** 1.5 million users in U.S.; 15-20% used "crack" cocaine

**Forms:**
- Cocaine hydrochloride (water-soluble powder)
- Crack (crystallized pure alkaloid; more potent)

**Administration:** Snorted, smoked, ingested, or injected

**Effects:**
- Produces sense of intense euphoria and mental alertness
- One of the most addictive drugs
- Psychologic dependence is profound

**Acute overdose:** Seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory arrest

**Cardiovascular effects:**
- Sympathomimetic agent
- Blocks reuptake of dopamine in CNS
- Blocks reuptake of epinephrine and norepinephrine at nerve endings
- Causes tachycardia, hypertension, peripheral vasoconstriction
- Induces myocardial ischemia through coronary vasoconstriction and platelet aggregation
- Triggers myocardial ischemia, possibly leading to infarction
- Can precipitate lethal arrhythmias
- Not necessarily dose-related

**CNS effects:**
- Hyperpyrexia (aberrations of dopaminergic pathways)
- Seizures

**Fetal effects:**
- Decreased blood flow to placenta
- Fetal hypoxia
- Spontaneous abortion
- Impaired neurologic development

**Chronic use:**
- Perforation of nasal septum (snorters)
- Decrease in lung diffusing capacity (smoke inhalers)
- Dilated cardiomyopathy

#### Heroin and Other Opioids

**Prevalence:**
- Almost 4 million people in U.S. have used heroin at least once
- In 2012, more than 650,000 people used during the year

**Characteristics:**
- Street heroin is cut (diluted) with talc or quinine
- Purity variable (2% to 90%)
- Administered intravenously or subcutaneously

**Effects:** Euphoria, hallucinations, somnolence, sedation

**Adverse effects:**

*Sudden death:*
- Related to overdose; yearly incidence 1% to 3%
- Due to loss of tolerance after incarceration
- Mechanisms: profound respiratory depression, arrhythmia and cardiac arrest, pulmonary edema

*Pulmonary disease:*
- Edema, septic embolism, lung abscess, opportunistic infections
- Foreign body granulomas from talc and other adulterants
- Found in lung, spleen, liver, lymph nodes

*Infections:*
- Skin and subcutaneous tissue, heart valves, liver, lungs
- More than 10% of addicted patients admitted to hospital have endocarditis
- Right-sided heart valves (especially tricuspid); mostly Staphylococcus aureus
- Viral hepatitis (most common infection)
- High incidence of HIV infection from sharing needles

*Skin lesions:*
- Abscesses, cellulitis, ulcerations from subcutaneous injections
- Scarring, hyperpigmentation, thrombosed veins

*Renal problems:*
- Amyloidosis (secondary to skin infections)
- Focal glomerulosclerosis
- Both cause heavy proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome

**Prescription opioid epidemic:**
- More widespread availability of hydrocodone and oxycodone
- 4.3 million Americans engaged in non-medical prescription opioid use (2014)
- Approximately 19,000 deaths from prescription opioid overdoses (mainly respiratory failure)
- Many heroin users start with prescription opioids, then switch to heroin

#### Marijuana

**Prevalence:**
- Most widely used illegal drug
- 22.2 million people in U.S. admitted use during previous month (2014)
- Several states have legalized recreational use

**Source:** Leaves of Cannabis sativa plant; contains psychoactive substance Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)

**Effects:**
- When smoked: about 5% to 10% of THC content absorbed
- Distorts sensory perception, impairs motor coordination
- Acute effects generally clear in 4-5 hours
- Continued use may progress to cognitive and psychomotor impairments
- Increases heart rate, sometimes blood pressure
- May cause angina in persons with coronary artery disease

**Lungs:**
- Chronic marijuana smoking causes laryngitis, pharyngitis, bronchitis, cough, hoarseness, asthma-like symptoms
- Mild but significant airway obstruction
- Smoking marijuana cigarette associated with 3-fold increase in tar inhaled compared to tobacco cigarette

**Beneficial effects:**
- Decreases intraocular pressure in glaucoma
- Combats intractable nausea from cancer chemotherapy

#### Other Illicit Drugs

**PCP and ketamine:** Related anesthetic agents

**LSD:** Most potent hallucinogen known; unpredictable effects on mood, affect, and thought

**Ecstasy (MDMA):** May deplete CNS serotonin, leading to sleep disorders, depression, anxiety, aggressive behavior

**Bath salts:** Synthetic cathinones chemically related to khat

### Summary: Drug Injury

- **Therapeutic drugs (ADRs)** or **nontherapeutic agents (drug abuse)** may cause drug injury
- Most frequently involved: anti-neoplastic agents, long-acting tetracyclines, antibiotics, HRT preparations, OCs, acetaminophen, aspirin
- HRT increases risk of endometrial and breast cancers and thromboembolism but does not protect against ischemic heart disease
- OCs have protective effect against endometrial and ovarian cancers but increase risk of thromboembolism and hepatic adenomas
- Acetaminophen overdose may cause centrilobular liver necrosis; early treatment with GSH-restoring agents may limit toxicity
- Aspirin blocks thromboxane A2 production, causing gastric ulceration and bleeding
- Common drugs of abuse: sedative-hypnotics, psychomotor stimulants, opioid narcotics, hallucinogens, cannabinoids

## Injury by Physical Agents

### Mechanical Trauma

Mechanical forces inflict various forms of damage depending on shape of colliding object, amount of energy discharged, and tissues/organs bearing impact.

**Types of injuries:**

**Abrasion:** Wound produced by scraping or rubbing skin surface; damages superficial layer (epidermal layer)

**Contusion (bruise):** Produced by blunt trauma; characterized by damage to vessel with extravasation of blood into tissues

**Laceration:** Tear or disruptive stretching of tissue by blunt object; has intact bridging blood vessels and jagged, irregular edges

**Incised wound:** Inflicted by sharp instrument; bridging blood vessels are severed

**Puncture wound:** Caused by long, narrow instrument; penetrating (pierces tissue) or perforating (creates exit wound)

**Gunshot wounds:** Special forms of puncture wounds; powder burns at close range

![Fig. 8.13 (A) Laceration of the scalp](Bridging strands of fibrous tissues evident. (B) Contusion from blunt trauma; skin intact but hemorrhage of subcutaneous vessels has produced extensive discoloration.)

**Vehicular accidents:**
- Hitting interior of vehicle or being hit by objects entering passenger compartment
- Being thrown from vehicle
- Being trapped in burning vehicle
- Common injuries: head trauma (windshield impact), chest injury (steering column), knee injury (dashboard)
- Chest injuries: sternal and rib fractures, heart contusions, aortic lacerations, lacerations of spleen and liver

### Thermal Injury

#### Thermal Burns

**Statistics:**
- 3500 deaths per year in U.S.
- More than 10 times that many hospitalizations
- Many victims are children (scalding by hot liquids)

**Improvements since 1970s:**
- Decreased mortality rates
- Shorter hospitalizations
- Better understanding of systemic effects of massive burns
- Better wound infection prevention
- Improved healing facilitation

**Severity depends on:**
- Depth
- Percentage of body surface involved
- Presence of inhalation injury
- Promptness and efficacy of therapy (fluid/electrolyte management, infection control)

**Burn classification:**

*Full-thickness burns:*
- Total destruction of epidermis and dermis
- Includes dermal appendages
- Third- and fourth-degree burns
- White or charred, dry, anesthetic

*Partial-thickness burns:*
- Deeper portions of dermal appendages spared
- First-degree: epithelial involvement only
- Second-degree: epidermis and superficial dermis
- Pink or mottled, blistered, painful

**MORPHOLOGY:**
- Full-thickness: coagulative necrosis adjacent to vital tissue
- Rapid accumulation of inflammatory cells and marked exudation

**Systemic effects:**
- Burns exceeding 50% of total body surface are grave and potentially fatal
- More than 20%: rapid shift of fluid into interstitial compartments → hypovolemic shock
- Protein loss into interstitial tissue causes generalized edema including pulmonary edema

**Inhalation injury:**
- Frequent in persons trapped in burning buildings
- Direct effect of heat on mouth, nose, upper airways
- Water-soluble gases (chlorine, sulfur oxides, ammonia) react with water to form acids or alkalis → inflammation and swelling → airway obstruction
- Lipid-soluble gases (nitrous oxide, products of burning plastics) reach deeper airways → pneumonitis
- Pulmonary manifestations may not develop for 24-48 hours

**Organ system failure from sepsis:**
- Leading cause of death in burned patients
- Burn site ideal for microorganism growth
- Most common: Pseudomonas aeruginosa
- Also: antibiotic-resistant strains, fungi (Candida)
- Impaired cellular and humoral defenses
- Pneumonia or septic shock with renal failure and/or ARDS

**Hypermetabolic state:**
- Develops with more than 40% body surface burned
- Resting metabolic rate may approach twice normal

#### Hyperthermia

**Heat cramps:**
- Loss of electrolytes through sweating
- Cramping of voluntary muscles during vigorous exercise
- Thermoregulation maintains normal core temperature

**Heat exhaustion:**
- Most common hyperthermic syndrome
- Sudden onset with prostration and collapse
- Failure of cardiovascular system to compensate for hypovolemia (water depletion)
- Usually brief recovery if victim can rehydrate

**Heat stroke:**
- High ambient temperatures and high humidity
- Thermoregulatory mechanisms fail; sweating ceases
- Rectal temperature of 106°F or higher = grave prognostic sign
- Mortality rate exceeds 50%
- Mechanism: marked generalized peripheral vasodilation with peripheral pooling of blood and decreased effective circulating blood volume
- May cause necrosis of muscles and myocardium
- Arrhythmias, disseminated intravascular coagulation common
- Prime candidates: elderly, persons with cardiovascular disease, healthy people undergoing physical stress

**Malignant hyperthermia:**
- Genetic condition from mutations in genes (e.g., RYR1) controlling calcium levels in skeletal muscle cells
- Exposure to certain anesthetics during surgery triggers rapid rise in calcium → muscle rigidity and increased heat production
- Mortality rate approximately 80% if untreated
- Falls to less than 5% if recognized and treated promptly with muscle relaxants

#### Hypothermia

**Predisposing factors:**
- Homeless alcoholics
- Wet or inadequate clothing
- Dilation of superficial blood vessels from alcohol ingestion

**Stages:**
- At approximately 90°F: loss of consciousness
- Lower core temperatures: bradycardia and atrial fibrillation

**Mechanisms of injury:**

*Direct effects:*
- Physical disruptions within cells
- High salt concentrations from crystallization of intra- and extracellular water

*Indirect effects (circulatory changes):*
- Slowly developing chilling: vasoconstriction and increased permeability → edema ("trench foot")
- Sudden sharp drops: vasoconstriction and increased blood viscosity → ischemic injury and degenerative changes in peripheral nerves
- Prolonged ischemia: hypoxic changes and infarction (gangrene of toes or feet)

### Electrical Injury

**Sources:**
- Low-voltage currents (home and workplace: 120 or 220 V)
- High-voltage currents (power lines, lightning)

**Types of injury:**
1. Burns
2. Ventricular fibrillation or cardiac/respiratory center failure

**Severity depends on:** Amperage and path of current through body

**Low voltage (household):**
- With low resistance (wet skin), sufficient current can pass to cause serious injury including ventricular fibrillation
- If current flow continues: heat generation causes burns at entry, exit, and internal organs
- Alternating current induces tetanic muscle spasm → irreversible clutching → prolonged current flow
- May cause spasm of chest wall muscles → death from asphyxia

**High voltage:**
- Similar damage but larger current flows
- More likely to produce paralysis of medullary centers and extensive burns

**Lightning:** Classic cause of high-voltage electrical injury

### Injury Produced by Ionizing Radiation

**Radiation:** Energy traveling as waves or high-speed particles

**Types:**

*Nonionizing radiation:*
- Ultraviolet (UV) and infrared light, microwaves, sound waves
- Can move atoms or cause vibration but not displace electrons

*Ionizing radiation:*
- Sufficient energy to remove tightly bound electrons
- Creates ionization cascade

**Sources of ionizing radiation:**
- X-rays and gamma rays (electromagnetic waves)
- High-energy neutrons, alpha particles, beta particles

**Relative damage:**
- Alpha particles: heavy damage in restricted area
- X-rays and gamma rays: dissipate energy over longer, deeper course; less damage per unit tissue

**Human exposure sources:**
- Medical devices and radioisotopes
- Exposure from radiologic imaging approximately doubled between early 1980s and 2006 (mainly from CT scans)

#### Radiation Units

- **Curie (Ci):** Disintegrations per second of radionuclide (1 Ci = 3.7 × 10^10 disintegrations/second)
- **Gray (Gy):** Energy absorbed per gram of tissue (1 Gy = 104 ergs/gram)
- **Centigray (cGy):** 100 ergs/gram; equivalent to 100 rads
- **Sievert (Sv):** Equivalent dose based on biologic effects (replaced rem)
- **Millisievert (mSv):** For x-radiation, 1 mSv = 1 mGy

#### Determinants of Biologic Effects

**Rate of delivery:**
- Divided doses allow cells to repair damage during intervals
- Fractional doses have cumulative effect only to extent repair is incomplete
- Normal cells repair and recover more rapidly than tumor cells

**Field size:**
- Small, carefully shielded fields can tolerate higher doses
- Smaller doses to larger fields may be lethal

**Cell proliferation:**
- Rapidly dividing cells more vulnerable to injury
- DNA damage compatible with survival in nondividing cells (neurons, muscle cells)
- In dividing cells, DNA damage detected by sensors → p53 upregulation → cell-cycle arrest or apoptosis
- Tissues with high cell turnover (gonads, bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, GI mucosa) extremely vulnerable

**Hypoxia:**
- ROS production from radiolysis of water is main mechanism of DNA damage
- Tissue hypoxia (e.g., center of poorly vascularized tumors) may reduce damage and effectiveness of radiotherapy

**Vascular damage:**
- Endothelial cells moderately sensitive to radiation
- Damage may cause narrowing or occlusion of blood vessels
- Leads to impaired healing, fibrosis, chronic ischemic atrophy
- May appear months or years after exposure

#### DNA Damage and Carcinogenesis

**Most important cellular target:** DNA

**Types of damage:**
- Single-base damage
- Single- and double-strand breaks
- Crosslinks between DNA and protein

**Repair:**
- Simple defects reparable by enzyme systems
- Linked to cell-cycle regulation through ATM and p53
- Double-strand breaks may persist or be repaired imprecisely (error prone)
- If checkpoints not functioning, cells with abnormal genomes survive and may form tumors

![Fig. 8.14 Effects of ionizing radiation on DNA](Direct and indirect effects through free radical formation; leads to DNA damage, failed or aberrant repair, and consequences including cell death, teratogenesis, or carcinogenesis.)

#### Fibrosis

**Common consequence of cancer radiotherapy:**
- Develops in irradiated field weeks or months after irradiation
- Replacement of dead parenchymal cells by connective tissue
- Formation of scars and adhesions
- Contributes: vascular damage, killing of tissue stem cells, release of cytokines/chemokines promoting inflammation

![Fig. 8.15 Vascular changes and fibrosis of salivary glands](Normal salivary gland compared with fibrosis and vascular changes (fibrointimal thickening, arteriolar sclerosis) caused by radiation.)

#### MORPHOLOGY

**Chromosomal changes:**
- Deletions, breaks, translocations, fragmentation
- Disorderly mitotic spindle
- Polyploidy and aneuploidy
- Nuclear swelling, condensation, chromatin clumping
- Breaks in nuclear membrane
- Apoptosis
- Giant cells with pleomorphic or multiple nuclei

**Cytoplasmic changes:**
- Cytoplasmic swelling
- Mitochondrial distortion
- ER degeneration
- Plasma membrane breaks

**Vascular changes:**
- Immediate: dilation
- Later or with higher doses: endothelial cell swelling and vacuolation, necrosis of capillary and venule walls
- May rupture or undergo thrombosis
- Later: endothelial cell proliferation, collagenous hyalinization, thickening of media → marked narrowing or obliteration of lumina
- Increase in interstitial collagen → scarring and contractions

#### Effects on Organ Systems

![Fig. 8.16 Overview of major morphologic consequences of radiation injury](Early changes occur in hours to weeks; late changes in months to years.)

**Hematopoietic and lymphoid systems:**
- Extremely susceptible to radiation injury
- High dose levels: severe lymphopenia within hours; shrinkage of lymph nodes and spleen
- Directly destroys lymphocytes
- Sublethal doses: regeneration from viable progenitors
- Marrow aplasia is dose-dependent
- Marrow precursors sensitive to radiant energy

**Blood count effects:**
- Granulocytes (half-life <1 day): brief rise, then neutropenia within days; nadir at second week; recovery 2-3 months
- Platelets (half-life 10 days): thrombocytopenia by end of first week; delayed recovery
- Red cells (half-life 120 days): anemia after 2-3 weeks; may persist for months
- Higher doses: more severe cytopenias and prolonged recovery
- Very high doses: permanent aplastic anemia

**Environmental exposure and cancer development:**
- Any dividing cell with mutations has potential to become cancerous
- Acute or prolonged exposures resulting in doses of 100 mSv cause serious consequences including cancer
- Documented in atomic bombing survivors, Chernobyl survivors, patients receiving radiation therapy
- Risk greatest in children (CT scans increase leukemia and malignant brain tumor risk)

**Occupational exposure and cancer development:**
- Radon: product of uranium decay
- Carcinogenic agents: polonium-214 and polonium-218 ("radon daughters"); emit alpha particles
- Deposited in lung → lung carcinomas in uranium miners
- Risks present in homes with very high radon levels
- Little evidence radon contributes to lung cancer in general population

## Nutritional Diseases

### Overview

The balance between nutrition and health is delicate. Both lack of nutrition and overnutrition are major health concerns.

### Malnutrition

**A healthy diet provides:**
1. Sufficient energy (carbohydrates, fats, proteins) for daily metabolic needs
2. Essential amino acids and fatty acids for synthesis of structural and functional proteins and lipids
3. Vitamins and minerals as coenzymes or hormones in metabolic pathways
4. Calcium and phosphate as structural components

**Types of malnutrition:**
- **Primary malnutrition:** One or all components missing from diet
- **Secondary (conditional) malnutrition:** Dietary intake adequate; results from malabsorption, impaired use/storage, excess losses, or increased requirements

**Causes of secondary malnutrition:**
- GI diseases
- Chronic wasting diseases
- Acute critical illness

### Common Causes of Dietary Insufficiencies

**Poverty:**
- Homeless people, elderly persons, children of the poor
- In poor countries: droughts, crop failure, livestock deaths

**Ignorance:**
- Infants, adolescents, pregnant women have increased nutritional needs
- Iron deficiency in infants exclusively fed artificial milk diets
- Polished rice diets may lack adequate thiamine
- Iodine often lacking in regions removed from oceans

**Chronic alcoholism:**
- Malnutrition and vitamin deficiencies (especially thiamine, pyridoxine, folate, vitamin A)
- Due to dietary deficiency, defective GI absorption, abnormal utilization/storage, increased metabolic needs, increased loss
- Failure to recognize thiamine deficiency may result in irreversible brain damage (Korsakoff psychosis)

**Acute and chronic illnesses:**
- Basal metabolic rate accelerated (may double with extensive burns)
- Increased daily requirements for all nutrients
- Common in advanced cancer and AIDS

**Self-imposed dietary restriction:**
- Anorexia nervosa, bulimia
- Affect persons concerned about body image or fearing cardiovascular disease

**Other causes:**
- GI diseases, malabsorption syndromes
- Specific drug therapies
- Total parenteral nutrition

### Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM)

**WHO definition:** State characterized by weight for height ratio below 3 standard deviations of WHO standards

**Prevalence:**
- About 16 million children under age 5 affected worldwide
- As many as 25% of children in poor countries
- Major contributor to high death rates among very young
- In refugee camps (e.g., Syria): up to 20% of children severely or moderately malnourished

**Previously called protein energy malnutrition (PEM):**
- Range of clinical syndromes from dietary intake of protein and calories inadequate to meet needs
- Two ends of spectrum: **marasmus** and **kwashiorkor**

**Protein compartments:**
- **Somatic compartment:** Proteins in skeletal muscles (affected more in marasmus)
- **Visceral compartment:** Protein stores in visceral organs, primarily liver (depleted more in kwashiorkor)

**Diagnosis:**
- Compare body weight for given height against standard tables
- Other parameters: fat stores, muscle mass, serum protein levels (albumin, transferrin)
- Skinfold thickness reflects fat stores
- Midarm circumference reflects muscle mass
- Gut microbiome may play role in pathogenesis

### Marasmus

**Cause:** Diet severely lacking in calories

**Features:**
- Growth retardation and loss of muscle mass from catabolism and depletion of somatic protein compartment
- Adaptive response providing amino acids as energy source
- Visceral protein compartment depleted only marginally; serum albumin normal or slightly reduced
- Subcutaneous fat mobilized and used as fuel
- Low leptin production → stimulates hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis → high cortisol → lipolysis
- Extremities emaciated; head appears too large for body
- Anemia and multivitamin deficiency manifestations
- Immune deficiency, particularly T-cell–mediated immunity
- Concurrent infections usually present

![Fig. 8.17 (A) Marasmus](Loss of muscle mass and subcutaneous fat; head appears too large for emaciated body.)

### Kwashiorkor

**Cause:** Protein deprivation relatively greater than reduction in total calories

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common form in African children weaned too early and fed almost exclusively carbohydrates
- Name from Ga language (Ghana): illness in baby after arrival of another child
- Also prevalent in impoverished Southeast Asia
- Less severe forms may occur worldwide with chronic diarrheal states, protein-losing enteropathies, nephrotic syndrome, extensive burns
- Rare cases in U.S. from fad diets or replacing milk with rice-based beverages

**Features:**
- Marked protein deprivation with severe loss of visceral protein compartment
- Hypoalbuminemia → generalized or dependent edema
- Weight typically 60% to 80% of normal (loss masked by edema)
- Relative sparing of subcutaneous fat and muscle mass (may be masked by edema)
- Characteristic skin lesions: alternating zones of hyperpigmentation, desquamation, hypopigmentation ("flaky paint" appearance)
- Hair changes: loss of color, alternating pale and darker bands, straightening, fine texture, loss of firm attachment
- Enlarged, fatty liver (from reduced synthesis of carrier protein for lipoproteins)
- Apathy, listlessness, loss of appetite
- Vitamin deficiencies, immune defects, secondary infections
- Inflammation from infection produces catabolic state aggravating malnutrition

![Fig. 8.17 (B) Kwashiorkor](Generalized edema (ascites, puffiness of face, hands, legs).)

### Secondary Malnutrition

**In U.S.:** Often develops in chronically ill, older, bedridden patients

**Statistics:**
- More than 50% of older residents in nursing homes are malnourished
- Weight loss of more than 5% from malnutrition increases mortality risk almost 5-fold

**Cachexia:**
- Severe form in patients with advanced cancer
- Wasting all too apparent; often presages death
- May appear before appetite decreases
- Mechanisms involve cytokines (particularly TNF) secreted by tumor cells
- TNF stimulates degradation of skeletal muscle proteins and fat mobilization

### MORPHOLOGY - SAM

**Hallmark changes:**
1. Growth failure
2. Peripheral edema in kwashiorkor
3. Loss of body fat and atrophy of muscle (more marked in marasmus)

**Liver:**
- Kwashiorkor: enlarged and fatty
- Marasmus: not fatty
- Superimposed cirrhosis rare

**Small bowel (kwashiorkor, rarely marasmus):**
- Decreased mitotic index in crypts
- Mucosal atrophy and loss of villi and microvilli
- Decreased small intestinal enzymes (often disaccharidase deficiency)
- Lactate intolerance; may not respond to full-strength milk diets
- Mucosal changes reversible with treatment

**Bone marrow:**
- Hypoplastic in both kwashiorkor and marasmus
- Decreased red cell precursors
- Usually hypochromic, microcytic anemia (iron deficiency)
- Folate deficiency may cause mixed microcytic-macrocytic anemia

**Brain:**
- In infants born to malnourished mothers with SAM during first 1-2 years
- Cerebral atrophy, reduced number of neurons, impaired myelination

**Other changes:**
- Thymic and lymphoid atrophy (more marked in kwashiorkor)
- Anatomic alterations from intercurrent infections (helminths, parasites)
- Other nutrient deficiencies (iodine, vitamins)

### Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia

**Anorexia nervosa:**
- Self-induced starvation with marked weight loss
- Occurs primarily in previously healthy young women obsessed with attaining or maintaining thinness

**Bulimia:**
- Binge eating followed by induced vomiting
- More common than anorexia nervosa; better prognosis
- Occurs in 1% to 2% of women and 0.1% of men; average age at onset 20 years

**Anorexia nervosa features:**
- Similar to SAM
- Endocrine effects prominent
- Amenorrhea from decreased gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion
- Cold intolerance, bradycardia, constipation, skin and hair changes (decreased thyroid hormone)
- Dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities common
- Fine pale body hair (lanugo)
- Decreased bone density (low estrogen → osteoporosis)
- Anemia, lymphopenia, hypoalbuminemia
- Increased susceptibility to cardiac arrhythmia and sudden death (from hypokalemia)

**Bulimia features:**
- Binge eating with huge amounts of carbohydrates followed by induced vomiting
- Menstrual irregularities common; amenorrhea in less than 50%
- Major complications from induced vomiting and chronic laxative/diuretic use:
  - Electrolyte imbalances (hypokalemia) → cardiac arrhythmias
  - Pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents
  - Esophageal and stomach rupture
- Diagnosis must rely on comprehensive psychologic assessment

### Vitamin Deficiencies

**General principles:**
- 13 vitamins necessary for health
- Four fat-soluble: A, D, E, K
- Remainder water-soluble
- Fat-soluble vitamins stored more readily but poorly absorbed in fat malabsorption disorders
- Certain vitamins can be synthesized endogenously (vitamin D from precursors, vitamin K and biotin by intestinal flora, niacin from tryptophan)
- Deficiency of single vitamin uncommon; single- or multiple-vitamin deficiencies may accompany concurrent SAM

### Vitamin A

**Major functions:**
- Maintenance of normal vision
- Regulation of cell growth and differentiation
- Regulation of lipid metabolism

**Forms:**
- Retinol, retinal, retinoic acid (similar biologic activities)
- Retinol: transport form and storage form (as retinol ester)
- Retinoids: natural and synthetic chemicals structurally related to vitamin A

**Sources:**
- Animal-derived foods: liver, fish, eggs, milk, butter (preformed vitamin A)
- Yellow and leafy green vegetables: carrots, squash, spinach (carotenoids, many are provitamins)
- β-carotene: most important provitamin; efficiently converted to vitamin A

**Metabolism:**
- Absorption requires bile, pancreatic enzymes, antioxidant activity
- Absorbed through intestinal wall; β-carotene converted to retinol
- Transported in chylomicrons; taken up by liver cells via apolipoprotein E receptor
- More than 90% of vitamin A reserves stored in liver (in perisinusoidal stellate cells)
- Storage sufficient for at least 6 months
- Mobilized retinol binds to retinol-binding protein (RBP); uptake by peripheral tissues via RBP receptors
- Retinol may be stored as retinyl ester or oxidized to retinoic acid

![Fig. 8.18 Vitamin A metabolism](From intestinal cell through liver storage to peripheral tissues.)

**Functions:**

*Vision:*
- Component of rhodopsin (rod cells) and three iodopsins (cone cells)
- Synthesis of rhodopsin: retinol → all-trans-retinal → 11-cis-retinal → rhodopsin with opsin
- Photon causes 11-cis-retinal → all-trans-retinal → configuration changes → nerve impulse
- All-trans-retinal reduced to retinol and lost to retina; continuous supply needed

*Cell differentiation:*
- Vitamin A and retinoids regulate orderly differentiation of mucus-secreting columnar epithelium
- Deficiency: squamous metaplasia (keratinizing epithelium)
- Activation of retinoic acid receptors (RARs) releases corepressors; forms heterodimers with retinoic X receptor (RXR)
- RAR/RXR heterodimers bind to retinoic acid response elements in regulatory regions of genes

*Metabolic effects:*
- RXR activated by 9-cis retinoic acid
- Forms heterodimers with PPARs (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors)
- PPARs regulate fatty acid metabolism (oxidation in fat and muscle, adipogenesis, lipoprotein metabolism)

*Immunity:*
- Vitamin A supplementation reduces morbidity and mortality from some forms of diarrhea
- Reduces mortality and complications in preschool children with measles
- Infections may reduce bioavailability of vitamin A (acute phase response inhibits RBP synthesis)
- Maintains and restores integrity of gut epithelium

*Other effects:*
- β-carotene and related carotenoids: photoprotective and antioxidant agents
- Retinoids affect embryonic development, cellular differentiation and proliferation, lipid metabolism
- All-trans-retinoic acid used to treat acute promyelocytic leukemia (induces differentiation and apoptosis through PML-RARα fusion protein)

**Deficiency states:**

*Vision:*
- Early manifestation: impaired vision in reduced light (night blindness)

*Epithelial effects:*
- Xerophthalmia (dry eye):
  - Xerosis conjunctivae: dryness of conjunctiva (lachrymal and mucus-secreting epithelium replaced by keratinized)
  - Bitot spots: keratin debris in opaque plaques
  - Keratomalacia: erosion of corneal surface → softening and destruction → total blindness

*Other epithelial changes:*
- Squamous metaplasia of respiratory and urinary tract epithelium
- Loss of mucociliary epithelium → pulmonary infections
- Desquamation of keratin debris → renal and bladder stones
- Follicular or papular dermatosis from hyperkeratinization

*Immune deficiency:*
- Higher mortality from common infections (measles, pneumonia, infectious diarrhea)
- Dietary supplements reduce mortality 20% to 30%

![Fig. 8.19 Vitamin A deficiency](Major consequences in eye and keratinizing metaplasia of epithelial surfaces.)

**Vitamin A toxicity:**

*Acute toxicity:*
- From eating polar bear liver, whale/shark/tuna liver
- Symptoms: headache, dizziness, vomiting, stupor, blurred vision (confused with brain tumor)

*Chronic toxicity:*
- Weight loss, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, bone and joint pain
- Retinoic acid stimulates osteoclasts → increased bone resorption → high fracture risk
- Synthetic retinoids for acne not associated with these complications
- Use in pregnancy must be avoided (teratogenic effect)

### Vitamin D

**Major function:** Maintenance of adequate plasma levels of calcium and phosphorus

**Functions:**
- Prevents rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults), hypocalcemic tetany
- Maintains ionized calcium concentration in extracellular fluid

**Sources:**
- Endogenous synthesis in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol by UV light (approximately 90% of needed vitamin D)
- Dietary sources: deep-sea fish, plants, grains
- Plant sources: ergosterol (converted to vitamin D in body)

**Metabolism:**
1. Absorption or skin synthesis
2. Binding to plasma α1-globulin (vitamin D-binding protein) → transport to liver
3. Conversion to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH-D) by 25-hydroxylase in liver
4. Conversion to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25-(OH)2-D] (most active form) by α1-hydroxylase in kidney

**Regulation of renal production:**
- Hypocalcemia → stimulates PTH → augments conversion to 1,25-(OH)2-D
- Hypophosphatemia directly activates α1-hydroxylase
- Increased 1,25-(OH)2-D downregulates synthesis via negative feedback

![Fig. 8.20 (A) Normal vitamin D metabolism](B) Vitamin D deficiency: inadequate substrate for renal hydroxylase, yielding deficiency of 1,25-(OH)2-D, deficient absorption of calcium and phosphorus, depressed serum levels, hypocalcemia activating parathyroid glands.)

**Functions of 1,25-(OH)2-D:**
- Stimulates intestinal calcium absorption
- Stimulates calcium resorption in renal distal tubules
- Collaborates with PTH to regulate blood calcium (upregulates RANK ligand on osteoblasts → activates osteoclasts)
- Promotes bone mineralization (stimulates osteoblasts to synthesize osteocalcin)
- Effects on bone depend on plasma calcium levels

**Deficiency states:**

*Rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults):*
- Causes: diets deficient in calcium and vitamin D, limited sunlight exposure, renal disorders, malabsorption
- Milder deficiency (vitamin D insufficiency) common among elderly

**Mechanism:**
- Deficiency causes hypocalcemia
- PTH production increased: (1) activates renal α1-hydroxylase, (2) mobilizes calcium from bone, (3) decreases renal calcium excretion, (4) increases renal phosphate excretion
- Serum calcium near normal but hypophosphatemia persists → impaired bone mineralization

**MORPHOLOGY - Rickets:**

*Basic derangement:* Excess of unmineralized bone matrix

*Rachitic changes:*
- Overgrowth of epiphyseal cartilage (inadequate provisional calcification)
- Persistence of distorted, irregular masses of cartilage projecting into marrow cavity
- Deposition of osteoid matrix on inadequately mineralized cartilaginous remnants
- Disruption of orderly replacement of cartilage by osteoid matrix
- Enlargement and lateral expansion of osteochondral junction
- Abnormal overgrowth of capillaries and fibroblasts from microfractures and stress

*Gross skeletal changes:*
- Infants: softened occipital bones (craniotabes), frontal bossing, squared head
- Rachitic rosary: overgrowth of cartilage at costochondral junction
- Pigeon breast deformity: inward bending of ribs
- Harrison groove: inward pull at diaphragm margin
- Pelvic deformation
- Ambulating children: lumbar lordosis, bowing of legs

![Fig. 8.21 Rickets](A) Normal costochondral junction. (B) Rachitic changes: absent palisade of cartilage; paler trabeculae are uncalcified osteoid. (C) Bowing of legs from poorly mineralized bone.)

**Osteomalacia (adults):**
- Newly formed osteoid matrix inadequately mineralized
- Excess persistent osteoid
- Bone contours not affected but bone weak and vulnerable to fractures
- Vertebral bodies and femoral necks most affected
- Histology: unmineralized osteoid seen as thickened pink layer around basophilic mineralized trabeculae

**Nonskeletal effects of vitamin D:**
- Vitamin D receptor present in many nucleated cells
- Macrophages, keratinocytes, breast, prostate, colon can produce 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D
- Pathogen-induced activation of Toll-like receptors increases vitamin D receptor expression and local synthesis
- May enhance clearance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis
- Low levels associated with 30% to 50% increase in colon, prostate, breast cancer incidence

**Toxicity:**
- Megadoses of orally administered vitamin D can cause hypervitaminosis
- Children: metastatic calcifications of soft tissues (kidney)
- Adults: bone pain and hypercalcemia
- Toxic potential so great it is a potent rodenticide

### Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)

**Deficiency causes scurvy:**
- Bone disease in growing children
- Hemorrhages and healing defects in children and adults

**History:** British Royal Navy ("limeys") given lime and lemon juice to prevent scurvy; identified and synthesized in 1932

**Characteristics:**
- Not synthesized endogenously in humans; entirely dependent on diet
- Present in milk, liver, fish, fruits, vegetables

**Functions:**
- Activates prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases from inactive precursors
- Allows hydroxylation of procollagen
- Inadequately hydroxylated procollagen cannot acquire stable helical configuration or be adequately crosslinked
- Poorly secreted from fibroblasts; secreted molecules lack tensile strength, more soluble, vulnerable to enzymatic degradation
- Collagen (highest hydroxyproline content) most affected → predisposition to hemorrhages
- Also suppresses collagen synthesis (independent of hydroxylation effects)
- Has antioxidant properties (scavenges free radicals, regenerates vitamin E)

![Fig. 8.22 Major consequences of impaired collagen formation from vitamin C deficiency](Poor vessel support results in bleeding tendency; effects on gums, skin, osteoblasts, osteoid matrix, osteocytes, periosteum and joints.)

**Deficiency states:**
- Scurvy no longer global problem due to food abundance
- Sometimes encountered in affluent populations: elderly, people living alone, chronic alcoholics
- Occasionally in patients on peritoneal or hemodialysis
- Food faddists

**Toxicity:**
- Megadoses do not protect against common cold
- Mild antihistamine action may provide slight relief
- Excreted in urine but may cause uricosuria and increased iron absorption

### Obesity

**Definition:** State of increased body weight from adipose tissue accumulation of sufficient magnitude to produce adverse health effects

**Epidemiology:**
- Major public health problem in developed countries
- Emerging problem in developing nations (e.g., India)
- In U.S.: prevalence increased from 13% to 34% (1960-2008)
- As of 2015: 68.6% of Americans aged 20-75 overweight; 17% of children
- Globally: WHO estimates 700 million adults obese (2015)

**Measurement:**
- **Body mass index (BMI):** weight (kg) / height (m)^2
- Normal: 18.5 to 25 kg/m^2
- Overweight: 25 to 30 kg/m^2
- Obese: >30 kg/m^2

**Fat distribution:**
- Central (visceral) obesity: fat in trunk and abdominal cavity (mesentery, around viscera)
- Higher risk than diffuse subcutaneous fat accumulation

**Etiology:**
- Complex; genetic, environmental, psychologic factors
- Simply stated: disorder of energy balance
- Regulated by neural and hormonal mechanisms with internal set point ("lipostat")
- Key player: **leptin** (product of LEP gene, secreted by adipocytes)

### Energy Balance Regulatory Circuitry

**Three components:**

**Afferent (peripheral) system:**
- Generates signals from various sites
- Main components:
  - **Leptin** (from fat cells): reduces food intake
  - **Adiponectin** (from fat cells): directs fatty acids to muscle for oxidation; decreases hepatic triglycerides; increases insulin sensitivity
  - **Insulin** (from pancreas)
  - **Ghrelin** (from stomach): stimulates appetite ("meal-initiating" signal)
  - **Peptide YY** (from ileum and colon): satiety signal

**Central processing:**
- Arcuate nucleus in hypothalamus integrates peripheral signals
- Generates new signals via:
  - POMC (proopiomelanocortin) and CART (cocaine-and amphetamine-regulated transcript) neurons
  - NPY (neuropeptide Y) and AgRP (agouti-related peptide) neurons

**Efferent system:**
- Organized along two pathways:
  - **Anabolic circuits:** promote food intake and weight gain
  - **Catabolic circuits:** enhance energy expenditure and weight loss

**Efferent pathways:**
- POMC/CART neurons activate efferent neurons → enhance energy expenditure through α-melanocyte stimulating hormone (MSH) → reduce food intake (anorexigenic effect)
- MSH signals through melanocortin receptor-4 (MC4R)
- NPY/AgRP neurons activate efferent neurons → promote food intake (orexigenic effect) and weight gain

![Fig. 8.23 Energy balance regulatory circuitry](Afferent adiposity signals (leptin, ghrelin, insulin, peptide YY) delivered to hypothalamus; inhibit anabolic circuits and activate catabolic circuits; effector arms influence food intake and energy expenditure.)

### Leptin

**Characteristics:**
- Secreted by fat cells; output regulated by adequacy of fat stores
- BMI and body fat stores directly related to leptin secretion
- With abundant adipose tissue: leptin crosses blood-brain barrier to hypothalamus → reduces food intake (stimulates POMC/CART, inhibits NPY/AgRP)
- With inadequate fat stores: leptin secretion diminished → food intake increased
- Also increases energy expenditure by stimulating physical activity, thermogenesis

**Leptin resistance:**
- Anorexigenic response blunted in obesity despite high leptin levels
- Injections fail to affect food intake and energy expenditure in obese humans
- Bypassed by intraventricular injection in mice

**Genetic mutations:**
- Loss-of-function mutations in leptin gene or receptor: massive obesity (rare)
- Mutations in MC4R: found in 4% to 5% of patients with massive obesity
- Underscores importance of leptin pathway in body weight control

### Adiponectin

**Characteristics:**
- Produced in adipose tissue
- Called "fat-burning molecule" and "guardian angel against obesity"
- Directs fatty acids to muscle for oxidation
- Decreases hepatic triglyceride content and glucose production
- Increases insulin sensitivity
- Anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, anti-atherogenic, anti-proliferative, cardioprotective effects
- Serum levels lower in obese individuals

### Gut Hormones

**Ghrelin:**
- Produced in stomach and arcuate nucleus
- Increases food intake by stimulating NPY/AgRP neurons
- Levels rise before meals, fall 1-2 hours after
- Drop attenuated in obese persons
- Levels lower in obese individuals; increase with weight reduction
- After gastric bypass surgery: rise much reduced (beneficial effects may partly be due to reduced gastric mucosa surface exposed to food)

**Peptide YY (PYY):**
- Secreted from endocrine cells in ileum and colon in response to food consumption
- Decreases appetite; augments satiety
- Acts by stimulating POMC/CART neurons in hypothalamus
- Reduces gastric emptying and intestinal motility ("ileal brake")
- Levels reduced with obesity

### Gut Microbiome

- Profiles differ between genetically obese mice and lean littermates
- Microbiome of obese mice harvests much more energy from food
- Colonization of germfree mice with microbiota from obese mice → increased body weight
- Relevance to human obesity tantalizing but unproven

### Clinical Consequences of Obesity

**Metabolic syndrome:**
- Central obesity at center of cluster of alterations
- Characterized by abnormalities of glucose and lipid metabolism with hypertension and proinflammatory state
- Caused by inflammasome response to free fatty acids and excess lipids → IL-1 secretion → insulin resistance

**Type 2 diabetes:**
- Obesity associated with insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia
- Excess insulin plays role in sodium retention, blood volume expansion, norepinephrine excess, smooth muscle proliferation (hypertension)

**Cardiovascular disease:**
- Hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol
- Association with heart disease related more to diabetes and hypertension than weight per se

**Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease:**
- Commonly associated with obesity and type 2 diabetes
- Can progress to fibrosis and cirrhosis

**Cholelithiasis:**
- Six times more common in obese than lean subjects
- Mechanism: increased body cholesterol, increased cholesterol turnover, augmented biliary excretion

**Hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome):**
- Constellation of respiratory abnormalities in very obese persons
- Hypersomnolence (night and day), sleep apnea, polycythemia, right-sided heart failure

**Osteoarthritis:**
- Marked adiposity predisposing factor
- Cumulative effects of wear and tear

### Summary: Obesity

- Obesity is a disorder of energy balance regulated by neurohumoral mechanisms
- Central (visceral) obesity associated with higher disease risk than subcutaneous fat
- Key regulators: **leptin** (reduces food intake, increases energy expenditure) and **adiponectin** ("fat-burning molecule")
- Gut hormones **ghrelin** (stimulates appetite) and **PYY** (satiety signal) rapidly control eating
- Associated with insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, certain cancers, NAFLD, gallstones
- Leptin plays key role in energy balance; output regulated by fat stores

## Diet and Systemic Diseases

### Diet and Atherosclerosis

**Key question:** Can dietary modification reduce serum cholesterol and prevent/retard atherosclerosis (coronary heart disease)?

**Facts:**
- Average U.S. adult consumes large amount of fat and cholesterol daily
- Ratio of saturated to polyunsaturated fatty acids approximately 3:1
- Lowering saturates to level of polyunsaturates causes 10% to 15% reduction in serum cholesterol within weeks
- Vegetable oils (corn, safflower) and fish oils contain polyunsaturated fatty acids

**Omega-3 fatty acids:**
- In fish oil (n-3 family) have more double bonds than omega-6 in vegetable oils
- Dutch men with 30 g fish daily showed substantially lower coronary heart disease deaths
- Other studies show supplements do not reduce cardiovascular risk

**Other dietary effects:**
- Sodium restriction reduces hypertension
- Dietary fiber may prevent diverticulosis
- Caloric restriction increases life span in experimental animals
- Garlic possibly protects against heart disease (unproven)

### Diet and Cancer

**Three aspects of concern:**
1. Content of exogenous carcinogens
2. Endogenous synthesis of carcinogens from dietary components
3. Lack of protective factors

**Exogenous carcinogens:**
- Aflatoxin: important in hepatocellular carcinomas in Asia and Africa
- Causes specific TP53 mutation (codon 249)
- Mutation used as molecular signature for aflatoxin exposure

**Endogenous synthesis:**
- Nitrosamines and nitrosamides suspected in gastric carcinoma
- Formed from nitrites and amines/amides from digested proteins
- Sources: sodium nitrite (preservative), nitrates (vegetables) reduced by gut bacteria

**High fat/low fiber diet and colon cancer:**
- High fat intake increases bile acids in gut
- Modifies intestinal flora favoring growth of microaerophilic bacteria
- Bile acids or metabolites may be carcinogens or promoters
- High-fiber diet protective: (1) increased stool bulk and decreased transit time, (2) capacity to bind carcinogens

**Antioxidants and cancer prevention:**
- Vitamins C and E, β-carotenes, selenium assumed to have anticarcinogenic effects
- No convincing evidence they act as chemopreventive agents
- Retinoic acid promotes epithelial differentiation; may reverse squamous metaplasia

## Summary: Environmental and Nutritional Diseases

**Environmental diseases:**
- Caused by exposure to chemical or physical agents in ambient, workplace, and personal environments
- Xenobiotics enter through inhalation, ingestion, skin contact
- Two-phase metabolism involving cytochrome P-450

**Air pollution:**
- Common pollutants: ozone, sulfur dioxide, acid aerosols, particles <10 µm
- CO: air pollutant and cause of death from accidents/suicide

**Heavy metals:**
- Lead: CNS defects (children), peripheral neuropathy (adults)
- Mercury: contaminated fish; Minamata disease
- Arsenic: skin lesions, carcinomas, polyneuropathy
- Cadmium: obstructive lung disease, kidney damage

**Tobacco:**
- Most preventable cause of death
- 90% of lung cancers occur in smokers
- Causes atherosclerosis, multiple cancers

**Alcohol:**
- 100,000+ deaths annually in U.S.
- Causes fatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy
- Major risk factor for oral, laryngeal, esophageal cancers

**Drug injury:**
- ADRs affect 7-8% of hospitalized patients
- Acetaminophen: leading cause of acute liver failure
- Aspirin: blocks thromboxane A2

**Physical injury:**
- Burns: systemic effects from fluid shifts and infection
- Radiation: DNA damage, carcinogenesis, fibrosis

**Nutritional diseases:**
- SAM: marasmus and kwashiorkor spectrum
- Vitamins A, D, C most important with wide-ranging effects
- Obesity: disorder of energy balance regulated by leptin and other hormones
- Diet composition contributes to atherosclerosis and cancer
