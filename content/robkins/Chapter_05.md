# Chapter 5: Diseases of the Immune System

## Innate Immunity

### Components of Innate Immunity

The major components of **innate immunity** are:

- **Epithelial barriers** that block the entry of microbes
- **Phagocytic cells** (mainly neutrophils and macrophages)
- **Dendritic cells (DCs)**
- **Natural killer (NK) cells** and other innate lymphoid cells
- **Plasma proteins**, including proteins of the **complement system**

The complement system was discussed in Chapter 3.

### Pattern Recognition Receptors

**Phagocytes**, **dendritic cells**, and many other cells (such as epithelial cells) express receptors that sense the presence of infectious agents and substances released from dead cells:

- **Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)**: Microbial structures recognized by innate receptors; shared among microbes of the same type; essential for microbe survival (so microbes cannot evade recognition by mutating these molecules)
- **Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)**: Substances released from injured and necrotic cells
- **Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)**: Cellular receptors that recognize PAMPs and DAMPs

> **Key concept**: Innate immunity uses about 100 different receptors to recognize 1000 molecular patterns.

Receptors of innate immunity are located in all cellular compartments where pathogens may be present:

- **Plasma membrane receptors**: Detect extracellular pathogens
- **Endosomal receptors**: Detect ingested microbes
- **Cytosolic receptors**: Detect microbes in the cytoplasm

### Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs)

The **Toll-like receptors (TLRs)** are the best-known pattern recognition receptors:

- There are **10 TLRs in mammals** that recognize a wide range of microbial molecules
- **Plasma membrane TLRs**: Recognize bacterial products such as **lipopolysaccharide (LPS)**
- **Endosomal TLRs**: Recognize viral and bacterial **RNA** and **DNA**

Recognition of microbes by these receptors activates transcription factors that stimulate production of:

- **Mediators of inflammation**
- **Anti-viral cytokines** (interferons)
- **Proteins that promote lymphocyte activation** and adaptive immune responses

### NOD-Like Receptors and the Inflammasome

**NOD-like receptors (NLRs)** are cytosolic receptors named after the founding members **NOD-1** and **NOD-2**:

- Recognize a wide variety of substances:
  - Products of necrotic cells (e.g., **uric acid**, **released ATP**)
  - Ion disturbances (e.g., loss of **K⁺**)
  - Some microbial products
- Several NLRs signal via a cytosolic multiprotein complex called the **inflammasome**

**The Inflammasome Pathway:**

1. Sensor protein (**NLRP3**) detects danger signals
2. Adapter protein recruits **caspase-1** (inactive)
3. Caspase-1 is converted to active form
4. Active caspase-1 cleaves **pro-IL-1β** to generate biologically active **IL-1β**
5. IL-1β mediates inflammation, recruits leukocytes, and induces fever

**Clinical correlation**: Gain-of-function mutations in NLRs result in **autoinflammatory syndromes** (periodic fever syndromes), which respond well to **IL-1 antagonists**. Examples include:

- **Gout**: Recognition of urate crystals by NLRs
- **Obesity-associated type 2 diabetes**: Inflammation from lipid/cholesterol crystal deposition
- **Atherosclerosis**: Inflammation from cholesterol crystal deposition

### Other Receptors for Microbial Products

| Receptor Type | Location | Target | Function |
|--------------|----------|--------|----------|
| **C-type lectin receptors (CLRs)** | Plasma membrane of macrophages and DCs | Fungal glycans | Elicit inflammatory reactions to fungi |
| **RIG-like receptors** | Cytosol | Viral RNA | Stimulate anti-viral cytokine production |
| **Cytosolic DNA sensors** | Cytosol | Microbial DNA | Stimulate anti-viral responses |
| **G protein-coupled receptors** | Neutrophils, macrophages, leukocytes | N-formylmethionyl peptides | Chemotactic responses to bacterial proteins |
| **Mannose receptors** | Various cells | Microbial sugars with terminal mannose | Induce phagocytosis |

> **Note**: Because all bacterial proteins (and few mammalian proteins, only those synthesized within mitochondria) are initiated by **N-formylmethionine**, the N-formylmethionyl receptor enables neutrophils to detect bacterial proteins.

### Reactions of Innate Immunity

The innate immune system provides host defense through two main reactions:

1. **Inflammation**
   - Cytokines and products of complement activation trigger vascular and cellular components of inflammation
   - Recruited leukocytes destroy pathogens, ingest, and eliminate damaged cells
   - (Described in Chapter 3)

2. **Anti-viral defense**
   - **Type I interferons** produced in response to viruses
   - Act on infected and uninfected cells
   - Activate enzymes that degrade viral nucleic acids and inhibit viral replication

Additionally, innate immunity generates signals that stimulate the subsequent, more powerful **adaptive immune response**.

## Adaptive Immunity

The **adaptive immune system** consists of lymphocytes and their products, including **antibodies**. In contrast to the limited repertoire of innate immunity, adaptive immunity can recognize a vast array of foreign substances.

### Types of Adaptive Immunity

| Type | Mediator | Target |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Humoral immunity** | **Antibodies** produced by **B lymphocytes (B cells)** | Extracellular pathogens in blood, mucosal surfaces, and tissues |
| **Cell-mediated (cellular) immunity** | **T lymphocytes (T cells)** | Intracellular microbes |

**T lymphocyte functions:**
- **Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs)**: Directly kill infected cells
- **Helper T cells**: Produce cytokines that activate phagocytes to kill ingested microbes

## Cells and Tissues of the Immune System

### Lymphocytes

Lymphocytes are present in the circulation and various lymphoid organs. Although morphologically similar, they consist of several functionally and phenotypically distinct populations.

**Key features:**
- **Lymphocytes develop** from precursors in generative (primary) lymphoid organs
  - **T lymphocytes**: Mature in the **thymus**
  - **B lymphocytes**: Mature in the **bone marrow**
- Each lymphocyte and its progeny (a **clone**) express a **single antigen receptor**
- Total lymphocyte population (~10¹² in humans) can recognize tens or hundreds of millions of antigens
- **Antigen receptor diversity** is encoded by variant DNA sequences created during lymphocyte maturation

**Mature lymphocyte populations:**
- **Naïve lymphocytes**: Express antigen receptors but have not responded to antigens
- **Effector lymphocytes**: Activated by antigen; perform functions that eliminate microbes
- **Memory lymphocytes**: Survive in a functionally silent state after antigen elimination; respond rapidly upon re-exposure

Lymphocytes recirculate through peripheral (secondary) lymphoid organs—the **lymph nodes**, **spleen**, and **mucosal tissues**—where foreign antigens are concentrated and bound to activate specific lymphocyte clones (**clonal selection**).

### T Lymphocytes

**Thymus-derived T lymphocytes** develop into effector cells of cellular immunity and "help" B cells produce antibodies against protein antigens:

- T cells constitute **60% to 70%** of lymphocytes in peripheral blood
- Major lymphocyte population in splenic periarteriolar sheaths and lymph node interfollicular zones

**Important characteristics:**
- T cells **cannot recognize free or circulating antigens**
- **>95% of T cells** sense only **peptide fragments** of proteins displayed by **MHC molecules**
- T cells only recognize antigens presented by other cells

**T-Cell Receptor (TCR) Complex:**
- The **TCR** is a heterodimer composed of **α and β protein chains** (disulfide-linked)
- Each chain has:
  - **Variable region**: Binds specific peptide antigen
  - **Constant region**: Interacts with signaling molecules
- TCR is noncovalently linked to the **CD3 complex** (γ, δ, ε proteins) and **ζ chains**
- CD3 proteins and ζ chains do not bind antigens; they initiate intracellular signals after TCR recognition

**Coreceptor molecules:**

| Molecule | Expressed On | Binds | Function |
|----------|--------------|-------|----------|
| **CD4** | 50-60% of T cells | **Class II MHC molecules** on APCs | Helper T cell function |
| **CD8** | ~40% of T cells | **Class I MHC molecules** | Cytotoxic T cell function |

**CD4⁺ T cells (Helper T cells):**
- Secrete cytokines that help B cells produce antibodies
- Help macrophages destroy phagocytosed microbes
- **Central role** highlighted by HIV infection destroying these cells

**CD8⁺ T cells (Cytotoxic T lymphocytes/CTLs):**
- Secrete cytokines
- **Primary role**: Directly kill virus-infected cells and tumor cells

**Other T cell proteins:**
- **CD28**: Receptor for costimulators (B7 molecules) induced on APCs by microbes
- **Adhesion molecules**: Strengthen bond between T cells and APCs; control migration to tissues

**Regulatory T lymphocytes:** Suppress immune responses (described later in context of tolerance).

**γδ T cells:**
- Express TCRs composed of **γ and δ chains** (do not express CD4 or CD8)
- Recognize nonprotein molecules (e.g., bacterial lipoglycans)
- Functional roles not well understood

**NKT cells:**
- Express markers of both T cells and NK cells
- Recognize microbial glycolipids
- May play a role in defense against some infections
- Antigen receptors recognize antigens independently of MHC molecules

### Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) Molecules

**MHC molecules** are fundamental to T-cell recognition of antigens. The **human MHC** is known as the **HLA (human leukocyte antigen) complex** on chromosome 6.

> **Key concept**: Each person expresses only one set of HLA genes. This phenomenon is called **MHC restriction**—T cells recognize only peptides displayed by that person's own MHC molecules.

**Class I MHC molecules:**
- Expressed on **all nucleated cells**
- Encoded by three loci: **HLA-A**, **HLA-B**, **HLA-C**
- Structure: Polymorphic **α chain** + invariant **β₂-microglobulin**
- **Peptide-binding cleft** contains polymorphic residues
- Bind peptides derived from **cytosolic proteins** (e.g., viral and tumor antigens)
- Conserved region binds **CD8** (ensures only CD8⁺ T cells respond)

**Class II MHC molecules:**
- Encoded by genes in **HLA-D region** (subregions: **DP**, **DQ**, **DR**)
- Structure: Heterodimers of **α and β subunits**
- Expression restricted to **APCs** (notably dendritic cells), **macrophages**, and **B cells**
- **Peptide-binding cleft** for antigenic peptides
- Bind peptides derived from **extracellular proteins** (ingested microbes)
- Bind **CD4**

**Other proteins encoded in MHC locus:**
- **Complement components**: C2, C3, Factor B
- **Cytokines**: **TNF**, **lymphotoxin**

**HLA polymorphism:**
- Highly polymorphic—thousands of alleles at each locus
- >10,000 total HLA genes; >3,500 HLA-B alleles alone
- **Polymorphism evolved** to enable display of and response to any conceivable microbial peptide
- **HLA genes are closely linked** on chromosome 6—passed en bloc from parent to offspring
- Each set of maternal and paternal HLA genes is an **HLA haplotype**
- **Siblings**: 25% chance of sharing the same two HLA haplotypes
- Unrelated donors: Very low probability of matching

> **Transplantation implication**: Each person has unique HLA alleles. Identical twins can accept grafts from each other without rejection. Even HLA-matched sibling grafts may be rejected due to differences in **minor histocompatibility loci**.

### B Lymphocytes

**B (bone marrow-derived) lymphocytes** produce antibodies (mediators of humoral immunity):

- Comprise **10% to 20%** of circulating peripheral lymphocyte population
- Also present in bone marrow and follicles of peripheral lymphoid organs

**B cell antigen recognition:**
- Membrane-bound **antibody of the IgM class** expressed on surface with signaling molecules (**B-cell receptor/BCR complex**)
- B cells recognize many chemical structures **without requiring MHC**:
  - Soluble or cell-associated proteins
  - Lipids, polysaccharides, nucleic acids, small chemicals
- Each antibody has a unique amino acid sequence (created by gene segment rearrangement)

**B cell activation molecules:**
- **Igα and Igβ**: Signaling molecules attached to BCR
- **CD21 (CR2)**: Receptor for complement breakdown product deposited on microbes

> **Note**: **Epstein-Barr virus** uses CD21 as a receptor to bind and infect B cells.

**After stimulation**, B cells differentiate into **plasma cells** that secrete large amounts of antibodies.

**Immunoglobulin isotypes:**

| Isotype | Location/Function |
|---------|------------------|
| **IgG** | Most abundant in circulation; crosses placenta; opsonization |
| **IgM** | First antibody produced; effective at complement activation |
| **IgA** | Major isotype in mucosal secretions |
| **IgE** | Present at very low concentrations; binds mast cells |
| **IgD** | Expressed on B cell surfaces; secreted at very low levels |

These isotypes differ in ability to activate complement and recruit inflammatory cells.

### Natural Killer (NK) Cells and Innate Lymphoid Cells

**NK cells** arise from the same common lymphoid progenitor as T and B lymphocytes but are **innate immune cells**:
- Functional **without prior activation**
- Do **not** express highly variable clonally distributed receptors for antigens

**NK cell receptors:**
- **Inhibitory receptors**: Recognize self **class I MHC molecules** (expressed on all healthy cells)
- **Activating receptors**: Recognize molecules expressed/upregulated on stressed or infected cells

> **Balance**: Normally, inhibitory receptor effects dominate over activating receptors, preventing spontaneous NK cell activation. During infections (especially viral) and stress, class I MHC expression decreases and activating receptor ligands increase, leading to NK cell activation.

**NK cell functions:**
- Kill infected or stressed cells
- Secrete **interferon-γ (IFN-γ)**, which activates macrophages to destroy ingested microbes
- Provide early defense against intracellular microbial infections

**Innate Lymphoid Cells (ILCs):**
- Lack TCRs but produce cytokines similar to T cells
- Classified into three groups producing:
  - **IFN-γ** (Group 1—related to TH1)
  - **IL-5** (Group 2—related to TH2)
  - **IL-17** (Group 3—related to TH17)
- NK cells are related to **Group 1 ILCs** based on IFN-γ production
- Mostly reside in tissues; provide early defense before T cells activate

### Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs)

**Dendritic cells (DCs)** are the most important APCs for initiating T-cell responses against protein antigens:

- Named for their numerous fine cytoplasmic processes (dendrites)

**Features of DCs:**
1. Located at sites of microbe entry (under epithelia) and in all tissue interstitia
   - Epidermal DCs called **Langerhans cells**
2. Express many receptors for capturing microbes: **TLRs** and **C-type lectin receptors**
3. Recruited to T-cell zones of lymphoid organs in response to microbes
4. Express high levels of **MHC** and molecules needed for T cell activation

**Plasmacytoid DCs:**
- Resemble plasma cells
- Present in blood and lymphoid organs
- Major sources of **type I interferon** (anti-viral cytokine)

**Follicular Dendritic Cells (FDCs):**
- Located in germinal centers of lymphoid follicles (spleen, lymph nodes)
- Bear **Fc receptors for IgG** and **receptors for C3b**
- Trap antigen bound to antibodies or complement proteins
- Display antigens to B lymphocytes (not involved in capturing antigens for T cells)

**Other APCs:**
- **Macrophages**: Ingest microbes and present peptides to T lymphocytes; T cells activate macrophages (central reaction of cell-mediated immunity)
- **B cells**: Present peptides to helper T cells and receive signals stimulating antibody responses

### Lymphoid Tissues

**Generative (primary/central) lymphoid organs:**
- **Thymus**: Site of T cell development
- **Bone marrow**: Site of production of all blood cells; B cell maturation

**Peripheral (secondary) lymphoid organs:**
- Sites where adaptive immune responses to microbes are initiated
- **Lymph nodes**: Encapsulated, highly organized collections along lymphatic channels
  - Sample antigens carried in lymph from interstitial fluids
  - DCs transport antigens from epithelia and tissues via lymphatic vessels
- **Spleen**: Important for immune responses to bloodborne antigens
  - Blood flows through sinusoids; enables trapping of antigens by resident DCs and macrophages
- **Cutaneous and mucosal lymphoid systems**: Located under epithelia of skin, GI, and respiratory tracts
  - Respond to antigens entering through epithelial breaches
  - Examples: **Pharyngeal tonsils**, **Peyer's patches**

**Distribution of lymphocytes in tissues:**

| Tissue | Approximate Number of Lymphocytes |
|--------|-----------------------------------|
| Lymph nodes | Multiple compartments |
| Spleen | Multiple compartments |
| Bone marrow | Significant numbers |
| Blood | Circulating pool |
| Skin | Resident population |
| Intestines | Large numbers (second only to lymph nodes) |
| Liver | Resident population |
| Lungs | Resident population |

**Within peripheral lymphoid organs:**
- **T lymphocytes**: Concentrated in **paracortex** (parafollicular cortex)
- **B lymphocytes**: Concentrated in **follicles** (around periphery/cortex)
  - Follicles with germinal centers indicate recent B cell response
- **Follicles contain FDCs** for B cell activation
- **Paracortex contains DCs** for antigen presentation to T lymphocytes
- In spleen: T cells in **periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths**; B cells in follicles

**Lymphocyte recirculation:**
- Naïve lymphocytes circulate through peripheral lymphoid organs where antigens are concentrated
- Effector lymphocytes migrate to sites of infection
- Important for T cells: patrol widely distributed lymphoid organs to find antigens
- **Plasma cells** do not migrate—secrete antibodies transported via blood and lymph to distant tissues

### Cytokines: Messenger Molecules of the Immune System

**Cytokines** are secreted proteins that mediate immune and inflammatory reactions. When defined molecularly, they are called **interleukins**.

**Characteristics:**
- Most have wide spectrum of effects
- Many produced by several cell types
- Most act on producing cell or neighboring cells
- Some (like IL-1) have systemic effects

**Cytokines in innate immune responses:**
- Produced rapidly after microbes are encountered
- Function: Induce inflammation, inhibit virus replication
- Include: **TNF**, **IL-1**, **IL-12**, **type I IFNs**, **IFN-γ**, **chemokines**
- Sources: **Macrophages**, **DCs**, **ILCs**, **NK cells** (also endothelial and epithelial cells)

**Cytokines in adaptive immune responses:**
- Produced principally by **CD4⁺ T lymphocytes** activated by antigen
- Function: Promote lymphocyte proliferation/differentiation, activate effector cells
- Main ones: **IL-2**, **IL-4**, **IL-5**, **IL-17**, **IFN-γ**
- Some limit/terminate responses: **TGF-β**, **IL-10**

**Cytokines stimulating hematopoiesis (colony-stimulating factors):**
- Stimulate formation of blood cell colonies from bone marrow progenitors
- Function: Increase leukocyte numbers; replace consumed leukocytes
- Sources: Marrow stromal cells, T lymphocytes, macrophages
- Examples: **GM-CSF**, **IL-7**

**Therapeutic applications:**
- **Inhibiting cytokines**: Control harmful inflammation (e.g., **TNF antagonists** for rheumatoid arthritis)
- **Administering cytokines**: Boost reactions (e.g., hematopoiesis, defense against viruses)

## Overview of Lymphocyte Activation and Adaptive Immune Responses

Adaptive immune responses develop in steps:
1. **Antigen recognition**
2. **Activation, proliferation, differentiation** of specific lymphocytes into effector and memory cells
3. **Elimination of antigen**
4. **Decline of response** (memory cells survive long-term)

### Capture and Display of Antigens

**Problem**: Microbes enter body anywhere; impossible for lymphocytes of every specificity to patrol every portal of entry.

**Solution**: 
- Microbes and protein antigens in epithelia/tissues are captured by **resident dendritic cells**
- DCs carry antigenic cargo to **draining lymph nodes** through which T cells recirculate
- Antigens are processed and displayed complexed with **MHC molecules** on cell surface
- Soluble antigens are captured and concentrated in follicles in lymph nodes and spleen for B cell recognition

**Costimulatory signals:**
- Microbe activates APCs via **pattern recognition receptors**
- APCs express molecules called **costimulators** and secrete cytokines
- Principal costimulators for T cells: **B7 proteins (CD80 and CD86)**
  - Expressed on APCs
  - Recognized by **CD28 receptor** on naïve T cells

> **Two-signal requirement**: Antigen ("signal 1") + costimulatory molecules produced during innate immune responses to microbes ("signal 2"). This ensures adaptive immune responses are induced by microbes, not harmless substances.

In immune responses to tumors and transplants, "signal 2" may be provided by substances released from necrotic cells (DAMPs).

### Cell-Mediated Immunity: Activation of T Lymphocytes and Elimination of Intracellular Microbes

**Process:**
1. Naïve T lymphocytes are activated by antigen and costimulators in peripheral lymphoid organs
2. Proliferate and differentiate into effector cells
3. Migrate to sites where antigen (microbe) is present

**Early CD4⁺ helper T cell responses:**
- Secretion of **IL-2** and expression of high-affinity **IL-2 receptors**
- IL-2 stimulates proliferation → increase in antigen-specific lymphocytes

**Helper T cell functions:**
- Mediated by **CD40-ligand (CD40L)** and **cytokines**
- CD40 (TNF-receptor family) engages CD40L on macrophages or B cells → activates them

**Effector CD4⁺ T cell subsets:**

| Subset | Signature Cytokines | Target Cells | Function | Host Defense | Disease Role |
|--------|---------------------|-------------|----------|--------------|-------------|
| **TH1** | **IFN-γ** | Macrophages | Classical macrophage activation | Intracellular pathogens | Autoimmunity, chronic inflammation |
| **TH2** | **IL-4, IL-5, IL-13** | Eosinophils, epithelial cells | IgE production, mucus secretion, alternative macrophage activation | Helminths | Allergy |
| **TH17** | **IL-17, IL-22** | Neutrophils | Recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes | Extracellular bacteria, fungi | Autoimmunity |

**CTL activation (CD8⁺ T cells):**
- Differentiate into CTLs that kill cells harboring cytoplasmic microbes
- **Principal killing mechanism**: **Perforin-granzyme system**
  - Perforin and granzymes stored in CTL granules
  - Released when CTLs engage targets (cells bearing appropriate class I MHC-bound peptides)
  - Perforin promotes entry of granzymes → activate caspases → apoptosis

**Regulation of T cell responses:**
- Balance between costimulatory and inhibitory receptors
- **CD28**: Major costimulatory receptor (binds B7)
- **CTLA-4** and **PD-1**: Coinhibitory receptors (block signals from TCR and CD28)
- Blocking coinhibitors enhances anti-tumor immune responses

### Humoral Immunity: Activation of B Lymphocytes and Elimination of Extracellular Microbes

Upon activation, B lymphocytes proliferate and differentiate into plasma cells secreting different classes of antibodies.

**Two major pathways of B cell activation:**

1. **T cell-independent**
   - Polysaccharide and lipid antigens have multiple identical epitopes
   - Simultaneously engage and cross-link several antibody molecules on each B cell
   - Initiate B cell activation

2. **T cell-dependent**
   - Protein antigens require help from **CD4⁺ T cells**
   - B cells act as APCs—ingest protein antigens, degrade, display peptides bound to **class II MHC**
   - Helper T cells express **CD40L** and secrete cytokines → activate B cells

**Antibody production:**
- Some progeny differentiate into **antibody-secreting plasma cells**
- Each plasma cell secretes antibodies with same specificity as original B cell receptor
- Polysaccharides/lipids → mainly **IgM**
- Protein antigens → production of **IgG, IgA, IgE** (via heavy-chain class/isotype switching)

**Helper T cell functions in B cell responses:**
- Stimulate **isotype switching** (different antibody functions, same specificity)
- Stimulate **affinity maturation** (antibodies with higher antigen affinity)
- These processes occur in **germinal centers**
- Helper T cells in germinal centers called **follicular helper T (Tfh) cells**

**Functions of antibodies:**

| Function | Mechanism | Antibody Type |
|---------|----------|---------------|
| **Neutralization** | Antibodies bind to microbes, prevent infection | All types |
| **Opsonization** | IgG coats microbes; phagocytes have Fc receptors | IgG |
| **Complement activation** | IgG and IgM activate classical pathway | IgG, IgM |
| **Mucosal immunity** | IgA secreted in mucosal tissues | IgA |
| **Transplacental transfer** | IgG actively transported across placenta | IgG |
| **Helminth killing** | IgE coats parasites; works with mast cells and eosinophils | IgE |

**Circulating IgG** has half-life of ~3 weeks (longer than most blood proteins due to special recycling mechanisms).

**Long-lived plasma cells** migrate to bone marrow and live for years, producing low levels of antibodies.

### Decline of Immune Responses and Immunologic Memory

- Majority of effector lymphocytes **die by apoptosis** after pathogen elimination
- Initial activation generates **long-lived memory cells** (may survive years)
- Memory cells:
  - More numerous than naïve cells specific for any antigen
  - Respond faster and more effectively upon re-exposure

> **Vaccination goal**: Generate memory cells.

## Hypersensitivity: Immunologically Mediated Tissue Injury

Immune responses that normally are protective are also capable of causing tissue injury. These are called **hypersensitivity reactions**, and resulting diseases are **hypersensitivity diseases**.

**Causes of pathologic immune responses:**
- Immune responses may be inadequately controlled
- Directed against normally harmless antigens
- Inappropriately targeted to host tissues

### Causes of Hypersensitivity Reactions

| Cause | Description | Examples |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Autoimmunity** | Reactions against self antigens | Failure of self-tolerance → autoimmune diseases |
| **Reactions against microbes** | Excessive reactions to microbial antigens; persistent antigens; cross-reactivity | Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis; tuberculosis; rheumatic heart disease |
| **Reactions against environmental antigens** | Unusual immune responses to noninfectious, harmless antigens | Allergies to pollens, animal danders, dust mites, metal ions, drugs |

**General principles:**
- Tissue injury mediated by same mechanisms that eliminate pathogens (antibodies, effector T lymphocytes, effector cells)
- Problem: stimuli difficult/impossible to eliminate (self antigens, persistent microbes, environmental antigens)
- Immune system has positive feedback loops; once started, difficult to control
- These diseases tend to be chronic and debilitating
- Often grouped as **immune-mediated inflammatory diseases**

### Classification of Hypersensitivity Reactions

**Four types** based on principal immune mechanism:

| Type | Name | Immune Mechanism | Histopathologic Lesions | Examples |
|------|------|-----------------|------------------------|---------|
| **Type I** | Immediate (allergy) | IgE antibody → mast cell activation → vasoactive amines and mediators | Vascular dilation, edema, smooth muscle contraction, mucus production | Anaphylaxis, allergies, bronchial asthma (atopic) |
| **Type II** | Antibody-mediated | IgG/IgM antibodies bind to cell/tissue antigens → phagocytosis, lysis, inflammation | Phagocytosis and lysis of cells; inflammation; functional derangements | Autoimmune hemolytic anemia, Goodpasture syndrome |
| **Type III** | Immune complex–mediated | Antigen-antibody complexes deposit → complement activation → leukocyte recruitment | Inflammation, necrotizing vasculitis (fibrinoid necrosis) | SLE, some forms of glomerulonephritis, serum sickness, Arthus reaction |
| **Type IV** | Cell-mediated (delayed) | Activated T lymphocytes → cytokine release → inflammation; T cell cytotoxicity | Perivascular cellular infiltrates, edema, granuloma formation, cell destruction | Contact dermatitis, multiple sclerosis, type 1 diabetes, tuberculosis |

> **Note**: Several immune reactions may coexist in one disease. Classification helps predict clinical manifestations and guide therapy.

### Immediate (Type I) Hypersensitivity

**Characteristics:**
- Tissue reaction occurs rapidly (typically within minutes)
- Interaction of antigen with **IgE antibody bound to mast cell surfaces**
- Initiated by **allergen** (antigen that triggers allergy)
- May occur as:
  - Local reaction (annoying): Seasonal rhinitis, hay fever
  - Debilitating: Asthma
  - Fatal: Anaphylaxis

**Sequence of Events:**

1. **Activation of TH2 cells and production of IgE antibody**
   - Allergens introduced by inhalation, ingestion, or injection
   - TH2 cells secrete **IL-4, IL-5, IL-13**
   - IL-4 stimulates B cells to undergo heavy-chain class switching to **IgE**
   - IL-5 activates eosinophils
   - IL-13 acts on epithelial cells, stimulates mucus secretion

2. **Sensitization of mast cells by IgE**
   - Mast cells derived from bone marrow precursors
   - Widely distributed in tissues (near blood vessels, nerves, subepithelial locations)
   - Express **FcεRI** (high-affinity receptor for Fc portion of IgE)
   - Even low serum IgE saturates these receptors
   - Antibody-bearing mast cells are "sensitized" to react if specific allergen binds

3. **Activation of mast cells and release of mediators**
   - Allergen binds to IgE on mast cells → cross-linking → biochemical signals → secretion

**Mediators of immediate hypersensitivity:**

| Category | Examples | Effects |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Vasoactive amines** | **Histamine** | Vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, smooth muscle contraction, mucus secretion |
| **Rapidly released mediators** | Chemotactic factors for neutrophils/eosinophils, **tryptase** | Tissue damage, kinin and complement activation |
| **Lipid mediators** | **Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2)**, **Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTB4)** | Bronchospasm, increased mucus secretion, vasodilation, chemotaxis |
| **Cytokines** | **TNF**, chemokines, **IL-4, IL-5** | Leukocyte recruitment/activation, amplify TH2 response |

**Why TH2 responses evolved** (protective functions):
- Important in combating parasitic infections
- IgE targets helminths for destruction by eosinophils and mast cells
- Mast cells involved in defense against bacterial infections
- Mast cell granule proteases may degrade snake venoms

**Development of Allergies:**

- **Atopy**: Increased propensity for immediate hypersensitivity reactions
- Atopic individuals have higher serum **IgE** and more **IL-4-producing TH2 cells**
- **50%** of atopic individuals have positive family history of allergy

**Genetic factors in allergy susceptibility:**
- **HLA molecules** (immune responsiveness to particular allergens)
- **Cytokines** controlling TH2 responses
- Component of **FcεRI**
- **ADAM33** (metalloproteinase involved in airway tissue remodeling)

**Environmental factors:**
- Environmental pollutants are important predisposing factors
- Dogs and cats in same environment as humans develop allergies; wild chimps do not despite genetic similarity
- Viral infections of airways are important triggers for **bronchial asthma**

> **Hygiene hypothesis**: Early childhood exposure to microbial antigens educates the immune system to prevent pathologic responses to environmental allergens. Too much hygiene may increase allergies later.

**Non-antigenic triggers**: ~20-30% of immediate hypersensitivity reactions triggered by temperature extremes, exercise (not TH2 cells or IgE; mast cells abnormally sensitive to nonimmune stimuli).

**Phases of immediate hypersensitivity:**

| Phase | Timing | Characteristics |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **Immediate response** | 5-30 minutes after exposure | Vasodilation, vascular leakage, smooth muscle spasm; subsides by 60 minutes |
| **Late-phase reaction** | 2-24 hours later | Inflammation, tissue destruction; mucosal epithelial cell damage |

**Late-phase reaction cells:** Neutrophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes (especially TH2 cells)

**Eosinophil products:**
- **Major basic protein**, **eosinophil cationic protein** (toxic to epithelial cells)
- **LTC4**, **platelet-activating factor** (promote inflammation)

**Clinical manifestations of Type I hypersensitivity:**

| Syndrome | Manifestations |
|----------|---------------|
| **Anaphylaxis** (drugs, bee sting, food) | Fall in blood pressure (shock), vascular dilation, airway obstruction (laryngeal edema), GI symptoms |
| **Bronchial asthma** | Airway obstruction, bronchial smooth muscle hyperactivity, inflammation, tissue injury |
| **Allergic rhinitis/sinusitis (hay fever)** | Increased mucus secretion, inflammation of upper airways and sinuses |
| **Food allergies** | Increased peristalsis, vomiting, diarrhea |

### Antibody-Mediated (Type II) Hypersensitivity

**Mechanism**: Antibodies directed against target antigens on cell surfaces or tissue components.

**Antibody-mediated tissue injury:**

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Opsonization and phagocytosis** | Cells coated (opsonized) with autoantibodies ± complement → phagocytosis by neutrophils/macrophages |
| **Inflammation** | Antibodies activate complement → recruit neutrophils and monocytes |
| **Cellular dysfunction** | Antibodies impair or dysregulate functions without causing injury |

**Examples of Type II diseases:**

| Disease | Target Antigen | Mechanism | Manifestations |
|---------|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Autoimmune hemolytic anemia** | Red blood cell membrane proteins | Opsonization, phagocytosis | Hemolysis, anemia |
| **Autoimmune thrombocytopenic purpura** | Platelet membrane proteins (GpIIb:IIIa) | Opsonization, phagocytosis | Bleeding |
| **Pemphigus vulgaris** | Intercellular junction proteins (desmogleins) | Antibody-mediated activation of proteases | Skin vesicles (bullae) |
| **Vasculitis (ANCA)** | Neutrophil granule proteins | Neutrophil degranulation | Vasculitis |
| **Goodpasture syndrome** | Basement membrane proteins (kidney, lung) | Complement- and Fc receptor–mediated inflammation | Nephritis, lung hemorrhage |
| **Acute rheumatic fever** | Streptococcal antigen; cross-reacts with myocardial antigen | Inflammation, macrophage activation | Myocarditis, arthritis |
| **Myasthenia gravis** | Acetylcholine receptor | Antibody inhibits neurotransmitter binding | Muscle weakness, paralysis |
| **Graves disease** | TSH receptor | Antibody stimulates receptor without hormone | Hyperthyroidism |
| **Pernicious anemia** | Intrinsic factor of gastric parietal cells | Neutralization of intrinsic factor | Abnormal erythropoiesis, anemia |

### Immune Complex–Mediated (Type III) Hypersensitivity

**Mechanism**: Antigen-antibody (immune) complexes formed in circulation deposit in blood vessels → complement activation → acute inflammation.

**Sources of antigen-antibody complexes:**
- **Exogenous**: Foreign protein (injection, infectious microbe)
- **Endogenous**: Self antigens (autoimmunity)

**Common sites of deposition:** Kidney (glomerulonephritis), joints (arthritis), small blood vessels (vasculitis)

**Systemic Immune Complex Disease (e.g., Serum Sickness):**

*Three phases:*

1. **Formation of immune complexes**
   - Protein antigen triggers immune response → antibodies produced (~1 week after injection)
   - Antibodies react with circulating antigen → form antigen-antibody complexes

2. **Deposition of immune complexes**
   - Medium-sized complexes (formed when antigen slightly in excess) are most pathogenic
   - Deposited in tissues where blood is filtered at high pressure (glomeruli, joints)

3. **Inflammation and tissue injury**
   - Complexes activate complement → recruit neutrophils
   - Leukocyte Fc receptors engage antibodies
   - Release of lysosomal enzymes and toxic molecules
   - Clinical features: Fever, urticaria, joint pain (arthralgia), lymphadenopathy, proteinuria

**Morphology of immune complex injury:**
- **Acute vasculitis** with fibrinoid necrosis and neutrophilic infiltration
- Kidney: Granular deposits of immunoglobulin and complement (immunofluorescence); electron-dense deposits along glomerular basement membrane

**Local Immune Complex Disease (Arthus Reaction):**
- Experimental model: Antigen injected into skin of immunized animal with preformed antibody
- Immune complexes form in vascular wall → acute vasculitis
- Evolves over hours; peaks at 4-10 hours
- Edema, hemorrhage, sometimes ulceration

### T Cell–Mediated (Type IV) Hypersensitivity

**Mechanism**: Two types of T cell reactions cause tissue injury:

1. **Cytokine-mediated inflammation** (mainly from CD4⁺ T cells)
2. **Direct cell cytotoxicity** (from CD8⁺ T cells)

**CD4⁺ T Cell–Mediated Inflammation:**

- Cytokines produced by T cells induce inflammation that may be chronic and destructive
- Prototype: **Delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH)**
  - Cutaneous reaction 24-48 hours after antigen administration (in contrast to immediate)
  - Naïve T cells activated in secondary lymphoid organs by dendritic cells
  - Differentiate into **TH1** and **TH17** effector cells under cytokine influence
  - TH1 cells secrete **IFN-γ** → activate macrophages
  - TH17 cells secrete **IL-17** → recruit neutrophils

**Clinical example: Tuberculin reaction (PPD skin test)**
- Intradermal injection of purified protein derivative (PPD/tuberculin)
- Previously exposed individuals: Redness and induration at 8-12 hours; peak at 24-72 hours
- Morphology: Mononuclear cell accumulation (CD4⁺ T cells, macrophages) around venules ("perivascular cuffing")

**Granulomatous inflammation:**
- Chronic DTH reactions against persistent microbes
- Over 2-3 weeks: Perivascular CD4⁺ T cell infiltrate replaced by macrophages
- Macrophages become large, flat, eosinophilic (**epithelioid cells**)
- Fusion under cytokine influence (e.g., IFN-γ) → **multinucleate giant cells**
- **Granuloma**: Aggregate of epithelioid cells surrounded by lymphocytes
- May develop fibroblast/connective tissue rim

**Contact dermatitis:**
- Common example of DTH tissue injury
- Caused by: **Urushiol** (poison ivy/oak), therapeutic drugs
- Environmental chemical binds to self proteins → modified peptides recognized by T cells
- Drug reactions often manifest as skin rashes

**CD8⁺ T Cell–Mediated Cytotoxicity:**
- CD8⁺ CTLs kill antigen-expressing target cells
- Important in: Type 1 diabetes, graft rejection, viral infections
- Virus-infected cells display viral peptides on class I MHC → recognized by CD8⁺ TCR → killing eliminates infection
- Sometimes responsible for cell damage (e.g., viral hepatitis)
- CD8⁺ T cells also produce IFN-γ and involve inflammatory reactions

## Autoimmune Diseases

**Autoimmunity**: Immune reactions against self ("auto") antigens.

**Autoimmune diseases** affect at least **1-2%** of the U.S. population.

**Classification:**

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Organ-specific** | Autoimmune hemolytic anemia, type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, Graves disease |
| **Systemic** | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, Sjögren syndrome |
| **Postulated autoimmune** | Inflammatory bowel disease, primary biliary cirrhosis, autoimmune hepatitis |

Systemic diseases (caused by immune complexes and autoantibodies) affect connective tissues and blood vessels → called **collagen vascular diseases** or **connective tissue diseases**.

### Immunologic Tolerance

**Tolerance**: State of unresponsiveness to antigen induced by exposure of specific lymphocytes to that antigen.

**Self-tolerance**: Lack of immune responsiveness to body's own tissue antigens.

**Central Tolerance** (in generative lymphoid organs):
- **T cells**: Immature T cells encountering self antigen in thymus undergo **apoptosis** (deletion/negative selection)
  - **AIRE** (autoimmune regulator) protein stimulates expression of peripheral tissue antigens in thymus
  - Mutations in AIRE → autoimmune polyendocrinopathy
  - Some CD4⁺ T cells survive → become **regulatory T cells**
- **B cells**: Immature B cells recognizing self antigen with high affinity may die by apoptosis
  - Some undergo **receptor editing** (rearrange genes, express new non-self-reactive receptors)

> **Limitation**: Not all self antigens present in thymus or bone marrow; some self-reactive lymphocytes escape to periphery.

**Peripheral Tolerance** (in peripheral tissues):

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Anergy** | Functional inactivation of lymphocytes encountering antigen without costimulatory signals |
| **Suppression by regulatory T cells** | CD4⁺ cells expressing high **CD25** (IL-2 receptor α chain) and **FOXP3** transcription factor; secrete **IL-10**, **TGF-β**; express **CTLA-4** |
| **Deletion by apoptosis** | Self-reactive T cells may die via **Bim** (pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family) or **Fas** pathway |
| **Sequestration** | Some antigens hidden from immune system (testis, eye, brain—"immune-privileged sites") |

> **Note**: Mutations in **FOXP3** cause **IPEX** (immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked) in humans. **FAS mutations** cause **ALPS** (autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome).

### Mechanisms of Autoimmunity

**General principle**: Breakdown of self-tolerance results from combined effects of:
- **Susceptibility genes** (influence lymphocyte tolerance)
- **Environmental factors** (infections, tissue injury) (alter display of and responses to self antigens)

**Genetic factors:**
- Autoimmune diseases run in families
- Higher concordance in monozygotic vs dizygotic twins
- Linked to **HLA locus**, especially **class II alleles** (HLA-DR, HLA-DQ)
- **Odds ratio**: Ranges from 3-4 (RA and HLA-DR4) to >100 (ankylosing spondylitis and HLA-B27)

**Role of infections:**
1. Tissue necrosis/inflammation → expression of costimulatory molecules on APCs → breakdown of T cell tolerance
2. **Molecular mimicry**: Microbial antigens share epitopes with self antigens → responses against microbe extend to self tissues
   - Example: **Rheumatic heart disease** (antibody against streptococci cross-targets cardiac antigens)

**Environmental triggers:**
- **UV radiation**: Causes cell death → exposure of nuclear antigens → pathologic immune responses (SLE flares)
- **Smoking**: Risk factor for RA (chemical modification of self antigens)
- **Microbiome**: Different commensal microbes may affect effector/regulatory T cell balance

**Additional factors:**
- **Gender bias**: Many autoimmune diseases more common in women
- **Epitope spreading**: Tissue injury exposes previously concealed epitopes → immune response spreads

### Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

**Definition**: Autoimmune disease with multiple organ involvement, vast array of autoantibodies (particularly **antinuclear antibodies/ANAs**), injury mainly from immune complex deposition and antibody binding.

**Target organs**: Skin, joints, kidney, serosal membranes (virtually any organ may be affected).

**Epidemiology:**
- Prevalence: Up to 400 per 100,000 in certain populations
- Age: Often presents in twenties/thirties (any age)
- **Female-to-male ratio**: 9:1 for reproductive age (17-55 years); 2:1 for childhood/after 65 years
- 2-3 fold higher prevalence in blacks and Hispanics

**Spectrum of Autoantibodies:**

| Specificity | % Positive | Disease Associations |
|-------------|------------|----------------------|
| **Double-stranded DNA** | 40-60% | Nephritis; **specific for SLE** |
| **U1-RNP** | 30-40% | Specific for SLE |
| **Smith (Sm) antigen** | 20-30% | **Specific for SLE** |
| **Ro (SS-A)** | 30-50% | Congenital heart block, neonatal lupus |
| **Phospholipid-protein complexes** | 30-40% | Anti-phospholipid syndrome (~10% of SLE) |
| Multiple nuclear antigens | 95-100% | "Generic ANAs"—found in other diseases |

**Anti-Nuclear Antibodies (ANA patterns):**

| Pattern | Reflects Antibody To | Commonly Seen In |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Homogeneous/diffuse** | Chromatin, histones, dsDNA | SLE |
| **Rim/peripheral** | dsDNA, nuclear envelope proteins | SLE |
| **Centromeric** | Centromeres | Systemic sclerosis |
| **Speckled** | Sm antigen, RNP, SS-A, SS-B | Various (least specific) |
| **Nucleolar** | RNA | Systemic sclerosis |

**Pathogenesis of SLE:**

*Genetic factors:*
- Familial association; 20% of unaffected first-degree relatives have autoantibodies
- Higher concordance in monozygotic (25%) vs dizygotic (1-3%) twins
- Odds ratio for HLA-DR2 or HLA-DR3: 2-3 (both haplotypes: ~5)
- **Complement deficiencies** (C1q, C2, C4) in ~10% of patients
- **FcγRIIb polymorphism** in some patients

*Environmental factors:*
- **UV light**: Exacerbates disease; induces apoptosis; may alter DNA to make it immunogenic
- **Sex hormones** and **X chromosome genes** (independent of hormones)
- Drugs: Hydralazine, procainamide, D-penicillamine (can induce SLE-like disorder)

*Immunologic factors:*
- Failure of B cell self-tolerance
- CD4⁺ helper T cells specific for nucleosomal antigens escape tolerance
- **Type I interferons**: Molecular signature indicates exposure to IFN-α (mainly from plasmacytoid DCs)
- **TLR signals**: TLR9 (DNA) and TLR7 (RNA) activate B cells specific for self nuclear antigens
- **BAFF**: Promotes B cell survival; increased production in some patients

*Model for SLE pathogenesis:*
1. UV radiation and environmental insults → apoptosis
2. Inadequate clearance of apoptotic nuclei → large burden of nuclear antigens
3. Abnormalities in B and T lymphocytes → defective tolerance
4. Self-reactive lymphocytes stimulated by nuclear self antigens → antibody production
5. Complexes bind Fc receptors on B cells and DCs; internalized
6. Nucleic acids engage TLRs → stimulate more autoantibody production
7. TLRs also activate DCs to produce interferons and cytokines
8. Cycle of antigen release and immune activation → high-affinity autoantibodies

**Mechanisms of Tissue Injury in SLE:**

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Immune complexes (Type III)** | DNA-anti-DNA complexes in glomeruli and blood vessels; low complement; granular deposits |
| **Autoantibodies (Type II)** | Anti-RBC, anti-WBC, anti-platelet antibodies → opsonization → phagocytosis → cytopenias |
| **LE cells/bodies** | ANAs bind exposed nuclei in damaged tissue → homogeneous "LE bodies"; phagocytes engulf → LE cells |
| **Anti-phospholipid syndrome** | Venous/arterial thromboses, recurrent miscarriages, cerebral ischemia |
| **Neuropsychiatric manifestations** | Possibly antibodies crossing blood-brain barrier (not established in all cases) |

**Morphology of SLE:**

*Kidney involvement (50% clinically significant; virtually always abnormal on EM/IF):*

| Class | Name | Characteristics |
|-------|------|----------------|
| **I** | Minimal mesangial | Immune deposits in mesangium; no structural changes |
| **II** | Mesangial proliferative | Mesangial cell proliferation; granular mesangial deposits |
| **III** | Focal lupus nephritis | <50% glomeruli affected; segmental/global lesions; necrosis; crescents |
| **IV** | Diffuse lupus nephritis | ≥50% glomeruli affected; most common and severe; wire-loop lesions |
| **V** | Membranous lupus nephritis | Diffuse capillary wall thickening; subepithelial deposits; nephrotic syndrome |
| **VI** | Advanced sclerosing | >90% glomerular sclerosis; end-stage |

*Other organs:*
- **Skin**: Butterfly rash (face); vacuolar degeneration of basal layer; perivascular inflammation
- **Joints**: Nonerosive synovitis
- **CNS**: Noninflammatory occlusion of small vessels; intimal proliferation
- **Serous membranes**: Pericarditis, pleuritis
- **Heart**: Pericarditis; myocarditis; Libman-Sacks endocarditis (verrucous deposits on valves)
- **Spleen**: Splenomegaly; onion-skin lesions (central penicilliary arteries)
- **Lungs**: Pleuritis; interstitial fibrosis

**Clinical features:**
- Variable multisystem disease
- May be acute or insidious
- Common presentation: Young woman with butterfly rash, fever, joint pain without deformity, pleuritic chest pain, photosensitivity
- Laboratory: ANAs (virtually 100%); various cytopenias; urinary abnormalities

**Course and prognosis:**
- Variable and unpredictable
- Rare acute cases: Death within weeks-months
- More commonly: Relapsing-remitting over years
- 5-year survival: ~90%; 10-year survival: ~80%
- Most common causes of death: Renal failure, intercurrent infections, coronary artery disease

### Drug-Induced Lupus Erythematosus

**Drugs causing SLE-like syndrome:** Hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, D-penicillamine, anti-TNF therapy

**Characteristics:**
- ANAs, especially anti-histone antibodies
- Disease remits after withdrawal of offending drug

## Amyloidosis

**Definition**: Condition associated with extracellular deposits of fibrillar proteins causing tissue damage and functional compromise.

**Pathogenesis:**
- Abnormal fibrils produced by aggregation of improperly folded proteins
- Normally soluble in proper folded configuration
- Fibrils bind proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans (charged sugars)
- "Amyloid" named for starch-like staining characteristics (actually unrelated to starch)

### Structure of Amyloid

- All amyloid deposits: **Nonbranching fibrils** with **β-pleated sheet conformation**
- ~95% fibril proteins; ~5% glycoproteins

### Classification and Types

| Type | Fibril Protein | Precursor | Clinical Setting |
|------|---------------|-----------|------------------|
| **AL** | Immunoglobulin light chains | Monoclonal B cell proliferation | Primary amyloidosis, multiple myeloma |
| **AA** | Amyloid-associated protein | SAA (serum amyloid-associated) protein | Reactive systemic (secondary) amyloidosis |
| **Aβ** | β-amyloid protein | APP (amyloid precursor protein) | Alzheimer disease |
| **ATTR** | Transthyretin | Normal or mutant transthyretin | Familial amyloid polyneuropathy, senile cardiac amyloidosis |

### Classification by Clinical Pattern

**Systemic (Generalized) Amyloidosis:**

| Category | Fibril Type | Associated Diseases |
|----------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Primary (AL)** | AL | Multiple myeloma, monoclonal plasma cell proliferation |
| **Secondary (AA)** | AA | Chronic inflammatory conditions (RA, IBD); chronic infections |

**Localized Amyloidosis:**
- Limited to single organ (lung, larynx, skin, bladder, tongue)
- Nodular deposits; often with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates

**Hereditary Amyloidosis:**
- **Familial Mediterranean fever**: Autosomal recessive; AA amyloidosis; mutations in pyrin gene
- **Familial amyloidotic polyneuropathies**: ATTR (mutant transthyretin); different organ involvement based on mutation

**Hemodialysis-associated:**
- **Aβ2m**: β₂-microglobulin
- Occurs in patients on long-term hemodialysis
- Features: Scapulohumeral periarthritis, carpal tunnel syndrome, flexor tenosynovitis

### Morphology and Diagnosis

**Gross:** Enlarged organ; gray, waxy, firm consistency

**Histologic features:**
- Amorphous, eosinophilic, hyaline, extracellular substance
- Begins between cells, often near basement membranes
- Progressive accumulation destroys cells
- In AL amyloidosis: Perivascular and vascular deposits common

**Diagnosis:**
- **H&E stain**: Amorphous, eosinophilic, hyaline, extracellular
- **Congo red stain**: Pink/red with ordinary light; **green birefringence** under polarized microscopy (diagnostic)
- **Electron microscopy**: Nonoriented thin fibrils
- **Subtyping**: Mass spectroscopy (immunohistochemistry not entirely reliable)

**Organ involvement patterns:**
- **AA amyloidosis**: Kidneys, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, adrenal glands, thyroid
- **AL amyloidosis**: Heart, GI tract, respiratory tract, peripheral nerves, skin, tongue (in addition to above)

### Clinical Correlations

| Type | Common Presentations |
|------|---------------------|
| **Renal amyloidosis** | Most common and serious; proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome |
| **Cardiac amyloidosis** | Restrictive cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias (senile systemic, ATTR) |
| **Neurologic** | Peripheral neuropathy (familial amyloidotic polyneuropathies) |

## Summary: The Normal Immune Response

**Innate Immunity:**
- Uses ~100 receptors to recognize 1000 molecular patterns
- Components: Epithelial barriers, phagocytes, NK cells, complement
- Manifests as inflammation; does not have fine antigen specificity or memory

**Adaptive Immunity:**
- Lymphocytes produce specific and diverse antigen receptors
- **T cells**: TCRs recognize peptide-MHC complexes
- **B cells**: Membrane antibodies recognize diverse antigens; differentiate to plasma cells secreting antibodies
- **NK cells**: Kill infected/stressed cells; express inhibitory receptors for MHC class I

**Antigen-Presenting Cells:**
- Capture microbes; transport to lymphoid organs; display to lymphocytes
- Most efficient: **Dendritic cells**

**Immune Response Overview:**
- Early reaction: Innate immunity (ready to respond)
- Later response: Adaptive immunity (more potent, specific, with memory)

**Cell-Mediated vs Humoral Immunity:**
- Cell-mediated: T lymphocytes combat cell-associated microbes
- Humoral: Antibodies effective against extracellular microbes

**Helper T Cell Functions (CD4⁺):**
- Help B cells make antibodies
- Activate macrophages to destroy ingested microbes
- Stimulate leukocyte recruitment
- Regulate all immune responses to protein antigens

**Cytotoxic T Lymphocyte Functions (CD8⁺):**
- Kill cells expressing cytoplasmic antigens (virus-infected, tumor cells)
- Produce cytokines

**Antibody Functions:**
- Neutralize microbes
- Promote phagocytosis and destruction
- Confer passive immunity to neonates
